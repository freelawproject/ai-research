19                iii.  Motion to Strike Under Rule 12(f)                 
20       Rule 12(f) provides that a court “may strike from a pleading an insufficient defense or any 
21  redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous matter.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(f).  A court will 
22  only consider striking a defense or allegation if it fits within one of these five categories.  Yursik 
23  v. Inland Crop Dusters Inc., No. CV-F-11-01602-LJO-JLT, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2011 WL 5592888</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (E.D. Cal. 
24  Nov. 16, 2011) (citing Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="173487"><a href="/opinion/173487/whittlestone-inc-v-handi-craft-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Whittlestone, Inc. v. Handi-Craft Co.">618 F.3d 970</a></span><pre class="inline">, 973–74 (9th Cir. 
25  2010)).  “[T]he function of a 12(f) motion to strike is to avoid the expenditure of time and money 
26  that must arise from litigating spurious issues by dispensing with those issues prior to trial.”  
27  Sidney-Vinstein v. A.H. Robins Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8916325"><a href="/opinion/8926587/sidney-vinstein-v-ah-robins-co/#885" aria-description="Citation for case: Sidney-Vinstein v. A.H. Robins Co.">697 F.2d 880, 885</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1983).  However, Rule 12(f) 
28  motions are “generally regarded with disfavor because of the limited importance of pleading in 
1  federal practice, and because they are often used as a delaying tactic.”  Neilson v. Union Bank of 
2  Cal., N.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2326612"><a href="/opinion/2326612/neilson-v-union-bank-of-california-na/#1152" aria-description="Citation for case: Neilson v. Union Bank of California, N.A.">290 F. Supp. 2d 1101, 1152</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 2003).  “Ultimately, whether to grant a motion 
3  to strike lies within the sound discretion of the district court.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2326612"><a href="/opinion/2326612/neilson-v-union-bank-of-california-na/" aria-description="Citation for case: Neilson v. Union Bank of California, N.A.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Unless it would prejudice the 
4  opposing party, courts freely grant leave to amend stricken pleadings.  Foman v. Davis, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/U.S./371/5/">371 U.S. 
5</a></span><pre class="inline">  178, 182 (1962); Howey v. U.S., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="312391"><a href="/opinion/312391/frank-howey-v-united-states-of-america-and-third-party-v-radio/#1190" aria-description="Citation for case: Frank Howey v. United States of America, and Third-Party...">481 F.2d 1187, 1190</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1973); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 
6  15(a)(2).  If the court is in doubt as to whether the challenged matter may raise an issue of fact or 
7  law, the motion to strike should be denied, leaving the assessment of the sufficiency of the 
8  allegations for adjudication on the merits after proper development of the factual nature of the 
9  claims through discovery.  See generally Whittlestone, Inc., 618 F.3d at 974–75.

13  Defenders of Wildlife, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/#561" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">504 U.S. 555, 561</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1992).  In class actions, “the named representatives 
14  must allege and show that they personally have been injured.”  Lierboe v. State Farm Mut. Auto. 
15  Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="784326"><a href="/opinion/784326/kristine-e-lierboe-individually-and-on-behalf-of-all-others-similarly/#1022" aria-description="Citation for case: Kristine E. Lierboe, Individually and on Behalf of All...">350 F.3d 1018, 1022</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2003) (quoting Pence v. Andrus, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8907993"><a href="/opinion/8919441/pence-v-andrus/" aria-description="Citation for case: Pence v. Andrus">586 F.2d 733</a></span><pre class="inline">, 736–37 
16  (9th Cir. 1978)).  The “injury must have actually occurred or must occur imminently; 
17  hypothetical, speculative or other ‘possible future’ injuries do not count in the standings 
18  calculus.”  Schmier v. U.S. Court of Appeals for Ninth Circuit, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="776473"><a href="/opinion/776473/michael-schmier-v-united-states-court-of-appeals-for-the-ninth-circuit-and/#820" aria-description="Citation for case: Michael Schmier v. United States Court of Appeals for the...">279 F.3d 817, 820</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2002) 
19  (citing Whitmore v. Arkansas, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9431985"><a href="/opinion/112418/whitmore-ex-rel-simmons-v-arkansas/#155" aria-description="Citation for case: Whitmore Ex Rel. Simmons v. Arkansas">495 U.S. 149, 155</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1990)).              
20       “California . . . ‘has adopted the common law rule’ that ‘[l]iability may [ . . . ] be imposed 
21  on one who aids and abets the commission of an intentional tort if the person [ . . . ] knows the 
22  other’s conduct constitutes a breach of a duty and gives substantial assistance or encouragement 
23  to the other to so act.’”  Solarmore Mgmt. Servs. Inc. v. Bankruptcy Estate of DC Solar Sols., Inc., 
24  No. , </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 358245</span><pre class="inline">, at *5 (E.D. Cal. Feb. 7, 2022) (quoting Casey v. U.S. Bank Nat’l Assn., 
25  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2262093"><a href="/opinion/2262093/casey-v-us-bank-national-assn/#1144" aria-description="Citation for case: Casey v. U.S. Bank National Ass&#x27;n">127 Cal. App. 4th 1138, 1144</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2005)).  “To satisfy the knowledge prong, the defendant must have 
26  actual knowledge of the specific primary wrong the defendant substantially assisted.”  Id. 
27  (internal quotation marks and citation omitted).  Here, the FAC alleges the following with respect 
28  to “aiding and abetting”:                                             
1            [Prime Administration] today owns and operates over 15,000 units 
             in California, Oregon, Washington, and Nevada . . . the same group 
2            of individuals operates and manages both Prime Administration and 
             the entities holding title to the Prime Properties . . . these limited 
3            partnerships and limited liability company titleholders are merely 
             instrumentalities of Prime Administration, are all part of the same 
4            scheme, and are controlled and managed collectively.  To the extent 
             the nominal titleholders have any independent existence, they are co-
5            conspirators and aiders and abettors of Prime Administration in 
             committing  the  conduct  described  in  [the  FAC].    The  nominal 
6            titleholders  knowingly  participated  in  the  common  scheme  and 
             giving  substantial  assistance  and  encouragement  to  Prime 
7            Administration,  including  allowing  Prime  Administration  to 
             implement  and  effectuate  the  unfair  and  unlawful  policies  and 
8            practices described in this [FAC] at the various Prime Properties 
             owned by the nominal titleholders.                          
9                                                                        
10  (ECF No. 1-2 at 11–12.)  The FAC further alleges “Defendants all knowingly aided and abetted 
11  the violations of law described in [the FAC] and knowingly aided and abetted the conduct related 
12  to the scheme, agreed to commit the unlawful and unfair conduct, and gave substantial assistance 
13  and encouragement in committing the unlawful and unfair conduct.”  (Id. at 15.)  Based on the 
14  foregoing allegations, specifically that “[t]he nominal titleholders knowingly participated in the 
15  common scheme and giving substantial assistance and encouragement to Prime Administration,” 
16  it is a reasonable inference that Plaintiff alleges the Standing Defendants and the Blue Rock/Park 
17  LaBrea Defendants knew “the other’s conduct constitute[d] a breach of a duty and g[a]ve[] 
18  substantial assistance or encouragement to the other to so act.’”  Solarmore Mgmt. Servs. Inc., 
19  </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 358245</span><pre class="inline">, at *5.  The Court therefore finds Plaintiffs adequately pleaded that the 
20  Standing Defendants aided and abetted the alleged unlawful conduct.  Accordingly, Defendants’ 
21  motion to dismiss the claims against the Standing Defendants is DENIED.

9                iii.  Claim Eight: Violation of UCL                     
10       Defendants argue Plaintiffs’ UCL claim fails because the FAC does not allege an unfair 
11  business practice and instead only states conclusory allegations.  (ECF No. 6 at 27–28.)  In 
12  opposition, Plaintiffs maintain they have pleaded enough facts, as they allege “Prime will assess 
13  late fees even when its tenants timely pay rent online, and assess late fees based on charges 
14  assessed after the due date for rent payment” and “Prime ignored or prevented Plaintiffs and class 
15  members from accomplishing the early termination process despite their best efforts to do so, and 
16  improperly charged them because of it.”  (ECF No. 11 at 23–24 (citing ECF No. 1-2 ¶¶ 56 n.4, 
17  57, 75, 94).)                                                         
18       The UCL prohibits “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice . . . .”  Cal. 
19  Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200.  California courts employ three different tests to evaluate whether a 
20  business practice is “unfair” under the UCL.  Roper v. Big Heart Pet Brands, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1981183"><a href="/opinion/1981183/lykins-v-aluminum-workers-international-union/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lykins v. Aluminum Workers International Union">510 F. Supp. 
21</a></span><pre class="inline">  3d 903, 919 (E.D. Cal. 2020).  First, “[t]he balancing test determines whether the alleged practice 
22  is immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous, or substantially injurious to consumers and 
23  requires the court to weigh the utility of the defendant’s alleged conduct against the gravity of 
24  harm to the alleged victim.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1981183"><a href="/opinion/1981183/lykins-v-aluminum-workers-international-union/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lykins v. Aluminum Workers International Union">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 919–20 (citing Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Ass’n, 182 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="2291830"><a href="/opinion/2291830/metro-goldwyn-mayer-inc-v-superior-court/#257" aria-description="Citation for case: Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, Inc. v. Superior Court">25  Cal. App. 4th 247, 257</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2010)).  Second, the Federal Trade Commission (“FTC”) test uses the 
26  definition of unfair in § 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act “and requires that: (1) the 
27  consumer injury be substantial; (2) the injury must not be outweighed by any countervailing 
28  benefits to consumers or competition; and (3) it must be an injury that consumers themselves 
1  could not reasonably have avoided.”  Id. at 920 (citing Drum, 182 Cal. App. 4th at 257).  Third, 
2  “the public policy test requires that the public policy which is a predicate to consumer unfair 
3  competition under the ‘unfair’ prong of the UCL must be tethered to specific constitutional, 
4  statutory, or regulatory provisions.”  Id. (citing Drum, 182 Cal. App. 4th at 257).  The Ninth 
5  Circuit has approved of the balancing test and the public policy test in consumer actions.  Hadley 
6  v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1104" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1104</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017) (citing Lozano v. AT&amp;T 
7  Wireless Servs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1429600"><a href="/opinion/1429600/lozano-v-at-t-wireless-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lozano v. AT &amp; T Wireless Services, Inc.">504 F.3d 718</a></span><pre class="inline">, 735–36 (9th Cir. 2007)).         
8       Here, Plaintiffs allege online reviews “from various Prime Properties discuss the 
9  assessment of improper late fees, . . . improper early termination fees, unlawful rent charges after 
10  providing thirty (30) days notice to vacate, [and] failure to return security deposits or provide an 
11  itemized statement of deductions from security deposits or receipt related thereto within 21 days.”  
12  (ECF No. 1-2 ¶¶ 56–57.)  Plaintiffs further allege:                   
13            During the time that Leaser and Wongsaroj occupied the apartment, 
             Defendants charged Leaser and Wongsaroj improper and exorbitant 
14            late fees, based on assessments for charges for sewer, water, and 
             garbage.  The late fees were caused because charges would be posted 
15            by Defendants to Leaser’s and Wongsaroj’s accounts after they were 
             due and thus could not be paid on time.                     
16                                                                        
17  (Id. at ¶ 75.)  Finally, Plaintiffs allege:                           
18            Defendants charged Eisman an early termination fee and a late fee 
             despite Eisman providing 30 days advance[] notice.  More than 21 
19            days after vacating her apartment, Eisman was provided a final 
             account statement that improperly listed her date of giving notice as 
20            May 30, 2017, rather than April 6, 2017, and improperly listed her 
             move  out  date  as  May  25,  2017  (i.e.,  before  Eisman  allegedly 
21            provided notice on May 30, 2017).  Defendants deducted these 
             charges from Eisman’s security deposit, which was wholly retained 
22            by Defendants.                                              
23  (Id. at 95 (emphasis omitted).)  Based on the foregoing allegations of improperly charging tenants 
24  fees and charges, failing to return security deposits or providing an itemized statement of 
25  deductions therefrom, the Court finds Plaintiffs adequately pleaded these practices are 
26  “substantially injurious to consumers” and that the utility of Defendants’ alleged conduct is 
27  minimal relative to the gravity of harm alleged by Plaintiffs.  See Roper, 510 F. Supp. 3d at 919–
28  20.  Accordingly, Defendants’ motion to dismiss Plaintiffs’ claim under the unfair prong of the 
1  UCL is DENIED.

13            C.   Second Motion to Dismiss                               
14                i.    Whether Defendants Can Bring a Subsequent Motion to Dismiss 
15       As an initial matter, Plaintiffs argue in their opposition that Defendants’ Rule 12(b)(1) 
16  motion is a de facto Rule 12(b)(6) motion that is untimely and should be denied, as “Rule 
17  12(g)(2) provides that a party who makes a motion under Rule 12 cannot make a subsequent Rule 
18  12 motion raising a defense or objection that was available to that party but omitted from its 
19  earlier motion.”  (ECF No. 17 at 11–12.)  Plaintiffs contend Defendants are arguing the merits as 
20  well as affirmative defenses before filing an answer and are asking the Court to review extrinsic 
21  evidence on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, but make no argument that the extrinsic evidence is subject 
22  to an exception.  (Id. at 12–13.)  Plaintiffs assert Defendants’ attempt to “shoehorn a Rule 
23  23(d)(1)(D) attack on Plaintiffs’ typicality” is improper for a Rule 12(b)(1) motion.  (Id. at 13.)  
24  Plaintiffs finally maintain “the jurisdiction[al] and substantive issues are . . . significantly 
25  intertwined,” and thus resolution under Rule 12(b)(1) is inappropriate.  (Id.) 
26       In reply, Defendants contend the motion is a factual attack on Plaintiffs’ Article III 
27  standing properly raised under Rule 12(b)(1) and in such a factual attack, the Court “may review 
28  evidence outside the pleadings to resolve factual disputes concerning the existence of jurisdiction 
1  without converting the motion to dismiss into a summary judgment motion.”  (ECF No. 19 at 6–
2  7.)  Defendants maintain the jurisdictional and substantive issues are not “intertwined,” as 
3  “Defendants are not claiming that Plaintiffs lack standing because the challenged late fees or 
4  early termination option fees are legal” but that “Plaintiffs did not suffer any injury because they 
5  did not pay the fees they seek to challenge.”  (Id. at 7.)            
6       The Court agrees with Defendants that their arguments challenging Plaintiffs’ standing is 
7  properly brought as a Rule 12(b)(1) motion and the Court must dismiss the action if it 
8  “determines at any time that it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(h)(3).  Thus, 
9  the Court agrees to rule on this motion.  However, Defendants’ Rule 12(f) motion to strike the 
10  class allegations relating to early termination options and late fees is improper because this 
11  defense or objection was available to Defendants when they filed their first motion to dismiss.  
12  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(g)(2).  Accordingly, Defendants’ motion to strike is DENIED.  To the extent 
13  the motion attempts to function as a statutory standing challenge or a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to 
14  dispute the merits of Plaintiffs’ claims, the Court declines to consider and address such arguments 
15  as improper because they were available to Defendants when they filed their first motion to 
16  dismiss.  See Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(g)(2).  To the extent the motion attempts to function as a Rule 
17  23(d)(1)(D) to eliminate class allegations, the Court again DENIES it as premature.  See In re 
18  Land Rover LR3 Tire Wear Prods. Liability Litig., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 5473736</span><pre class="inline">, at *2; In re Wal-Mart 
19  Stores, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1950713"><a href="/opinion/1950713/in-re-wal-mart-stores-inc-wage-hour-litigation/#615" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. Wage &amp; Hour Litigation">505 F. Supp. 2d at 615</a></span><pre class="inline">.
