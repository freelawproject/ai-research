<opinion type="dissent">
<author id="b308-7">Hood, J.</author>
<p id="Alp"><em>(dissenting). </em>I agree that a cause of action can be maintained for failure to diagnose Christine’s pregnancy. However, I would also find on the facts of this case that the grandparents can recover child-rearing costs and that their damages are not precluded as a matter of law but are to be determined by the trier of fact.</p>
<p id="b308-8">In May, 1983, Mrs. Rinard took Christine, who was then fifteen years old, to see Dr. Biczak. Mrs. Rinard chose Biczak because he had previously conducted Mrs. Rinard’s yearly physicals. The visit was prompted when Mrs. Rinard discovered that <page-number citation-index="1" label="297">*297</page-number>Christine had been sexually active and feared she might be pregnant. The pregnancy tests performed at that time were negative.</p>
<p id="b309-4">Late in November, Mrs. Rinard again called defendant because Christine had not had a period since September 7, 1983. An appointment was made and Mrs. Rinard took Christine to defendant’s office on December 5, 1983. At that examination, Biczak questioned Christine concerning whether she had engaged in sexual intercourse since the last appointment. Christine’s response was negative. Actually, Christine had been having sexual relations with her stepbrother, but Christine testified that he had never penetrated her and at the time of the examination she believed that there had to be penetration in order for there to be "sexual intercourse.”</p>
<p id="b309-5">Biczak concluded that Christine was not pregnant. He believed she was simply going through a phase of irregular menses which is common for teenagers. No pregnancy tests were taken. Christine was in fact pregnant at that time. Mrs. Rinard brought Christine to see Biczak again in April, 1984. Christine again said that she had not had a menstrual period since September, 1983, and that she had not had sexual intercourse since May, 1983. Biczak diagnosed secondary amenorrhea. He reached this conclusion because Christine was having no menstrual periods as opposed to menstrual irregularity and because he had eliminated pregnancy as a source of Christine’s problems. Biczak believed that the abdominal bloating Christine was then experiencing was due to gastrointestinal problems.</p>
<p id="b309-6">Mrs. Rinard then decided to take her daughter to a gynecologist who informed Mrs. Rinard that Christine was pregnant. The child was delivered in July, 1984, by emergency Caesarean section be<page-number citation-index="1" label="298">*298</page-number>cause of a seizure Christine experienced while in labor. The child was born healthy and full term. The Rinards adopted the child in August, 1984.</p>
<p id="b310-4">The complaint alleged that Biczak’s negligence in failing to timely diagnose her pregnancy deprived Christine of the opportunity to terminate the unplanned pregnancy. Only the Rinards sought recovery for the expenses incurred for Christine’s pregnancy care and for the support and maintenance of the child.</p>
<p id="b310-5">I agree with the majority’s holding that a cause of action can be maintained for failure to diagnose Christine’s pregnancy. In <em>Clapham v Yanga, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 47</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">300 NW2d 727</a></span> (1980), lv gtd <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1981), dis by stipulation <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1982), this Court impliedly recognized a cause of action for failure to diagnose a pregnancy. The patient was fourteen years old when her mother took her to the doctor for a medical examination. Both the daughter and the grandparents were awarded damages. This Court did not address the viability of the claim, but decided a damages issue with reference to principles generally applicable in medical malpractice actions.</p>
<p id="b310-6">In arguing that plaintiffs’ claim must be denied despite <em>Clapham, </em>defendant relies on <em>Rieck v Medical Protective Co of Fort Wayne, Indiana, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">64 Wis 2d 514</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">219 NW2d 242</a></span> (1974). In <em><span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">Rieck</a></span>, </em>the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that a complaint alleging that a clinic and an obstetrician failed to determine and timely inform the mother that she was pregnant so that the pregnancy could be terminated did not state a cause of action. The Wisconsin court reasoned that, even where negligence can be established as the cause in fact of certain resulting damages, recovery could be denied on public policy grounds.</p>
<p id="b310-7">Some of the same public policy arguments raised <page-number citation-index="1" label="299">*299</page-number>by the <em><span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">Rieck</a></span> </em>court were considered by this Court in <em>Troppi v Scarf, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">31 Mich App 240</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">187 NW2d 511</a></span> (1971), in determining that a pharmacist could be liable for negligently failing to supply a prescribed oral contraceptive. The <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi</a></span> </em>Court found the birth of a healthy child to be a compensable injury. Since <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi</a></span>, </em>the Michigan courts have continued to treat other claims involving unplanned births as malpractice claims. <em>Eisbrenner v Stanley, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">106 Mich App 357</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">308 NW2d 209</a></span> (1981)(physician’s failure to diagnose rubella); <em>Stephens v Spiwak, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">61 Mich App 647</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">233 NW2d 124</a></span> (1975)(negligently performed tubal ligation); <em><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">Clapham, supra</a></span> </em>(misdiagnosis of pregnancy).</p>
<p id="b311-6">Similarly, the only other court which has directly addressed the wrongful diagnosis claim viewed the case basically as one for malpractice in which the person responsible must respond for the resulting damages. <em>Ziemba v Sternberg, </em><span class="citation" data-id="5793024"><a href="/opinion/5934790/ziemba-v-sternberg/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ziemba v. Sternberg">45 AD2d 230</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="5793024"><a href="/opinion/5934790/ziemba-v-sternberg/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ziemba v. Sternberg">357 NYS2d 265</a></span> (1974). See also <em>Cockrum v Baumgartner, </em>95 Ill 2d 193; <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner">447 NE2d 385</a></span> (1983), in which the Illinois Supreme Court addressed only the issue of damages, but treated the claim as one for malpractice.</p>
<p id="b311-7">The Michigan cases have continued to follow <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi</a></span> </em>and to view the unplanned pregnancy or birth claim as a question of the physician’s duty to ascertain and advise the parent so that available options can be exercised. In the absence of any directive from the Legislature or the Supreme Court that this type of claim should not be allowed, the wrongful diagnosis claim should also be recognized. See <em>Proffitt v Bartolo, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#46" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">162 Mich App 35, 46</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">412 NW2d 232</a></span> (1987).</p>
<p id="b311-8">Having determined that this is a viable cause of action, the next question is whether it can be brought by the grandparents of the child. Defendant would preclude plaintiffs from recovery on <page-number citation-index="1" label="300">*300</page-number>the basis that any duty he owed the daughter did not extend to her parents since they were not his patients. He would distinguish <em>Clapham </em>and its award to the grandparents because in <em>Clapham </em>the natural parent was also a plaintiff.</p>
<p id="b312-5">The extension of the physician’s duty to third parties has been recognized in limited situations. In <em>Duvall v Goldin, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9670923"><a href="/opinion/1708803/duvall-v-goldin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Duvall v. Goldin">139 Mich App 342</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9670923"><a href="/opinion/1708803/duvall-v-goldin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Duvall v. Goldin">362 NW2d 275</a></span> (1984), this Court recognized that the plaintiff, injured in an auto accident, could bring a claim against a doctor based on his negligence in treating a patient who caused the accident.</p>
<p id="b312-6">The question is whether the facts establish a relationship between the parties such that the community will impose a legal obligation or duty upon the defendant for the benefit of plaintiffs. <em>Id., </em>p 347. A defendant’s special relationship with a nonparty can form the basis for a cause of action where it was foreseeable that the defendant’s conduct would create a risk of harm to the plaintiff and where policy considerations do not preclude recognition of the asserted duty. <em>Id., </em>p 349.<footnotemark>1</footnotemark> See also <em>Shepard v Redford Community Hospital, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2138970"><a href="/opinion/2138970/shepard-v-redford-community-hospital/" aria-description="Citation for case: Shepard v. Redford Community Hospital">151 Mich App 242</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2138970"><a href="/opinion/2138970/shepard-v-redford-community-hospital/" aria-description="Citation for case: Shepard v. Redford Community Hospital">390 NW2d 239</a></span> (1986). <em>Welke v Kuzilla, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9703718"><a href="/opinion/1976104/welke-v-kuzilla/" aria-description="Citation for case: Welke v. Kuzilla">144 Mich App 245</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9703718"><a href="/opinion/1976104/welke-v-kuzilla/" aria-description="Citation for case: Welke v. Kuzilla">375 NW2d 403</a></span> (1985).</p>
<p id="b312-7">The doctor-patient relationship between Biczak and Christine supplies the special relationship. It was foreseeable that a doctor’s failure to diagnose a pregnancy could result in someone’s having to provide for the child. The likelihood in this case <page-number citation-index="1" label="301">*301</page-number>that the expense would fall on the grandparents is not so rare or unusual an occurrence as to be considered unforeseeable. <span class="citation" data-id="9670923"><a href="/opinion/1708803/duvall-v-goldin/#352" aria-description="Citation for case: Duvall v. Goldin"><em>Duvall, supra, </em>p 352</a></span>. Having found the harm to plaintiffs foreseeable, I find no policy reason which would preclude recovery. The only person who would profit from a holding that the grandparents are not entitled to damages would be the defendant doctor who would be allowed to escape the consequences of his own negligence. <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/#60" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga"><em>Clapham, supra, </em>p 60</a></span>. As noted in <em><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">Clapham, supra,</a></span> </em>p 61:</p>
<blockquote id="AgE">The only thing novel about this case is our holding that the grandparents of a minor child’s baby may obtain damages where they provide for the infant’s care, just as the baby’s parents might have. See <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi, supra;</a></span> Stephens v Spiwak, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">61 Mich App 647</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">233 NW2d 124</a></span> (1975), lv den <span class="citation no-link">395 Mich 761</span> (1975); <em>Green v Sudakin, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">81 Mich App 545</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">265 NW2d 411</a></span> (1978), lv den <span class="citation no-link">403 Mich 855</span> (1978). We believe this is a sound and minor extension of existing precedent. A child’s grandparents cannot be treated like total strangers in respect to a claim such as the one presented here.</blockquote>
<p id="b313-6">Nor would I find that somehow Christine’s parents are precluded from receiving damages because they voluntarily assumed legal responsibility of the child through adoption. See <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/#58" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga"><em>Clapham, supra, </em>p 58</a></span>. Once the determination is made that liability extends to these plaintiffs in these circumstances, a change in their legal status does not change their right to recover.</p>
<p id="b313-7">The final question is what recovery is available. The majority would bar recovery of -damages for the emotional and economic expenses of raising the child and thereby discard the "benefits rule” set forth in <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi, supra.</a></span> Troppi </em>approached the recovery of expenses arising from an unplanned <page-number citation-index="1" label="302">*302</page-number>pregnancy as coming under the general tort rule that the wrongdoer is liable for all injuries directly resulting from the wrongful acts. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#246" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><em>Troppi, supra, </em>pp 246, 252</a></span>. Although recognizing that benefits may naturally flow from the birth of a healthy child, the <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi</a></span> </em>Court refused to find as a matter of law that these benefits would always outweigh the unexpected costs associated with raising the unplanned child. By allowing the finder of fact to determine the final award by subtracting the benefits from the assessed damages, the Court sought to provide a rule with sufficient flexibility to allow the trier of fact to evaluate all the circumstances of a case and arrive at a reasonable amount of damages. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><em>Troppi, supra, </em>pp 254-257</a></span>.</p>
<p id="b314-5">Defendant argues that the benefits rule is a minority opinion among the jurisdictions which have considered this type of recovery and that we should now reject it and align ourselves with the majority view which precludes recovery for the costs of raising the child. I would decline to do so.</p>
<p id="b314-6">To a great extent, those courts which have rejected the <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi</a></span> </em>approach, see, e.g., <em><span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner">Cockrum, supra;</a></span> Morris v Sanchez, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez">746 P2d 184</a></span> (Okla, 1987), echo the reasoning of <em><span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">Rieck, supra,</a></span> </em>and raise issues similar to those addressed in <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi</a></span> </em>when the rule was adopted. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><em>Troppi, supra, </em>pp 254-262</a></span>. In general, those courts which would deny recovery begin with the premise that the parents cannot be damaged by the birth of a normal, healthy baby since human life is so valued that the benefits associated with birth and life will always outweigh any claimed damages.<footnotemark>2</footnotemark> Those courts which align with <em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi</a></span> </em>in general take the view that compen<page-number citation-index="1" label="303">*303</page-number>sation is sought as a direct result of the physician’s negligence and is recoverable in the same manner as other malpractice awards.<footnotemark>3</footnotemark></p>
<p id="b315-5">I do not agree that allowing plaintiffs to be compensated for expenditures suffered as a result of the physician’s negligence somehow disparages the value of human life or ignores the potential benefits of parenthood.</p>
<p id="b315-6">The injury here is not the child, but the economic reality associated with the unplanned expenses which are the consequences of defendant’s negligence. As noted in <em>Jones v Malinowski, </em>299 Md 257, 270; <span class="citation" data-id="2379505"><a href="/opinion/2379505/jones-v-malinowski/#436" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Malinowski">473 A2d 429, 436</a></span> (1984), a negligent sterilization case:</p>
<blockquote id="b315-7">The parents seek damages, not because they do not love and want to keep the unplanned child, but because the direct, foreseeable and natural consequences of the physician’s negligence has forced upon them burdens which they sought and had a right to avoid ....</blockquote>
<p id="b315-8">By allowing recovery I would join with the court in <em>Ochs v Borrelli, </em>187 Conn 253, 259; <span class="citation" data-id="2079084"><a href="/opinion/2079084/ochs-v-borrelli/#885" aria-description="Citation for case: Ochs v. Borrelli">445 A2d 883, 885-886</a></span> (1982), and reject the</p>
<blockquote id="b315-9">premise that a recognition of the economic costs of parenthood is necessarily a negative judgment on the child who occasions them. We may take judicial notice of the fact that raising a child from birth to maturity is a costly enterprise, and hence injurious, although it is an experience that abundantly recompenses most parents with intangible rewards. There can be no affront to public policy in our recognition of these costs and no inconsistency in our view that parental pleasure softens but does not eradicate economic reality._</blockquote>
<p id="b316-5"><page-number citation-index="1" label="304">*304</page-number>The unplanned birth in this case has caused plaintiffs to expend both money and emotional energy to discharge a familial responsibility. See <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#192" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez"><em>Morris, supra, </em>p 192</a></span> (Kauger, J., concurring and dissenting in part). The finder of fact presumably has determined that the Rinards would not otherwise have experienced these expenditures except for the defendant’s negligence. In these circumstances, the benefits rule continues to be an appropriate means of determining damages.</p>
<p id="b316-6">Defendant also appeals the trial court’s refusal to give four requested nonstandard jury instructions. The requested instructions either concern the use and qualifications of an expert witness, issues more properly the concern of the court than the jury, or they relate to the weight to be given the expert’s testimony. The trial court gave the Standard Jury Instructions on credibility of witnesses and professional negligence which adequately and fairly represented the applicable law to the jury. <em>Beadle v Allis, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1894469"><a href="/opinion/1894469/beadle-v-allis/#525" aria-description="Citation for case: Beadle v. Allis">165 Mich App 516, 525-526</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1894469"><a href="/opinion/1894469/beadle-v-allis/" aria-description="Citation for case: Beadle v. Allis">418 NW2d 906</a></span> (1987).</p>
<p id="b316-7">Furthermore, a trial court may properly refuse an instruction where the SJI Committee recommends against an instruction, unless the instruction is necessary to accurately state the law and the matter is not otherwise adequately covered in the instructions. MCR 2.516(D)(3); <em>Siirila v Barrios, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9659804"><a href="/opinion/1619071/siirila-v-barrios/#595" aria-description="Citation for case: Siirila v. Barrios">398 Mich 576, 595-597</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9659804"><a href="/opinion/1619071/siirila-v-barrios/" aria-description="Citation for case: Siirila v. Barrios">248 NW2d 171</a></span> (1976). The SJI Committee recommends that no instruction on the weighing of expert testimony be given (SJI2d 4.10). I agree with the committee commentary that matters related to weighing expert testimony are best left to the argument of counsel. The requested instructions did not set forth considerations relevant to the jury’s deliberation and the court did not abuse its discretion in rejecting them. MCR 2.516(D)(4).</p>
<p id="b317-3"><page-number citation-index="1" label="305">*305</page-number>Defendant’s final argument is that the trial court erred in admitting evidence over his objection. The decision to admit certain evidence rests within the sound discretion of the trial court and that decision will not be set aside absent an abuse of discretion. <em>Hadley v Trio Tool Co, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1655998"><a href="/opinion/1655998/hadley-v-trio-tool-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Trio Tool Co.">143 Mich App 319</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1655998"><a href="/opinion/1655998/hadley-v-trio-tool-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Trio Tool Co.">372 NW2d 537</a></span> (1985). The court allowed testimony regarding an office visit when the patient was six months pregnant as relevant to the claim of malpractice and the defendant’s lack of medical knowledge. Testimony regarding the patient’s seizures at the time of delivery was admitted as relevant to plaintiffs’ claim. Given the nature of the claim in this case, the court did not abuse its discretion in allowing the testimony.</p>
<p id="b317-4">Defendant’s post-trial motions for summary disposition, judgment notwithstanding the verdict and a new trial all basically claimed that the Rinards could not have a claim against Biczak because he did not owe them any legal duty. In reviewing the trial court’s denial of these motions, this Court views the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and decides if judgment for the nonmoving party is precluded as a matter of law. This Court will not reverse the decision of the trial court absent an abuse of its discretion. <em>Ritchie v Michigan Consolidated Gas Co, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1966176"><a href="/opinion/1966176/ritchie-v-michigan-consolidated-gas-co/#367" aria-description="Citation for case: Ritchie v. Michigan Consolidated Gas Co.">163 Mich App 358, 367-368</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1966176"><a href="/opinion/1966176/ritchie-v-michigan-consolidated-gas-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ritchie v. Michigan Consolidated Gas Co.">413 NW2d 796</a></span> (1987). Having found that plaintiffs’ claims were not precluded as a matter of law, I would affirm the trial court’s denial of defendant’s motions and affirm the jury verdict for plaintiffs.</p>
