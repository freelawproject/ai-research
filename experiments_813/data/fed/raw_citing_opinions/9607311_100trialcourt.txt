13        Now before the Court for consideration is the motion to dismiss the first amended 
14   complaint (“FAC”) filed by Defendant Mondelez International, Inc. (“Defendant”).  The Court has 
15   reviewed the parties’ papers, relevant legal authority, and the record in this case and finds the 
16   matter suitable for disposition without oral argument.  See N.D. Civ. L.R. 7-1(b).  For the 
17   following reasons, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion with LEAVE TO AMEND.  
18                              BACKGROUND                                 
19        Plaintiff Avi Klammer (“Plaintiff”) brings this putative class action challenging the 
20   labeling of Defendant’s Enjoy Life Lentil Chips (the “Products”).1  Plaintiff alleges that the 
21   Products are labeled as “high protein” and “protein-packed” when in fact they are not high in 
22   protein and do not provide a good source of protein.  (FAC ¶¶ 4-5.)  Specifically, Plaintiff alleges 
23   the following representations and omissions are misleading: (1) protein content claims on the front 
24   and back of the Products’ labels; (2) the claims that each product is “high protein” and “protein 
25   packed,” and (3) the omission of the percentage of the Daily Recommended Value (“DRV”) for 
26   protein in the Nutrition Facts panel.  (Id. ¶¶ 19-22.)                
27                                                                         
1        Plaintiff purchased the Sea Salt and Garlic &amp; Parmesan flavors of the chips on multiple 
2   occasions.  Most recently, Plaintiff purchased the Products at a Walmart in San Leandro.  (Id. ¶ 9.)  
3   Plaintiff alleges that the label statements led him to believe that consuming the products would 
4   provide a good source of protein.  (Id. ¶ 11.)                        
5        Plaintiff asserts claims for violations of: California’s Consumer Legal Remedies Act, Civil 
6   Code sections 1750, et seq. (“CLRA”); California’s False Advertising Law, Business and 
7   Professions Code sections 17500, et seq. (“FAL”); and California’s Unfair Competition Law, 
8   Business and Professions Code sections 17200, et seq. (“UCL”).  Plaintiff also asserts claims for 
9   breach of express warranties and unjust enrichment.  Plaintiff seeks injunctive relief, declaratory 
10   relief, disgorgement, restitution, and damages.                       
11                               ANALYSIS                                  
12   A.   Applicable Legal Standard.                                       
13        A motion to dismiss is proper under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) where the 
14   pleadings fail to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.  A court’s “inquiry is limited to 
15   the allegations in the complaint, which are accepted as true and construed in the light most 
16   favorable to the plaintiff.”  Lazy Y Ranch Ltd. v. Behrens, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1361176"><a href="/opinion/1361176/lazy-y-ranch-ltd-v-behrens/#588" aria-description="Citation for case: Lazy Y Ranch Ltd. v. Behrens">546 F.3d 580, 588</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).  Even 
17   under the liberal pleading standard of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(a)(2), “a plaintiff’s 
18   obligation to provide ‘grounds’ of his ‘entitle[ment] to relief’ requires more than labels and 
19   conclusions, and formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.”  Bell Atl. 
20   Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 555</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007) (citing Papasan v. Allain, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9430664"><a href="/opinion/111742/papasan-v-allain/#286" aria-description="Citation for case: Papasan v. Allain">478 U.S. 265, 286</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1986)).  
21   Pursuant to Twombly, a plaintiff cannot merely allege conduct that is conceivable but must instead 
22   allege “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.”  Id. at 570.  “A claim 
23   has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the 
24   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 
25   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (citing Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#556" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 556</a></span><pre class="inline">).

10   B.   Plaintiff’s Consumer Deception Claims Fail to Satisfy the Reasonable Consumer

11

12                                                                         
    “reasonable consumer” standard, i.e. that members of the public are likely to be deceived.  See 
13                                                                         
    Williams v. Gerber Prods. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d 934, 938</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008); see Fink v. Time Warner Cable, 
14                                                                         
    </pre><span class="citation" data-id="866709"><a href="/opinion/866709/fink-v-time-warner-cable/#741" aria-description="Citation for case: Fink v. Time Warner Cable">714 F.3d 739, 741</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2d Cir. 2013) (citing Oswego Laborers Local 214 Pension Fund v. Marine 
15                                                                         
    Midland Bank, N.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2086398"><a href="/opinion/2086398/oswego-laborers-local-214-pension-fund-v-marine-midland-bank-n-a/#26" aria-description="Citation for case: Oswego Laborers&#x27; Local 214 Pension Fund v. Marine Midland...">85 N.Y.2d 20, 26</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1995)).  “Likely to deceive” implies more than a mere 
16                                                                         
    possibility that the advertisement might conceivably be misunderstood by some few consumers 
17                                                                         
    viewing it in an unreasonable manner.  Rather, the phrase indicates that the representation is such 
18                                                                         
    that it is probable that a significant portion of the general consuming public or of targeted 
19                                                                         
    consumers, acting reasonably in the circumstances, could be misled.  Lavie v. Procter &amp; Gamble 
20                                                                         
    Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9749498"><a href="/opinion/2283365/lavie-v-procter-gamble-co/#508" aria-description="Citation for case: Lavie v. Procter &amp; Gamble Co.">105 Cal. 21                                                                         
    “deceptive advertisements were likely to mislead a reasonable consumer acting reasonably under 
22                                                                         
    the circumstances”).

    misrepresentations were not false, misleading, or deceptive as a matter of law.  See, e.g., In re

4        1.   “High Protein”                                              
5        Plaintiff argues that the phrase “high protein” on the front packaging is misleading because 
6   it deceives consumers into thinking that the chips are “high” in protein.  Plaintiff alleges that the 
7   FDA permits labeling products as “high protein” only where the product contains at least twenty 
8   percent of the daily reference value (“DRV”) of protein.  (FAC ¶ 32 (citing </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 C.F.R. § 101.54</span><pre class="inline">).)  
9   Plaintiff alleges that the Product, which contains 3 grams of protein or 6% of the DRV, does not 
10   meet this threshold and is thus misleading.                           
11        Defendant first argues that Plaintiff’s theory of consumer deception fails because a 
12   reasonable consumer viewing the packaging in context would understand that the phrase “high 
13   protein” refers to the lentil flour from which the Products are made rather than the protein content 
14   of the Product itself.                                                
15        To determine whether Defendant’s labeling would mislead a reasonable consumer, the 
16   Court must consider the packaging “as a whole.”  Freeman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="706088"><a href="/opinion/706088/michael-freeman-plaintiff-appellant-v-the-time-inc-magazine-company/#290" aria-description="Citation for case: Michael FREEMAN, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. the TIME, INC.,...">68 F.3d at 290</a></span><pre class="inline">; Weiss v. Trader Joe’s 
17   Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">838 F. App’x 302</span><pre class="inline">, 303 (9th Cir. 2021) (“[P]roduct packaging should be examined in its full 
18   context because it would be unreasonable to cherry-pick discrete statements to prove deception.”).  
19   Viewing the packaging as a whole, the Court finds that the phrase “high protein” would not lead 
20   the reasonable consumer to believe that the chips themselves are high in protein.  The phrase “high 
21   protein” never appears in isolation.  It is used on the packaging to describe lentils and lentil flour.2  
22   It is implausible that a reasonable consumer would understand the phrase “high protein” to refer to 
23   the quantity of protein contained in the chips.  See Stewart v. Kodiak Cakes, LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">568 F. Supp. 3d 
24</span><pre class="inline">   1056, 1069 (S.D. Cal. 2021) (finding it implausible that “healthy” statement made in the context 
25   of describing whole grains would deceive consumer as to fat content of products).   
26        Plaintiff contends that the presence of the phrase “high protein” is misleading regardless of 
27                                                                         
1   the context.  In opposition, Plaintiff asserts that the phrase “high protein lentils” with an arrow 
2   pointing to the chips would deceive the consumer and argues that the phrase “high protein lentils 
3   finely milled into flour” is in a small print.  These arguments fail to meaningfully address the fact 
4   that “high protein” is never used in isolation and always used in connection with lentils or lentil 
5   flour, not the chips themselves.  Accepting Plaintiff’s arguments would require the Court to 
6   impermissibly ignore the context of the packaging.  Moreover, to the extent Plaintiff contends that 
7   the phrase “high protein” is misleading in any context unless it complies with FDA regulations, 
8   the Court is unconvinced.  Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged that the reasonable consumer is 
9   sufficiently aware of the FDA regulations such that they would be misled by the Product’s alleged 
10   lack of conformity with the regulations.  See Forrett v. Gourmet Nut, Inc., No. 22-cv-02045-BLF, 
11   </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 6768217</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 11, 2022) (finding that “a reasonable consumer is not 
12   sufficiently familiar with FDA regulations to know the guideline for “high” in protein is ten 
13   grams.”).                                                             
14        The Court concludes Plaintiff has failed to plausibly allege consumer deception based on 
15   the “high protein” statements on the front label.                     
16        2.   “Protein-Packed”                                            
17        Plaintiff also challenges the phrase “protein-packed” on the back label of the package.  The 
18   phrase appears in a sentence reading “This chip’s unique recipe results in a protein-packed, 
19   crunchy experience, bursting with flavor as you savor a great-tasing ‘better-for-you’ chip.”  (FAC 
20   ¶ 22.)  Plaintiff alleges that the phrase “protein-packed” leads consumers to believe the products 
21   are “high” in protein or constitute an excellent source of protein.   
22        Defendant challenges this theory as implausible for three reasons: (1) the phrase “protein-
23   packed” is non-actionable puffery; (2) the Nutrition Facts panel resolves any ambiguity over the 
24   protein content of the chips; and (3) Plaintiff has failed to adequately allege he relied on the 
25   “protein-packed” statement in deciding to purchase the chips.         
26        The Ninth Circuit has held that “generalized, vague and unspecific assertions constitute[e] 
27   mere ‘puffery’ upon which a reasonable consumer [cannot] rely.”  Glen Holly Entm’t, Inc. v.

2   misdescriptions of specific or absolute characteristics of a product are actionable.”  Cook, Perkiss

10   buyer would rely” and the latter was a “specific and measurable advertisement claim of product 
11   superiority based on product testing.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6945094"><a href="/opinion/7041988/southland-sod-farms-v-stover-seed-co/#1145" aria-description="Citation for case: Southland Sod Farms v. 12        Here, Defendant’s use of “protein-packed” is puffery.  “Packed” is not a “specific and 
13   measurable claim” that is “capable of being proved false or of being reasonably interpreted as 
14   statement of objective fact.”  Coastal Abstract Serv., Inc. v. First Am. Title Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="763256"><a href="/opinion/763256/coastal-abstract-service-inc-v-first-american-title-insurance-company/" aria-description="Citation for case: Coastal Abstract Service, Inc. v. First American Title...">173 F.3d 725</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
15   731 (9th Cir. 1998).  Like the statement “packed with power” in Elias and “less is more” in 
16   Southland Sod, “protein packed” is a generalized claim about the Product that is not measurable.  
17   There is no objective definition of “protein packed,” and even if there were, Plaintiff has not 
18   alleged one.                                                          
19        The context in which the phrase appears on the packaging further underscores the 
20   conclusion that the phrase is puffery.  The full statement reads: “This chip’s unique recipe results 
21   in a protein-packed, crunchy experience, bursting with flavor as you savor a great-tasting ‘better-
22   for-you’ chip.”  (FAC ¶ 20.)  This statement “merely states in general terms that one product is 
23   superior,” and accordingly, is not actionable.  Cook, Perkiss and Liehe, Inc., 911 F.2d at 246.  A 
24   reasonable consumer viewing the packaging would not interpret the phrase as a factual claim 
25   about the protein content in the chips, but rather a subjective claim that Defendant’s chips are 
26   superior to other chip brands.

5   source,” “contains,” or “provides”).                                  
6        Recently, a district court considering a similar “protein-packed” claim determined that the

8   based claims under the UCL, FAL, and CLRA and breach of warranty claims based on the

13   the plaintiff’s theory of deception to the extent it was based on 21 C.F.R. section 101.54 because 
14   “packed” was not an FDA-regulated term.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  Forrett is instructive here.

8        Plaintiff relies on Williams to argue that Defendant cannot rely on information in the 
9   Nutrition Facts panel to correct a misstatement elsewhere on the packaging.  In Williams, the 
10   Ninth Circuit said that “reasonable consumers should [not] be expected to look beyond misleading 
11   representations on the front of the box to discover the truth from the ingredient list in small print

18   diagrams adorning the packaging, as there were in Williams, from which any inference could be 
19   drawn or on which any reasonable belief could be based,” a reasonable consumer can be expected 
20   to look at other parts of the label.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8414293"><a href="/opinion/8442970/ebner-v-fresh-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ebner v. Fresh, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (emphases in original).

5   labeling claims that were intended to appeal to consumers interested in protein-focused products” 
6   and “to the best of his recollection, [he] read and relied on Defendant’s labeling representations 
7   including… ‘protein-packed’…”  (Id. ¶ 10.)                            
8        Defendant argues the Court should not consider Plaintiff’s allegations of reliance because 
9   they are inconsistent with his initial complaint, in which he alleged that he relied on “the high 
10   protein representations printed on the front of the Product.”  (Dkt. No. 1, Compl. ¶ 8.)  Thus, 
11   Defendant urges the Court to reject Plaintiff’s inconsistent allegations.

13   earlier assertion made in the same proceeding.”  Airs Aromatics, LLC v. Victoria’s Secret Stores 
14   Brand Mgmt., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2655174"><a href="/opinion/2655174/airs-aromatics-llc-v-opinion-victorias-secret-stores-brand-management/#600" aria-description="Citation for case: Airs Aromatics, LLC v. Opinion Victoria&#x27;s Secret Stores...">744 F.3d 595, 600</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014) (internal citation and quotation marks 
15   omitted).  However, the allegations in the amended complaint do not directly contradict those in 
16   the initial complaint.  In the initial complaint, Plaintiff alleged he “relied upon Defendant’s 
17   labeling and advertising claims, namely, the high protein representations” on the front label.  
18   (Compl. ¶ 8.)  Plaintiff’s amended complaint is not a direct contradiction of that allegation.  He 
19   does not disclaim his reliance on the front label, rather, he clarifies that he read and relied on the 
20   back label as well.  The Court does not find this allegation directly contradictory.  However, 
21   Plaintiff’s claims based on the “protein packed” representation nevertheless fail for the reasons 
22   discussed above.                                                      
23   C.   Plaintiff Fails to Plausibly Allege Reliance For his Omissions-Based Theory. 
24        Plaintiff’s final theory of consumer deception is based on Defendant’s purported omission 
25   of the DRV percentage for protein.  Plaintiff alleges Defendant was required to include the DRV, 
26   and Defendant’s failure to disclose this information was false and misleading.  Defendant argues 
27   this theory fails because Plaintiff does not allege that he saw or relied on the Nutrition Facts panel 
1        Actual reliance is an essential element of a fraudulent omission claim.  Cohen v. 
2   DIRECTV, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2260165"><a href="/opinion/2260165/cohen-v-directv-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cohen v. DirecTV, Inc.">178 Cal.App.4th 966</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009).  A plaintiff may demonstrate actual reliance by 
3   pleading that “had the omitted information been disclosed, he would have been aware of it and 
4   behaved differently.’”  Daniel v. Ford Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3159382"><a href="/opinion/3159382/margie-daniel-v-ford-motor-company/#1225" aria-description="Citation for case: Margie Daniel v. Ford Motor Company">806 F.3d 1217, 1225</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2015) (quoting 
5   Mirkin v. Wasserman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9539140"><a href="/opinion/1154737/mirkin-v-wasserman/#1093" aria-description="Citation for case: Mirkin v. Wasserman">5 Cal. 4th 1082, 1093</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1993)).                   
6        Here, Plaintiff alleges he “relied upon Defendant’s labeling and advertising claims, 
7   namely, the protein representations and omissions printed on the front and back of the Product.”  
8   (FAC ¶ 9.)  He also alleges “the products did not have a percentage of Daily Recommended Value 
9   (“DRV”) listed for protein.”  (Id. ¶ 10.)                             
10        These conclusory allegations are insufficient to establish that Plaintiff relied on the 
11   purported omission of the DRV in deciding to purchase the Product.  Plaintiff’s vague allegation 
12   that he relied on “labeling claims” on the “front and back of the Product” does not establish that he 
13   reviewed or relied on the Nutrition Facts panel in deciding to make his purchase.  Nor does the 
14   allegation that “the products did not have a percentage of Daily Recommended Value (“DRV”) 
15   listed for protein” establish that he read the Nutrition Facts panel or that its contents affected his 
16   purchasing decision.  Absent allegations that he read and relied on the Nutrition Facts panel, 
17   Plaintiff cannot plausibly allege that he was deceived by its lack of disclosures.  Pardini v. 
18   Unilever U.S., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8710399"><a href="/opinion/8727201/pardini-v-unilever-united-states-inc/#1060" aria-description="Citation for case: Pardini v. Unilever United States, Inc.">961 F. Supp. 2d 1048, 1060</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2013) (“Plaintiff has not pled that she 
19   ever looked at the nutrition panel.  As such, it is implausible that she was deceived by its lack of 
20   disclosures.”).  Thus, Plaintiff has failed to plausibly allege that had the omitted information been 
21   disclosed, he would have been aware of it and behaved differently.  The court dismisses the claims 
22   premised on this theory with leave to amend.
