8                              JURISDICTION                               
9     A preliminary issue is the court’s jurisdiction because the plaintiffs have withdrawn their 
10   federal claims, eliminating federal-question jurisdiction. They also allege diversity jurisdiction. 
11   Federal courts have original jurisdiction if there is compete diversity of citizenship among the 
12   opposing parties, and the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs. 
13   </pre><span class="citation no-link">28 U.S.C. § 1332</span><pre class="inline">(a)(1).                                               
14     The parties are completely diverse.14 The parties do not dispute the amount in controversy. But 
15   the court must consider jurisdiction as a threshold issue. The issue here is whether the plaintiffs 
16   have met their burden to establish the amount in controversy. Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. 
17   of Am., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="117845"><a href="/opinion/117845/kokkonen-v-guardian-life-insurance-co-of-america/#377" aria-description="Citation for case: Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America">511 U.S. 375, 377</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1994)                                      
18     “The amount in controversy includes claims for general and special damages (excluding costs 
19   and interest), attorney[’]s fees if recoverable by statute or contract, and punitive damages if 
20   recoverable as a matter of law.” J. Marymount, Inc. v. Bayer Healthcare, LLC, No. C 09-03110 
21   JSW, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2009 WL 4510126</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 30, 2009) (quotation omitted). “Generally, the 
22   amount in controversy is determined from the face of the pleadings.” Crum v. Circus Circus 
23   Enterprises, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="771047"><a href="/opinion/771047/gail-crum-v-circus-circus-enterprises-circus-circus-casinos-inc-circus/#1131" aria-description="Citation for case: Gail Crum v. 24   long as the claim is made in good faith.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="771047"><a href="/opinion/771047/gail-crum-v-circus-circus-enterprises-circus-circus-casinos-inc-circus/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gail Crum v. 16                          STANDARD OF REVIEW                             
17     A complaint must contain a “short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is 
18   entitled to relief” to give the defendant “fair notice” of what the claims are and the grounds upon 
19   which they rest. Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2); Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 555</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007). A 
20   complaint does not need detailed factual allegations, but “a plaintiff’s obligation to provide the 
21   ‘grounds’ of his ‘entitlement to relief’ requires more than labels and conclusions, and a formulaic 
22   recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do. Factual allegations must be enough to 
23   raise a claim for relief above the speculative level[.]” Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 555</a></span><pre class="inline"> (cleaned up). 
24     To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual allegations, which 
25   when accepted as true, “‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="85610"><a href="/opinion/85610/ross-v-doe-on-the-demise-of-barland/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ross v. Doe on the Demise of Barland">26   U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 570</a></span><pre class="inline">). “A claim has facial plausibility when 
27   the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the 
1   ‘probability requirement,’ but it asks for more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted 
2   unlawfully.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. 21                               ANALYSIS                                  
22     The Citi defendants moved to dismiss the remaining claims on the following grounds: (1) 
23   claim one for declaratory relief because it is predicated only on past wrongs; (2) claim five for 
24   negligence because Citi does not owe the plaintiffs a duty of care; (3) claim six for conversion 
25   because the plaintiffs did not have a possessory right to the $40,000 at the time of the alleged 
26   conversion, and the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC) in any event preempts the claim; (6) claim 
27   seven for the common count for money had and received because it is predicated on underlying 
1   plausibly plead a breach of the line-of-credit agreement; (8) claim nine for financial elder abuse 
2   because the plaintiffs did not plead the claim with the requisite particularity under Rule 9(b); and 
3   (9) claim 10 for a violation of the UCL because it is predicted on the dismissed claims.15 The court 
4   grants the motion to dismiss.                                         
5                                                                         
6   1.  Claim One: Declaratory Relief                                     
7     The court dismisses the claim for declaratory relief because the plaintiffs seek to redress past 
8   wrongs covered by their other claims.                                 
9     The parties do not dispute that the federal Declaratory Judge Act applies in diversity cases.16 
10   </pre><span class="citation no-link">28 U.S.C. § 2201</span><pre class="inline">; Guccione v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A., No. 14-cv-04587-LB, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2015 WL 
11</span><pre class="inline">   1968114, at *20–21 (N.D. Cal. May 1, 2015) (citing In re Adobe Sys., Inc. Privacy Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8851821"><a href="/opinion/8866232/bridgens-v-dollar-sav-bank-of-kansas-city/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bridgens v. Dollar Sav. Bank of Kansas City">66 F. 
12</a></span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d 1197, 1219 (N.D. Cal. 2014)). The Act permits a federal court to “declare the rights and 
13   legal relations” of parties to “a case of actual controversy.” Khalsa v. Hali, No. 13-cv-03575-PSG, 
14   </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 3883713</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 6, 2014) (quoting </pre><span class="citation no-link">28 U.S.C. § 220</span><pre class="inline">; other quotation 
15   omitted). A court applies a two-step inquiry to determine whether a declaratory judgment is 
16   appropriate: (1) it considers whether a case or controversy exists within the court’s jurisdiction, 
17   and (2) if it does, it must decide whether to exercise jurisdiction. </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> (quotation omitted).  
18     Here, the plaintiffs generally allege tortious conduct from the alleged wrongful retention of the 
19   $40,0000: negligence, conversion, and financial elder abuse.17 Past tortious conduct does not 
20   provide an adequate basis for a declaratory relief action. “[N]o basis for declaratory relief exists 
21   where only past wrongs are involved and one single judgment will likely resolve the parties’ 
22   dispute without any impact on future conduct.” Id.; Philips Med. Cap., LLC v. Med. Insights 
23   Diagnostics Ctr., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2530589"><a href="/opinion/2530589/philips-medical-capital-llc-v-medical-insights-diagnostics-centers-inc/#1048" aria-description="Citation for case: Philips Medical Capital, LLC v. Medical Insights...">471 F. Supp. 2d 1035, 1048</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2007) (in case involving contract 
24   and tort claims, the court dismissed the claim for declaratory relief; “Declaratory relief is

7   2.  Claim Five: Negligence                                            
8     The court dismisses the negligence claim because the plaintiffs did not plausibly plead that the 
9   defendants owed them a duty of care.                                  
10     The elements of negligence are duty, breach, causation, and damages. See Hayes v. Cnty. of 
11   San Diego, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1038647"><a href="/opinion/1038647/hayes-v-county-of-san-diego/#629" aria-description="Citation for case: Hayes v. 12   unless their involvement in a transaction goes beyond their “conventional role as a mere lender of 
13   money.” See, e.g., Nymark v. Heart Fed. Sav. &amp; Loan Ass’n, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2118918"><a href="/opinion/2118918/nymark-v-heart-federal-savings-loan-assn/" aria-description="Citation for case: Nymark v. Heart Federal Savings &amp; Loan Ass&#x27;n">231 Cal. App. 3d 1089</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1095–96 
14   (1991). Still, “Nymark does not support the sweeping conclusion that a lender never owes a duty 
15   of care to a borrower.” Alvarez v. BAC Home Loans Servicing, L.P., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5667301"><a href="/opinion/5811809/alvarez-v-bag-home-loans-servicing-lp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Alvarez v. Bag Home Loans Servicing, L.P.">228 Cal. App. 4th 941</a></span><pre class="inline">, 945 
16   (2014). A duty may arise even where the lender remains within its “conventional role” of merely 
17   loaning money. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5667301"><a href="/opinion/5811809/alvarez-v-bag-home-loans-servicing-lp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Alvarez v. Bag Home Loans Servicing, L.P.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> For example, in Alvarez, the court held that in light of California’s Homeowner 
18   Bill of Rights, once a lender undertakes to consider a loan-modification request, it owes the 
19   borrower a duty to use reasonable care in handling that request. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5667301"><a href="/opinion/5811809/alvarez-v-bag-home-loans-servicing-lp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Alvarez v. Bag Home Loans Servicing, L.P.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 945–52.

24   lender and thus do not plausibly plead a duty of care. Nymark, 231 Cal. App. 3d at 1095–96; see 
25   Gosal v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., No. 18-cv-00908 JAM AC (PS), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WL 2984875</span><pre class="inline">, at *5 (E.D.

12   3.  Claim Six: Conversion                                             
13     Citi contends that the plaintiffs do not plausibly plead conversion because Mr. Chie paid the 
14   $40,000 to Citi to apply to the home-equity line of credit, and the UCC in any event preempts the 
15   claim.22 The plaintiffs do not state a claim for conversion.          
16     “[A]ny act of dominion wrongfully exerted over the personal property of another inconsistent 
17   with the owner’s rights thereto constitutes conversion.” Mindys Cosmetics, Inc. v. Dakar, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="149846"><a href="/opinion/149846/mercado-berrios-v-cancel-alegria/#590" aria-description="Citation for case: Mercado-Berrios v. Cancel-Alegria">611 F.3d 
18   590, 601</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2010) (quoting McCafferty v. Gilbank, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2190374"><a href="/opinion/2190374/mccafferty-v-gilbank/#576" aria-description="Citation for case: McCafferty v. Gilbank">249 Cal. App. 2d 569, 576</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1967)). To 
19   prove conversion, a plaintiff “must establish: (1) ownership of or right to possess the property in 
20   question at the time of the conversion, (2) that the defendant disposed of the plaintiff’s property 
21   rights or converted the property by a wrongful act, and (3) damages.” Swingless Golf Club Corp. 
22   v. Taylor, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2475734"><a href="/opinion/2475734/swingless-golf-club-corp-v-taylor/#910" aria-description="Citation for case: Swingless Golf Club Corp. v. Taylor">732 F. Supp. 2d 899, 910</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2010) (citing Oakdale Village Grp. v. Fong, 43 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">23   Cal. App. 4th 539</span><pre class="inline">, 544 (1996)).                                       
24     In circumstances like these, when a person tenders money to a bank, intending that it be 
25   credited to the person’s loan, the person does not retain possessory interest in the money and 
26                                                                         
27                                                                         
1   instead has surrendered that interest to the bank. As a result, there is no claim for conversion. See 
2   Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2650741"><a href="/opinion/2650741/rutherford-holdings-llc-v-plaza-del-rey/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey">223 Cal. App. 4th 221</a></span><pre class="inline">, 233–34 (2014). 
3     In Rutherford Holdings, for example, a buyer contracted with a vendor to buy a mobile-home 
4   park and paid a $3 million deposit that was nonrefundable unless the vendor materially breached the 
5   purchase agreement or refused to close. The closing date passed, and neither party performed. The 
6   buyer sued under theories of recovery that included conversion. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2650741"><a href="/opinion/2650741/rutherford-holdings-llc-v-plaza-del-rey/#225" aria-description="Citation for case: Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey">Id. at 225, 233</a></span><pre class="inline">. Because the escrow 
7   agreement did not have a contractual provision for return of the money, title to the deposit transferred 
8   to the vendor on deposit. Id.at 234–35. The buyer thus could not allege that he owned or possessed 
9   the deposit at the time of the alleged conversion and did not state a conversion claim. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2650741"><a href="/opinion/2650741/rutherford-holdings-llc-v-plaza-del-rey/#235" aria-description="Citation for case: Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey">Id. at 235</a></span><pre class="inline">.  
10     Other cases reach similar results in the context of deposits with banks. Schertzer v. Bank of 
11   Am., N.A., </pre><span class="citation no-link">445 F. Supp. 3d 1058</span><pre class="inline">, 1092–93 (S.D. Cal. 2020) (dismissing conversion claim because 
12   “[a]ny money became the literal property of [Bank of America] upon deposit in the bank and not 
13   subject to a claim of conversion”); Chavez v. Bank of Am. Corp., No. C 10-0653-JCS, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 
14</span><pre class="inline">   1594272, at *11 (N.D. Cal. May 4, 2012) (“California law is well-settled in this area that a claim 
15   against the bank for conversion will not lie. Because title to a deposit passes immediately to the 
16   bank upon deposit, a depositor has no conversion claim against a bank under California law”). 
17     Because there is no plausible claim for conversion, on this briefing, the court does not reach 
18   Citi’s argument that the UCC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Com. Code § 3420</span><pre class="inline">(a), preempts the conversion claim.

21     The court dismisses the common count for money had and received because the claim is

24   to be used for the benefit of the plaintiff; (2) the money was not used for the plaintiff’s benefit;

27   Cal. App. 4th at 230. The claim is viable “whenever one person has received money which 
1   belongs to another, and which in equity and good conscience should be paid over to the latter.”

1   6.  Claim Nine: Financial Elder Abuse                                 
2     The defendants move to dismiss the plaintiffs’ ninth claim for financial elder abuse because the 
3   plaintiffs failed to plead Citi’s scienter with particularity under Rule 9(b).26 The plaintiff did not 
4   plead Citi’s wrongful intent, and the court thus dismisses the claim.  
5     Elder financial abuse occurs when a person or entity “[t]akes, secretes, appropriates or retains 
6   real or personal property of an elder” or assists in doing so “for a wrongful use or with intent to 
7   defraud, or both.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Welf. &amp; Inst. Code § 15610.30</span><pre class="inline">(a)(1), (2); York v. Bank of Am., No. 14-cv-
8   02471-RS, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 WL 392928</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 2, 2016). “A person or entity shall be deemed 
9   to have taken, secreted, appropriated, obtained, or retained property for a wrongful use if, among 
10   other things, the person or entity knew or should have known that this conduct is likely to be 
11   harmful to the elder or dependent adult” sixty-five years of age or over. </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Welf. &amp; Inst. Code §§ 
12</span><pre class="inline">   15610.27, .30(b). “Thus, a plaintiff must aver (1) that the defendant took, secreted, appropriated or 
13   retained real or personal property, (2) of an elder, (3) for a wrongful use or with intent to defraud, 
14   or both.” York, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 WL 392928</span><pre class="inline"> at *4 (citing Stebley v. Litton Loan Servicing, LLP, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3302348"><a href="/opinion/3303055/charles-l-donohoe-co-v-superior-court-of-glenn/" aria-description="Citation for case: Charles L. Donohoe Co. v. Superior Court of Glenn">202 Cal. 
15</a></span><pre class="inline">   App. 4th 522, 528 (2012)).
