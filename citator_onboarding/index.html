The AI Citator

A citator answers one question a lawyer asks constantly: “Is this case still good law?” It tracks how every later decision has treated an earlier one — affirmed, reversed, overruled, distinguished, criticized, and so on. Historically, commercial citators did this with large manual editorial teams. We are building an open, AI-powered citator on top of CourtListener’s corpus of U.S. court opinions. This site walks the project: the benchmark we measure against, the experiments that got us here, and the design we’re currently iterating on.

The actors — quick glossary

Citing case
The opinion we’re reading — the one containing the citation.
Cited case
An earlier opinion referenced by the citing case. A citing case typically references many cited cases — the citator’s job is to classify how each one was treated.
Target case
The specific cited case currently being classified or analyzed.
Acting case
The case that actually performs the treatment. Usually this is the citing case itself; the exception is Related Reference treatments, where the citing case only narrates that some third opinion acted on the cited case.

The Taxonomy page has the full definitions, the direction diagrams, the four severity tiers, and the 43-treatment grid — everything definitional lives there.

Why a citator matters

For the lawyer

Every brief, motion, and ruling stands on cited authorities. If the authority was overruled or reversed, the argument collapses. A citator is what a researcher checks before relying on a case — it is part of the irreducible workflow of legal practice.

For open law

Many existing citators are closed, commercial, and built on opaque editorial work. An open, transparent citator over the public corpus removes a major moat that keeps legal research expensive and proprietary — especially for solo practitioners, non-profits, and researchers.

Why it’s a hard problem

Extreme class skew

About 94% of all citations are the neutral “Cited by.” The rare classes (Overruled, Abrogated, Questioned) are exactly the ones lawyers care most about — and the ones with the fewest examples to evaluate against.

Interpretive labels

Treatments are not deterministic facts. Did a court “Limit” a holding or just “Distinguish” it? At the margins, experts disagree — which makes both ground truth and evaluation genuinely hard.

Messy source text

Opinion text is assembled from publisher markup, often falling back to OCR. Broken paragraphs, non-breaking-space noise, trial-court line numbers, and duplicate records all sit upstream of the modeling problem and cap the achievable quality.

Scope and attribution

A single paragraph can cite three cases and distinguish only one of them. Working out which sentence applies to which citation, whether the court is acting itself or merely reporting another court’s treatment, takes real legal reading — not pattern matching.
