18   Grand Canyon Education Incorporated (“GCE”), Brian Mueller, Dan Bachus, and Stan

21     I.   Background2                                                   
22        This case arises out of Ogdon’s enrollment in an online master’s degree program 
23   offered by Grand Canyon University (“University”). GCE once operated the University as 
24   a for-profit institution, but after the Department of Education imposed regulations on for-
25   profit colleges to better inform prospective students about their odds of getting a job in 
26   their chosen field, Defendants restructured in an effort to evade those new regulations.

7     II.  Legal Standard                                                
8        “To avoid a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, a complaint need not contain detailed factual 
9   allegations; rather, it must plead ‘enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on 
10   its face.’” Clemens v. DaimlerChrysler Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1199775"><a href="/opinion/1199775/clemens-v-daimlerchrysler-corp/#1022" aria-description="Citation for case: Clemens v. DaimlerChrysler Corp.">534 F.3d 1017, 1022</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008) 
11   (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007)). Well-pled factual allegations 
12   are accepted as true and construed in the light most favorable to the plaintiff. Cousins v. 
13   Lockyer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1459853"><a href="/opinion/1459853/cousins-v-lockyer/#1067" aria-description="Citation for case: Cousins v. Lockyer">568 F.3d 1063, 1067</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2009). The Court’s task merely is to determine 
14   whether those well-pled factual allegations plausibly state a claim to relief under governing 
15   law. See Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009).                
16     III.  Analysis                                                     
17     A. Rule 9(b)                                                       
18        The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure ordinarily require a complaint to contain only 
19   “a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief,” Fed. 
20   R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2), but they set a heightened pleading standard for allegations of fraud. “In 
21   alleging fraud . . . a party must state with particularity the circumstances constituting fraud 
22   or mistake. Malice, intent, knowledge, and other conditions of a person’s mind maybe 
23   alleged generally.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b). “Averments of fraud must be accompanied by the 
24   who, what, when, where, and how of the misconduct charged.” Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. 
25   USA, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1106" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d 1097, 1106</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2003) (quotations and citation omitted). Typically, 
26   Rule 9(b)’s heightened pleading standard applies only to averments of fraud; “[t]he rule 
27   does not require that allegations supporting a claim be stated with particularity when those 
28   allegations  describe  non-fraudulent  conduct.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1104" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">Id.  at  1104</a></span><pre class="inline">.  But  “[i]n  some  cases,  the 
1   plaintiff may allege a unified course of fraudulent conduct and rely entirely on that course 
2   of conduct as the basis of a claim. In that event, the claim is said to be ‘grounded in fraud’ 
3   or  to  ‘sound  in  fraud,’  and  the  pleading  of  that  claim  as  a  whole  must  satisfy  the 
4   particularity requirement of Rule 9(b).” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1103" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">Id. at 1103-04</a></span><pre class="inline">.             
5        Defendants argue—and Ogdon doesn’t appear to dispute—that Counts I though IV 
6   of the FAC are predicated on a unified course of fraudulent conduct, such that those claims 
7   must be plead with the specificity Rule 9(b) demands. Defendants further argue the FAC 
8   fails to satisfy Rule 9(b) because it (1) doesn’t specify which Defendants allegedly engaged 
9   in specific instances of fraud and (2) doesn’t identify the specific content of any alleged 
10   fraudulent statement. (Doc. 61-1 at 12-14.) The Court disagrees.     
11        First, the FAC adequately identifies which Defendants are being sued for what. 
12   When multiple defendants are sued in connection with an alleged fraudulent scheme, there 
13   is no requirement that the plaintiff identify every instance of fraudulent conduct for every 
14   defendant. Swartz v. KPMG LLP, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="796813"><a href="/opinion/796813/theodore-c-swartz-v-kpmg-llp-and-presidio-advisory-services-inc/#764" aria-description="Citation for case: Theodore C. Swartz v. Kpmg Llp, and Presidio Advisory...">476 F.3d 756, 764</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2007). Though “Rule 9(b) 
15   does not allow a complaint to merely lump multiple defendants together,” in fraud suits 
16   involving multiple  defendants  it is sufficient for a plaintiff to identify the role each 
17   defendant played in the alleged fraudulent scheme. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="796813"><a href="/opinion/796813/theodore-c-swartz-v-kpmg-llp-and-presidio-advisory-services-inc/#764" aria-description="Citation for case: Theodore C. Swartz v. Kpmg Llp, and Presidio Advisory...">Id. at 764-65</a></span><pre class="inline">. Here, the FAC identifies 
18   the role each Defendant played in the alleged fraudulent scheme (Doc. 18 ¶¶ 229-233.) The 
19   FAC also specifies when certain actions are alleged to have been taken on behalf or under 
20   the supervision of Defendants, it identifies by name several University counselors who 
21   made allegedly fraudulently representations, and for others it details job titles, training, and 
22   supervision.  (Id.  ¶¶  113-25,  174-85,  195-96.)  These  allegations  are  sufficient  to  put 
23   Defendants on notice of their roles in the alleged fraudulent scheme. 
24        Next,  the  FAC  identifies  the  specific  content  of  the  alleged  fraud,  namely 
25   misrepresentations about whether the University’s program met Ogdon’s needs. Ogdon 
26   claims that her various counselors, both pre- and post-enrollment, knew she wanted to 
27   practice in California and, when asked, repeatedly assured her that the program in which 
28   she was enrolled would meet the requirements for licensure in California.  
1        Defendants’ arguments to the contrary aren’t about the specificity with which 
2   Ogdon has alleged the fraud. Instead, Defendants argue that these statements are not 
3   actionable for reasons other than their specify.                     
4        Defendants argue that Granitz’s assurance that the University had an excellent 
5   program that would meet Ogdon’s needs is non-actionable puffery. The Court agrees that 
6   describing the University’s program as “excellent” is non-actionable puffery but disagrees 
7   that assuring Ogdon the program would meet her needs falls into the same category. 
8   Whether the University’s program met the needs of someone like Ogdon who wanted to 
9   practice as a mental health therapist in California is not necessarily a statement of opinion: 
10   it’s plausibly a fact capable of verification.                       
11        Defendants next argue that Granitz’s alleged representations about the University’s 
12   accreditation are not actionable because they are true. This argument is unpersuasive for 
13   two reasons. First, at this stage, the Court accepts the allegations in the complaint as true, 
14   so the Court must presume that Ogdon’s program was not properly accredited. Second, on 
15   this point the parties talk crossways. Defendants argue that Grantiz’s alleged representation 
16   was true because the University is regionally accredited as an institution by HLC, and 
17   California does not accredit discrete college programs. But in her response brief, Ogdon 
18   explains that she has used the word “accreditation” in her FAC as a term of art to describe 
19   whether a professional degree program meets the minimum requirements for obtaining a 
20   corresponding professional license in a given state. And Ogdon plausibly alleges that the 
21   program she completed at the University did not meet those requirements in California. 
22   The Court won’t dismiss the FAC based on what might turn out to be a semantic dispute.  
23        Finally, Defendants argue that post-enrollment statements by Landa and Bibb are 
24   not actionable because Ogdon could not plausibly have relied on them in making her 
25   decision to enroll at the University. This argument is unpersuasive for two reasons. First, 
26   as the Court understands the FAC, Ogdon claims the post-enrollment statements of Landa 
27   and Bibb induced her to maintain her enrollment at the University. Had she not been misled 
28   into believing the University’s program would qualify her for licensure in California, 
1   Ogdon might have cut her losses earlier and enrolled in a different institution. Second, 
2   reasonable reliance generally is a question of fact inappropriate for resolution at the motion 
3   to dismiss stage.                                                    
4        “Rules 8(a) and 9(b) must be read in conjunction with one another.  [A] [p]laintiff 
5   may state allegations of fraud in short, plain statements, provided that said statements put 
6   [d]efendants on adequate notice of the conduct alleged to be fraudulent.”  Reilly v. Charles 
7   M. Brewer, Ltd. Profit Sharing Plan, No. CIV 02-2218-PHX-EHC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2004 WL 7339615</span><pre class="inline">, at 
8   *4 (D. Ariz. Sep. 30, 2004). Indeed, Rule 9(b) serves four purposes: 
9             First,  the  rule  ensures  that  the  defendant  has  sufficient 
              information to formulate a defense by putting it on notice of 
10             the conduct complained of. . . . Second, Rule 9(b) exists to 
              protect defendants from frivolous suits.  A third reason for the 
11             rule is to eliminate fraud actions in which all the facts are 
              learned after discovery.  Finally, Rule 9(b) protects defendants 
12             from harm to their goodwill and reputation.                
13   U.S. ex rel. Stinson, Lyons, Gerlin &amp; Bustamante, P.A. v. Blue Cross Blue Shield of 
14   Georgia, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1427853"><a href="/opinion/1427853/united-states-ex-rel-stinson-lyons-gerlin-bustamante-pa-v-blue/#1056" aria-description="Citation for case: United States Ex Rel. Stinson, Lyons, Gerlin &amp;...">755 F. Supp. 1055, 1056-57</a></span><pre class="inline"> (S.D. Ga. 1990). “A court should hesitate to 
15   dismiss a complaint under Rule 9(b) if the court is satisfied (1) that the defendant has been 
16   made aware of the particular circumstances for which she will have to prepare a defense at 
17   trial, and (2) that plaintiff has substantial prediscovery evidence of those facts.” Harrison 
18   v. Westinghouse Savannah River Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="764072"><a href="/opinion/764072/edwin-p-harrison-and-united-states-of-america-party-in-interest-v/#784" aria-description="Citation for case: Edwin P. Harrison, and United States of America, Party in...">176 F.3d 776, 784</a></span><pre class="inline"> (4th Cir. 1999). Here, the Court 
19   is satisfied that Ogdon has sufficient prediscovery evidence of the misconduct she has 
20   alleged, and that the FAC is sufficiently detailed to put Defendants on fair notice of the 
21   particular circumstances for which they will need to prepare a defense.  
22     B. RICO                                                            
23        Ogdon brings claims under RICO </pre><span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 1962</span><pre class="inline">(a), (c), and (d). To state a 
24   plausible § 1962(c) claim, Ogdon must allege that Defendants “participate[d] in (1) the 
25   conduct of (2) an enterprise that affects interstate commerce (3) through a pattern (4) of 
26   racketeering activity[.]” Eclectic Properties East, LLC v. Marcus &amp; Millichap Co., 751 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="6931222"><a href="/opinion/7029292/weyherlivsey-constructors-inc-v-prevetire/#997" aria-description="Citation for case: Weyher/Livsey Constructors, Inc. v. Prevetire">27   F.3d 990, 997</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014). Section 1962(a) makes it unlawful for any person to reinvest 
28   income received from a pattern of racketeering activity. And Section 1962(d) makes it 
1   unlawful to conspire to violate these other provisions. Defendants raise a host of arguments 
2   for why Ogdon’s RICO claims fail, but the Court need not address them all because it finds 
3   two dispositive.                                                     
4        First, Ogdon has not plausibly alleged that Defendants conducted the affairs of a 
5   separate, distinct enterprise. RICO defines enterprise broadly to include “any individual, 
6   partnership, corporation, association, or other legal entity, and any union or group of 
7   individuals associated in fact although not a legal entity.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 1961</span><pre class="inline">(4).  “[A]n 
8   association-in-fact enterprise is . . . a continuing unit that functions with a common 
9   purpose.” Boyle v. United States, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="145864"><a href="/opinion/145864/boyle-v-united-states/#944" aria-description="Citation for case: Boyle v. United States">556 U.S. 938, 944, 948</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009). However, “proof of a 
10   ‘pattern of racketeering activity’ is not, by itself, proof of an ‘enterprise.’”  Odom v. 
11   Microsoft Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="797760"><a href="/opinion/797760/james-odom-v-microsoft-corporation-a-washington-corporation-best-buy-co/#549" aria-description="Citation for case: James Odom v. Microsoft Corporation, a Washington...">486 F.3d 541, 549</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2007) (internal citation omitted). “The 
12   enterprise and its activity are two separate things,” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="797760"><a href="/opinion/797760/james-odom-v-microsoft-corporation-a-washington-corporation-best-buy-co/#551" aria-description="Citation for case: James Odom v. Microsoft Corporation, a Washington...">Id. at 551</a></span><pre class="inline">, and liability under RICO 
13   “depends on showing that the defendants conducted or participated in the conduct of the 
14   ‘enterprise’s affairs,’ and not just their own affairs.” Reves v. Ernst &amp; Young, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="112817"><a href="/opinion/112817/united-states-v-louisiana/#170" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Louisiana">507 U.S. 
15   170, 185</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1993) (emphasis in original). Here, the FAC plausibly alleges that Defendants 
16   fraudulently  induced  Ogdon  into  enrolling  and  maintaining  her  enrollment  at  the 
17   University. But the FAC does not plausibly allege that Defendants’ conduct were anything 
18   other  than  their  ordinary  business  affairs.  Indeed,  Ogdon  alleges  that  the  “common 
19   purpose” underpinning the alleged enterprise was to enrich GCE, its officers, and its 
20   shareholders. (Doc. 18 ¶¶ 51-55.) But profit maximization is a normal purpose of a for-
21   profit corporation like GCE. Although Defendants are not entitled to conduct their business 
22   affairs in a fraudulent manner, doing so does not transform those affairs into the conduct 
23   of an association-in-fact enterprise that is separate and distinct from the business itself.  
24        Second, Ogdon’s FAC does not allege each Defendant committed at least two acts 
25   of racketeering activity. “[I]n a multi-defendant § 1962(c) action, such as this one, a 
26   plaintiff must allege that each defendant individually committed at least two predicate 
27   acts.” Kerrigan v. ViSalus, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7232071"><a href="/opinion/7314166/kerrigan-v-visalus-inc/#605" aria-description="Citation for case: Kerrigan v. Visalus, Inc.">112 F. Supp. 3d 580, 605</a></span><pre class="inline"> (E.D. Mich. 2015). The FAC 
28                                                                        
1   fails to specifically and particularly allege that each named Defendant committed at least 
2   two separate acts of mail or wire fraud.                             
3        For these reasons, the Court dismisses Ogdon’s RICO claims.     
4     C. State Law Claims                                                
5        1.  FAL                                                         
6        Ogdon claims Defendants violated California’s FAL, </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 
7</span><pre class="inline">   17500, which makes it unlawful for a business to disseminate any statement that “is untrue 
8   or misleading, and which is known, or which by the exercise of reasonable care should be 
9   known, to be untrue or misleading.” Schellenbach v. GoDaddy.com, LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8777596"><a href="/opinion/8793587/schellenbach-v-godaddycom-llc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Schellenbach v. GoDaddy.com, LLC">321 F.R.D. 613</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
10   619 (D. Ariz. 2017) (internal citations omitted). Defendants argue that this claim fails 
11   because  it  is  predicated  on  private  conversations  between  Ogdon  and  University 
12   counselors, rather than on advertisements disseminated to the public. 
13        The Court is not persuaded that the FAL requires a plaintiff to allege reliance on a 
14   public advertisement. “The [FAL’s] prohibition extends to the use of false or misleading 
15   oral statements,” People v. Dollar Rent-A-Car Sys., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2148959"><a href="/opinion/2148959/people-v-dollar-rent-a-car-systems-inc/#128" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Dollar Rent-A-Car Systems, Inc.">211 Cal.App.3d 119, 128</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">16   App. 1989</span><pre class="inline">), which is what Ogdon has alleged here. What’s more, Ogdon alleges that these 
17   oral misrepresentations by counselors were part of a broader pattern of sales tactics to 
18   induce students to enroll. These statements plausibly fall under the purview of the FAL. 
19        In arguing otherwise, Defendants note that claims under the FAL are evaluated 
20   under a “reasonable consumer standard,” which asks whether “members of the public are 
21   likely to be deceived.” Williams v. Gerber Prods. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d 934, 938</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008) 
22   (internal quotations and citations omitted). Defendants extrapolate from this standard that 
23   the only false or misleading statements cognizable under the FAL are those disseminated 
24   to the public at large. But this conclusion doesn’t necessarily follow. To the contrary, “the 
25   reasonable consumer standard requires a probability ‘that a significant portion of the 
26   general  consuming  public  or  of  targeted  consumers,  acting  reasonably  in  the 
27   circumstances, could be misled.’” Ebner v. Fresh, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8414293"><a href="/opinion/8442970/ebner-v-fresh-inc/#965" aria-description="Citation for case: Ebner v. Fresh, Inc.">838 F.3d 958, 965</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2016) 
28   (emphasis added, and quoting Lavie v. Procter &amp; Gamble Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9749498"><a href="/opinion/2283365/lavie-v-procter-gamble-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lavie v. Procter &amp; Gamble Co.">105 Cal.App.4th 496</a></span><pre class="inline">, (Cal. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">1   App. 2003</span><pre class="inline">)). Here, Ogdon plausibly alleges that the targeted consumers—current and 
2   prospective students—were reasonably misled by Defendants’ statements concerning the 
3   accreditation of its professional degree programs. This is sufficient to survive dismissal.  
4        2.  CLRA                                                        
5        The CLRA prohibits “unfair methods of competition and unfair or deceptive acts or 
6   practices.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1770</span><pre class="inline">. Defendants argue Ogdon’s CLRA claim fails because 
7   she hasn’t alleged fraud with particularity, nor has she alleged actual or reasonable reliance 
8   on Defendants’ purported misrepresentations. For reasons already discussed in this order, 
9   the Court disagrees with both these arguments and therefore denies Defendants’ motion to 
10   dismiss Ogdon’s CLRA claim.                                          
11        3.  UCL                                                         
12        The UCL prohibits unfair competition, defined as “any lawful, unfair or fraudulent 
13   business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising.” Cal. Civ. 
14   Code § 17200.                                                        
15        Unlawful Practice. Under the “unlawful” prong, the UCL “borrows violations of 
16   other laws and treats them as unlawful practices that [the UCL] makes independently 
17   actionable.” Alvarez v. Chevron Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="613006"><a href="/opinion/613006/alvarez-v-chevron-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Alvarez v. Chevron Corp.">656 F.3d 925</a></span><pre class="inline">, 933 n.8 (9th Cir. 2011) (internal 
18   quotations and citation omitted). “If a plaintiff cannot state a claim under the predicate law, 
19   . . . [the UCL] claim also fails.” Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F.Supp.3d 1074</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1094 
20   (N.D.  Cal.  2017)  (citation  omitted).  Here,  Ogdon  alleges  Defendants  engaged  in  an 
21   unlawful business act by violating (1) the Higher Education Act, (2) the FAL and CLRA, 
22   and (3) unspecified federal and state laws and regulations.          
23        Defendants argue that Odgon cannot maintain a UCL claim predicated on violations 
24   of the HEA because that law lacks a private cause of action. See Parks School of Business, 
25   Inc. v. Symington, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="693410"><a href="/opinion/693410/parks-school-of-business-inc-dba-parks-college-a-new-mexico-corporation/#1484" aria-description="Citation for case: Parks School of Business, Inc., Dba Parks College, a New...">51 F.3d 1480, 1484-85</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1995) (explaining that the HEA does 
26   not confer a private cause of action). The Court disagrees. Although the HEA “lacks an 
27   express private cause of action, . . . private UCL claims are barred only when the underlying 
28   statute either actually bars private rights of action or provides a ‘safe harbor’ that renders 
1   the alleged conduct lawful. Walker v. Life Ins. Co. of the Sw., 681 Fed. App’x. 599, 601 
2   (9th Cir. 2017). Defendants have not cited to the Court any provision of the HEA explicitly 
3   barring private rights of action. The HEA simply lacks a provision expressly providing for 
4   one, and the Ninth Circuit has declined to read one into the statute by implication. Nor have 
5   Defendants identified any safe harbor provision in the HEA rendering their alleged conduct 
6   lawful. For these reasons, the Court is unpersuaded by Defendants’ argument that Odgon 
7   cannot maintain a UCL claim predicated on alleged violations of the HEA. 
8        Defendants also argue that Odgon cannot maintain a UCL claim predicated on 
9   violations of the FAL and CLRA because Ogdon fails to state plausible claims under those 
10   provisions. For reasons already discussed, the Court finds the FAC plausibly states FAL 
11   and CLRA claims and, therefore the Court rejects this portion of Defendants’ argument. 
12        The Court agrees with Defendants, however, that Ogdon has not adequately alleged 
13   a UCL claim predicated on violations of unspecified state and federal laws and regulations. 
14   Defendants are entitled to know what specific predicate laws are at issue.  See Ketab Corp. 
15   v. Mesriani &amp; Assocs., 2:14-cv-07241-RSWL (MRW), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2015 WL 8022874</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (C.D. Cal. 
16   Dec. 4, 2015) (dismissing UCL claims where the pleading failed to identify the specific 
17   predicate law alleged to have been violated). This catch-all aspect of Ogdon’s unlawful 
18   practices UCL claim is dismissed.                                    
19         Fraudulent Business Act. The UCL also prohibits fraudulent business practices. 
20   Defendants argue Ogdon hasn’t plausibly alleged a fraudulent business act UCL claim 
21   because she has not alleged fraud with particularity. Because the Court finds Ogdon has 
22   adequately alleged her fraud claim, it rejects this portion of Defendants’ argument. 
23        Unfair Practices. Finally, “[t]he ‘unfair’ prong of the UCL creates a cause of action 
24   for a business practice that is unfair even if not proscribed by some other law.” In re Adobe 
25   Sys., Inc. Privacy Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7228410"><a href="/opinion/7310506/in-re-adobe-systems-inc-privacy-litigation/#1226" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Adobe Systems, Inc. Privacy Litigation">66 F.Supp.3d 1197, 1226</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2014). California courts have 
26   not settled on a definition of “unfair” conduct, but two possible tests have emerged: (1) the 
27   so-called “tethering test,” under which requires the alleged unfair conduct to be tethered to 
28   a specific constitutional, statutory, or regulatory provision, and (2) the “balancing test,” 
1   under which the Court examines the challenges business practice and determines whether 
2   it  is  “immoral,  unethical,  oppressive,  unscrupulous  or  substantially  injurious  to 
3   consumers,” and “weigh[s] the utility of the defendant’s conduct against the gravity of the 
4   harm to the alleged victim.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7228410"><a href="/opinion/7310506/in-re-adobe-systems-inc-privacy-litigation/" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Adobe Systems, Inc. Privacy Litigation">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (internal quotations and citations omitted). 
5        The Court finds the FAC plausibly states a claim under either test. Ogdon has 
6   plausibly tethered the alleged unfair conduct to perceived violations of the HEA, FAL, and 
7   CLRA. And, as alleged, Defendants’ conduct—at bottom, lying to or misleading current 
8   and prospective students in order to induce them to enroll in degree programs that will 
9   leave  them  in  debt  but  with  little  prosect  of  gainful  employment  in  their  chosen 
10   professions—plausibly is immoral, unethical, unscrupulous, and substantially injurious to 
11   consumers, and the Court can discern no utility in such conduct that would so clearly 
12   outweigh such harms as to mandate dismissal of this claim. For these reasons, the Court 
13   finds Ogdon has plausibly alleged a UCL unfair conduct claim.        
14     D. Unjust Enrichment                                               
15        Defendants argue Ogdon can’t bring an unjust enrichment claim because the parties’ 
16   relationship is governed by a specific contract, namely her enrollment agreement with the 
17   University. The Court denies Defendants’ motion to dismiss Ogdon’s unjust enrichment 
18   claim because the alleged enrollment agreement is outside the pleadings. 
19     E. Class Action Standing                                           
20        Lastly, Defendants argue that Ogdon lacks class action standing to represent persons 
21   who enrolled in degree programs other than her own. The Court agrees with Ogdon that 
22   this argument is more appropriately raised and resolved at the class certification stage, 
23   where the Court may more concretely grapple with whether Ogdon is an adequate and 
24   typical  representative  of  the  class  she  seeks  to  certify.  The  Court  therefore  denies 
25   Defendants’ request to dismiss Ogdon’s class action allegations to the extent they pertain 
26   to degree programs other than her own, but without prejudice to Defendants’ reasserting 
27   these standing arguments at the class certification stage, if appropriate.
