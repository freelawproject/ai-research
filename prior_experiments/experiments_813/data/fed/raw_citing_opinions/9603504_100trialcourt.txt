17        In this putative class action, Plaintiffs allege that Defendant Google, LLC violates state 
18   consumer protection laws by distributing game applications (“apps”) containing “Loot Boxes” 
19   through its Play Store.  A Loot Box, which may be purchased in-app during play of some games, 
20   offers the player a randomized chance to receive an item designed to enhance the gaming 
21   experience, such as a better weapon, faster car, or more desirable player appearance.  The player 
22   does not know what specific item any given Loot Box contains at the time of purchase, so the 
23   player runs the risk of being dissatisfied.  Plaintiffs allege that Loot Boxes are illegal slot 
24   machines1 under California law, and that Google is civilly liable to all persons in the United States 
25   who purchased a Loot Box in an app downloaded from the Google Play Store.

3     III.  DISCUSSION                                                    
4        Google seeks dismissal of the FAC without leave to amend, arguing that Plaintiffs still 
5   have not alleged a viable claim despite the Court’s guidance in the Prior Dismissal Order.  First, 
6   Google asserts that Plaintiffs have not alleged any facts that would cause the Court to reconsider 
7   its conclusion that Google is immune from suit under Section 230 of the CDA.  Second, Google 
8   contends that Plaintiffs once again have failed to state a claim.  Third, Google argues that as a 
9   matter of law, Loot Boxes are not illegal slot machines under California law.   
10        In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Section 230 immunity does not apply, their claims are 
11   adequately alleged, and Loot Boxes are illegal slot machines.  Plaintiffs also argue that some 
12   claims do not depend on the illegality of Loot Boxes, and may proceed even if Loot Boxes are not 
13   illegal slot machines.  Google responds to the latter argument in its reply.   
14        The Court begins by addressing Google’s assertion of Section 230 immunity.  Next, the 
15   Court addresses whether Plaintiffs have stated a claim for relief.  The Court then takes up the 
16   question of whether Loot Boxes are illegal slot machines under California law.  Finally, the Court 
17   addresses Plaintiffs’ argument that some claims do not depend on the illegality of Loot Boxes. 
18        A.   Section 230 Immunity                                        
19        Section 230 of the CDA “protects certain internet-based actors from certain kinds of 
20   lawsuits.”  Barnes v. Yahoo!, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1196509"><a href="/opinion/1196509/barnes-v-yahoo-inc/#1099" aria-description="Citation for case: Barnes v. Yahoo!, Inc.">570 F.3d 1096, 1099</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2009).  As relevant here, Section 
21   230(c)(1) provides that “[n]o provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as 
22   the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider.”  
23   </pre><span class="citation no-link">47 U.S.C. § 230</span><pre class="inline">(c)(1).  “No cause of action may be brought and no liability may be imposed under 
24   any State or local law that is inconsistent with this section.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">47 U.S.C. § 203</span><pre class="inline">(e)(3).  “The majority 
25   of federal circuits have interpreted the CDA to establish broad federal immunity to any cause of 
26   action that would make service providers liable for information originating with a third-party user 
27   of the service.”  Perfect 10, Inc. v. CCBill LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="797970"><a href="/opinion/797970/perfect-10-inc-v-ccbill-llc/#1118" aria-description="Citation for case: Perfect 10, Inc. v. CCBill LLC">488 F.3d 1102, 1118</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2007) (quotation 
1        “This grant of immunity applies only if the interactive computer service provider is not 
2   also an ‘information content provider,’ which is defined as someone who is ‘responsible, in whole 
3   or in part, for the creation or development of’ the offending content.  Fair Hous. Council of San 
4   Fernando Valley v. Roommates.com, LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9567660"><a href="/opinion/1301306/fair-housing-coun-san-fernando-v-roommates-com/#1162" aria-description="Citation for case: Fair Housing Coun., San Fernando v. Roommates. Com">521 F.3d 1157, 1162</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008) (quoting § 
5   230(f)(3)).  A website does not become responsible for development of the offending content 
6   merely providing “neutral tools” that a third party utilizes to create illegal content.  Id. at 1169.  A 
7   website falls within the exception to Section 230 immunity only “if it contributes materially to the 
8   alleged illegality of the conduct.”  Id. at 1168.                     
9        In Barnes, the Ninth Circuit created a three-prong test for Section 230 immunity.  See 
10   Dyroff v. Ultimate Software Grp., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4427022"><a href="/opinion/4649769/kristanalea-dyroff-v-the-ultimate-software-group/#1097" aria-description="Citation for case: Kristanalea Dyroff v. the Ultimate Software Group">934 F.3d 1093, 1097</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2019) (discussing Barnes 
11   test).  “Immunity from liability exists for ‘(1) a provider or user of an interactive computer service 
12   (2) whom a plaintiff seeks to treat, under a state law cause of action, as a publisher or speaker (3) 
13   of information provided by another information content provider.’”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4427022"><a href="/opinion/4649769/kristanalea-dyroff-v-the-ultimate-software-group/" aria-description="Citation for case: Kristanalea Dyroff v. the Ultimate Software Group">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Barnes, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="203884"><a href="/opinion/203884/united-states-v-croto/#1100" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Croto">570 F.3d

15   plaintiff’s claims should be dismissed.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="203884"><a href="/opinion/203884/united-states-v-croto/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Croto">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">

23   most common interactive computer services.”  Dyroff, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4427022"><a href="/opinion/4649769/kristanalea-dyroff-v-the-ultimate-software-group/#1097" aria-description="Citation for case: Kristanalea Dyroff v. the Ultimate Software Group">934 F.3d at 1097</a></span><pre class="inline">; see also Roommates, 521

7        Plaintiffs allege that Loot Box prizes have subjective value to gamers that may not be

9   holding that a player’s subjective enjoyment in a virtual game item renders that virtual game item

11   contrary, the Court finds Plaintiffs’ theory to be untenable.         
12        Plaintiffs allege that Loot Box prizes have monetary value because they can be traded in 
13   legitimate digital markets or in “gray markets.”  FAC ¶¶ 156-59.  However, Plaintiffs do not allege 
14   that either of the two games they downloaded from the Play Store – Final Fantasy and Dragon Ball 
15   Z – allow transfer or sale of Loot Box items.  Moreover, the Court takes judicial notice of the 
16   Google Play Terms of Service prohibiting the sale or transfer of any in-app content, including 
17   Loot Box items.  The Ninth Circuit has held that a virtual item cannot constitute a thing of value 
18   where its sale would violate applicable terms of use.  See Kater v. Churchill Downs Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8598801"><a href="/opinion/8619551/united-states-v-rondon-garcia/#784" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Rondón-García">886 F.3d

20   the Terms of Use.  The virtual chips cannot constitute a ‘thing of value’ based on this prohibited 
21   use.”).  Plaintiffs attempt to distinguish Kater on the basis that it addressed a Washington statute 
22   defining “thing of value” differently that the California statutes at issue here.  However, this Court 
23   concludes that differences in the underlying criminal statutes do not preclude application of 
24   Kater’s holding that a virtual item cannot be considered a thing of value based on its alleged 
25   market value where the terms of use preclude transfer of the item.

1   conclusion, the Court need not and does not address Google’s argument that Loot Boxes are not

4             2.   Plaintiffs’ Theory of Liability Based on Immorality of Loot Boxes 
5        Plaintiffs contend that they may proceed on their UCL unfair prong claim (Claim 2) and 
6   their unjust enrichment claim (Claim 4) whether or not Loot Boxes are illegal slot machines.  They

9   Google promoted “unlawful and unfair” Loot Boxes.  FAC ¶ 226.         
10        As Google points out, “courts in this district have held that where the unfair business 
11   practices alleged under the unfair prong of the UCL overlap entirely with the business practices

13   survive if the claims under the other two prongs of the UCL do not survive.”  Hadley v. Kellogg

15        The motion to dismiss all claims is GRANTED based on the Court’s determinations that
