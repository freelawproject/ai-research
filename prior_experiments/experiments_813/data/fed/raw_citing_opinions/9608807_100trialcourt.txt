14        Pending before the Court is Defendant Takara Sake USA Inc’s (“Takara”) Motion to 
15   Dismiss pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure (“Rule”) 12(b)(6).  ECF No. 20.  Plaintiff 
16   Tunick filed an Opposition (ECF No. 25) and Defendant filed a Reply (ECF No. 26).  The Court 
17   finds this matter suitable for disposition without oral argument and VACATES the June 15, 2023 
18   hearing.  See Civ. L.R. 7-1(b).  For the reasons stated below, the Court GRANTS IN PART AND 
19   DENIES IN PART the motion.1                                           
20                            II.  BACKGROUND                              
21        Plaintiff is a citizen of California and currently resides in San Diego, California.  ECF No. 
22   18 ¶ 15.  Defendant Takara is a corporation with its principal place of business in Berkeley, 
23   California.  Id. ¶ 17.  Takara is responsible for the manufacturing, labeling, advertising, 
24   distribution, and sale of the “Sho Chiku Bai” branded sakes (“Products”).  Id. ¶¶ 1, 17. 
25        Sake is a traditional Japanese alcohol made from fermented rice and is Japan’s national 
26   beverage.  Id. ¶ 26.  The Japanese government banned home brewing of sake without a license.  
27                                                                         
1   Id. ¶ 28.  Almost all sake products in the United States are imported from Japan.  Id. ¶ 33.  In 
2   2018, a research study indicated that nearly 80% of consumers stated that they only purchase sake 
3   produced in Japan.  Id. ¶ 35.                                         
4        In or around August 2020, Plaintiff purchased the 375 ml bottle of Sho Chiku Bai Nigori 
5   Unfiltered Sake Product in San Diego.  Id. ¶ 15.  Tunick believed this Product was made in Japan 
6   based on the “Sho Chiku Bai” brand name, Japanese lettering displayed on the front label of the 
7   Nigori Unfiltered Sake Product, as well as a gold emblem stating “Licensed by TaKaRa Japan, 
8   Since 1851” on the front label.  Id.  Had Tunick known the Product was not made in Japan, he 
9   would not have purchased it, or would have paid significantly less for it.  Id.  
10        Tunick alleges that (1) Sho Chiku Bai Nigori Unfiltered Sake, (2) Sho Chiku Bai Classic 
11   Junmai; and (3) Sho Chiku Bai Tokubetsu Junmai are all deceptively labeled and marketed as 
12   made in Japan, when they are actually produced in California.  Id. ¶¶ 36-37.  Tunick alleges that 
13   the Products’ labels deceptively suggest they are made in Japan based on: 1) large, bold Japanese 
14   lettering throughout the Products’ front labels; 2) a gold emblem which states “Licensed by 
15   TaKaRa Japan, Since 1851”; and the brand name “Sho Chiku Bai,” which translates to “the Three 
16   Friends of Winter.”  Id. ¶ 38.                                        
17        Tunick also alleges that Takara manufactures and sells products actually made in Japan 
18   with substantially similar labeling, which adds to the deception.  Id. ¶ 43.  Further, Tunick alleges 
19   that manufacturers negotiate with stores in an attempt to select prime shelf space for their 
20   products, and that Takara’s products are placed right alongside products made in Japan.  Id. ¶¶ 50, 
21   52.                                                                   
22        On February 8, 2023, Plaintiff filed a Complaint.  ECF No. 1.  On April 12, 2023, Takara

4                          III.  LEGAL STANDARD                           
5        A motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) “tests the legal 
6   sufficiency of a claim.  A claim may be dismissed only if it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff 
7   can prove no set of facts in support of his claim which would entitle him to relief.”  Cook v. 
8   Brewer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="212527"><a href="/opinion/212527/cook-v-brewer/#1004" aria-description="Citation for case: Cook v. Brewer">637 F.3d 1002, 1004</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (citation and quotation marks omitted).  Rule 8 
9   provides that a complaint must contain a “short and plain statement of the claim showing that the 
10   pleader is entitled to relief.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2).  Thus, a complaint must plead “enough facts 
11   to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.”  Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
12   570 (2007).  Plausibility does not mean probability, but it requires “more than a sheer possibility 
13   that a defendant has acted unlawfully.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#687" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 687</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009).  A complaint 
14   must therefore provide a defendant with “fair notice” of the claims against it and the grounds for 
15   relief.  Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 555</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quotations and citation omitted).   
16        In considering a motion to dismiss, the court accepts factual allegations in the complaint as 
17   true and construes the pleadings in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.  Manzarek v. 
18   St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1194077"><a href="/opinion/1194077/manzarek-v-st-paul-fire-marine-insurance/#1031" aria-description="Citation for case: Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Insurance">519 F.3d 1025, 1031</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008); Erickson v. Pardus, 551 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="85310"><a href="/opinion/85310/the-amiable-isabella/" aria-description="Citation for case: The Amiable Isabella">19   U.S. 89</a></span><pre class="inline">, 93–94 (2007).  However, “the tenet that a court must accept a complaint’s allegations as 
20   true is inapplicable to threadbare recitals of a cause of action’s elements, supported by mere 
21   conclusory statements.”  Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. at 678</a></span><pre class="inline">.

23   request to amend the pleading was made, unless it determines that the pleading could not possibly

25   banc) (citations and quotations omitted).  However, a court “may exercise its discretion to deny 
26   leave to amend due to ‘undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive on part of the movant, repeated 
27   failure to cure deficiencies by amendments previously allowed, undue prejudice to the opposing

11        Normally, when adjudicating a motion to dismiss brought pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6), the 
12   Court’s consideration of extra-pleading materials is limited and matters outside of the pleading 
13   cannot be considered without converting the motion into a motion for summary judgment.  See 
14   Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6); 12(d).  There are two exceptions, however, the incorporation-by-
15   reference doctrine and judicial notice under Federal Rule of Evidence 201.  Khoja v. Orexigen 
16   Therapeutics, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#998" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d 988, 998</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018).  Under Federal Rule of Evidence 201, the 
17   Court may take judicial notice of matters that are (1) generally known within the trial court’s 
18   territorial jurisdiction or (2) capable of accurate and ready determination by resort to sources 
19   whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned.  Fed. R. Evid. 201(b).  Under the doctrine of 
20   incorporation-by-reference, the Court may consider a document not attached to the complaint 
21   provided the complaint “necessarily relies” on the document or contents thereof, the document’s 
22   authenticity is uncontested, and the document’s relevance is uncontested.  Coto Settlement v. 
23   Eisenberg, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1405974"><a href="/opinion/1405974/coto-settlement-v-eisenberg/#1038" aria-description="Citation for case: Coto Settlement v. 25   reference into a complaint if the plaintiff refers extensively to the document or the document 
26   forms the basis of the plaintiff’s claim.”).  “The defendant may offer such a document, and the 
27   district court may treat such a document as part of the complaint, and thus may assume that its

8   (last visited June 11, 2023).  The Court does not consider such a definition to be essentially

10   Merriam Webster, https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/produce (last visited June 11,

13   produce it, is more limited in Japan.  See ECF No. 18 ¶ 28 (noting that the Japanese government

4        Takara argues that Tunick’s express warranty claim fails because “Licensed by TaKaRa

6   ECF No. 20 at 14-15.                                                  
7        Under California law, “a plaintiff asserting a breach of warranty claim must allege facts 
8   sufficient to show that: (1) the seller’s statements constitute an affirmation of fact or promise or a 
9   description of the goods; (2) the statement was part of the basis of the bargain; and (3) the 
10   warranty was breached.”  Mattero v. Costco Wholesale Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7250278"><a href="/opinion/7332360/mattero-v-costco-wholesale-corp/#1115" aria-description="Citation for case: Mattero v. Costco Wholesale Corp.">336 F. Supp. 3d 1109, 1115</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">11   Cal. 2018</span><pre class="inline">) (citing Weinstat v. Dentsply Internat., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2255890"><a href="/opinion/2255890/weinstat-v-dentsply-international-inc/#1227" aria-description="Citation for case: Weinstat v. Dentsply International, Inc.">180 Cal. App. 4th 1213, 1227</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2010)).  
12   Statements on a product’s label are part of the bargain between buyer and seller, Weinstat, 180 
13   Cal. App. 4th at 1227, and create express warranties, Mattero, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7250278"><a href="/opinion/7332360/mattero-v-costco-wholesale-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Mattero v. Costco Wholesale Corp.">336 F. Supp. 3d at 1115</a></span><pre class="inline">-16 (citing 
14   several cases).  Courts look to the context of the statement to determine whether an express 
15   warranty has been made, including the label.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7250278"><a href="/opinion/7332360/mattero-v-costco-wholesale-corp/#1116" aria-description="Citation for case: Mattero v. Costco Wholesale Corp.">Id. at 1116</a></span><pre class="inline"> (holding the “environmentally 
16   responsible” claim on defendant’s dish soap and laundry detergent product labels constitutes an 
17   affirmative fact or promise that the products do not have “toxic and hazardous ingredients”); Jones 
18   v. Nutiva, Inc., No. 16-CV-00711-HSG, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 WL 5210935</span><pre class="inline">, at *9 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 22, 2016) 
19   (finding plaintiff adequately alleged that a virgin coconut oil label, in its full context, constitutes 
20   an affirmative fact or promise).  “The determination as to whether a particular statement is an 
21   expression of opinion or an affirmation of a fact is often difficult, and frequently is dependent 
22   upon the facts and circumstances existing at the time the statement is made.”  Keith v. Buchanan, 
23   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2108129"><a href="/opinion/2108129/keith-v-buchanan/#21" aria-description="Citation for case: Keith v. Buchanan">173 Cal. App. 3d 13, 21</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. Ct. App. 1985).  As such, “courts liberally construe sellers’ 
24   affirmations of quality in favor of injured consumers.”  Nutivia, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 WL 5210935</span><pre class="inline">, at *9 
25   (simplified).                                                         
26        “[C]ourts in this district regularly hold that stating a claim under California consumer 
27   protection statutes is sufficient to state a claim for express warranty.”  Hadley, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7245130"><a href="/opinion/7327214/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">273 F. Supp. 3d at 
1</a></span><pre class="inline">   (N.D. Cal. Nov. 3, 2015) (because plaintiffs satisfied the reasonable consumer standard with 
2   respect to their California consumer protection claims, the same “allegations [we]re sufficient to 
3   state a claim for breach of express warranty”)).  See also Ham v. Hain Celestial Grp., Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">70 F. 
4</span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d 1188, 1195 (N.D. Cal. 2014) (denying motion to dismiss breach of express warranty 
5   claim “for the same reasons as the consumer protection and misrepresentation-based claims.”).
