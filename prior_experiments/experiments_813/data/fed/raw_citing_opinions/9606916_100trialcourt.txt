7    II.  FAILURE TO STATE A CLAIM – RULE 12(B)(6)                        
8        “A motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a 
9   claim upon which relief can be granted ‘tests the legal sufficiency of a claim.’”  Conservation 
10   Force v. Salazar, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="221641"><a href="/opinion/221641/conservation-force-v-salazar/#1241" aria-description="Citation for case: Conservation Force v. Salazar">646 F.3d 1240, 1241-42</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (quoting Navarro v. Block, </pre><span class="citation no-link">250 F.3d 
11</span><pre class="inline">   729, 732 (9th Cir. 2001)).  When determining whether a claim has been stated, the Court accepts 
12   as true all well-pled factual allegations and construes them in the light most favorable to the 
13   plaintiff.  Reese v. BP Expl. (Alaska) Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="219976"><a href="/opinion/219976/reese-v-bp-exploration-alaska-inc/#690" aria-description="Citation for case: Reese v. BP Exploration (Alaska) Inc.">643 F.3d 681, 690</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011).  But the Court need 
14   not “accept as true allegations that contradict matters properly subject to judicial notice” or 
15   “allegations that are merely conclusory, unwarranted deductions of fact, or unreasonable 
16   inferences.”  In re Gilead Scis. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1400355"><a href="/opinion/1400355/in-re-gilead-sciences-securities-litigation/#1055" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Gilead Sciences Securities Litigation">536 F.3d 1049, 1055</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008) (internal quotation 
17   marks and citations omitted).  While a complaint need not contain detailed factual allegations, it 
18   “must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible 
19   on its face.’”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 
20   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007)).  A claim is facially plausible when it “allows the court to draw the 
21   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  On a motion to 
22   dismiss, the Court’s review is limited to the face of the complaint and matters judicially 
23   noticeable.  MGIC Indem. Corp. v. Weisman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="478039"><a href="/opinion/478039/mgic-indemnity-corporation-v-lawrence-i-weisman-thomas-p-dunn-ln/#504" aria-description="Citation for case: Mgic Indemnity Corporation v. Lawrence I. Weisman, Thomas...">803 F.2d 500, 504</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1986); N. Star Int’l v. 
24   Ariz. Corp. Comm’n, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="426434"><a href="/opinion/426434/north-star-international-a-nevada-corporation-plaintiff-appellant-v-the/#581" aria-description="Citation for case: NORTH STAR INTERNATIONAL, a Nevada Corporation,...">720 F.2d 578, 581</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1983).                
25      A.  Fraud                                                          
26            1.  Rule 9(b)                                                
27        When a party pleads a cause of action for fraud or mistake, it is subject to the heightened 
1   particularity the circumstances constituting fraud or mistake.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b) (emphasis 
2   added).  “Malice, intent, knowledge, and other conditions of a person’s mind may be alleged 
3   generally.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="426434"><a href="/opinion/426434/north-star-international-a-nevada-corporation-plaintiff-appellant-v-the/" aria-description="Citation for case: NORTH STAR INTERNATIONAL, a Nevada Corporation,...">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Rule 9(b) requires that the circumstances constituting any alleged fraud be pled 
4   “specific[ally] enough to give defendants notice of the particular misconduct . . . so that they can 
5   defend against the charge and not just deny that they have done anything wrong.”  Kearns v. Ford 
6   Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208595"><a href="/opinion/1208595/kearns-v-ford-motor-co/#1124" aria-description="Citation for case: Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.">567 F.3d 1120, 1124</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2009) (quoting Bly-Magree v. California, </pre><span class="citation no-link">236 F.3d 
7</span><pre class="inline">   1014, 1019 (9th Cir. 2001)).  Claims of fraud must be accompanied by the “who, what, when, 
8   where, and how” of the misconduct alleged.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  If a “claim is said to be ‘grounded in fraud’ or to 
9   ‘sound to fraud,’ [then] the pleading of that claim as a whole must satisfy that particularity 
10   requirement of Rule 9(b).”  Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1103" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d 1097, 1103-04</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
11   2003).                                                                
12        The applicability of Rule 9(b) hinges not on the elements of the claim but rather on the 
13   nature of the allegations themselves: “Rule 9(b) applies to ‘averments of fraud’ in all civil cases in 
14   federal district court,” including “particular averments of fraud” even when fraud is not an 
15   essential element of the claim.  Vess, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1103" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d at 1103</a></span><pre class="inline">; see also Kearns, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208595"><a href="/opinion/1208595/kearns-v-ford-motor-co/#1124" aria-description="Citation for case: Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.">567 F.3d at 1124</a></span><pre class="inline"> (“Where 
16   fraud is not an essential element of a claim, only those allegations of a complaint which aver fraud 
17   are subject to Rule 9(b)&#x27;s heightened pleading standard.”).  Fraud can thus be averred “by 
18   specifically alleging fraud, or by alleging facts that necessarily constitute fraud (even if the word 
19   ‘fraud’ is not used).”  Vess, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1105" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d at 1105</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citations omitted).   
20            2.  UCL fraud prong, CLRA, FAL, and common law fraud by omission 
21        Plaintiff brings four fraud-based claims: (1) violation of the fraud prong of the UCL, 
22   Compl. ¶¶ 180-190 (Count 5); (2) violation of the FAL, id. ¶¶ 191-205 (Count 6); (3) violation of 
23   the CLRA, id. ¶¶ 222-236 (Count 8); and (4) common law fraud by omission, id. ¶¶ 206-221 
24   (Count 7).                                                            
25        “Broadly stated: The UCL prohibits ‘any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or 
26   practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising and any act prohibited by the 
27   FAL’ ([Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code] § 17200); the FAL prohibits advertising ‘which is untrue or 
1   be untrue or misleading’ ([id. at] § 17500); and the CLRA prohibits specified ‘unfair methods of 
2   competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices’ ([Cal.] Civ. Code § 1770, subd. (a)).”  Hill v. 
3   Roll Int’l Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5665723"><a href="/opinion/5810290/hill-v-roll-international-corp/#1301" aria-description="Citation for case: Hill v. Roll International Corp.">195 Cal. App. 4th 1295, 1301</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011) (alterations omitted).  All three statutes 
4   prohibit fraudulent misrepresentations and omissions.  See In re Seagate Tech. LLC Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8799598"><a href="/opinion/8815122/hendrey-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hendrey v. United States">233 F. 
5</a></span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d 776, 788 (N.D. Cal. 2017).  Further, courts in this district have consistently held that 
6   “plaintiffs in misrepresentation cases must allege that they actually read the challenged 
7   representations” to state a claim.  In re Yahoo! Inc. Customer Data Sec. Breach Litig., No. 16-
8   MD-02752-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 3727318</span><pre class="inline">, at *27-28 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 30, 2017) (citation omitted) 
9   (dismissing UCL fraud claim for failure to plead actual reliance); see also Bruton v. Gerber Prods. 
10   Co., No. 12-CV-02412-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 172111</span><pre class="inline">, at *6, *9 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 15, 2014) (citing In re 
11   Tobacco II Cases, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9685293"><a href="/opinion/1801796/in-re-tobacco-ii-cases/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Tobacco II Cases">46 Cal. 4th 298</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009)) (dismissing UCL, FAL, and CLRA claims for lack of 
12   statutory standing based on plaintiff’s failure to allege he viewed alleged misrepresentations).    
13        “Under California law, a claim of fraud by omission requires a showing of (1) the 
14   concealment or suppression of material fact, (2) a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) 
15   intentional concealment with intent to defraud, (4) justifiable reliance, and (5) resulting damages.”  
16   Edwards v. FCA US LLC, No. 22-cv-01871-WHO, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 1814144</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. June 2, 
17   2022) (quoting Lewis v. Google LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">851 F. App’x 723</span><pre class="inline">, 725 (9th Cir. 2021)). 
18              a.  Misrepresentation                                      
19        HP argues that the fraud-based claims should be dismissed because So does not plead an 
20   affirmative consumer-facing misrepresentation with particularity as required by Rule 9(b), and he 
21   does not plead reliance on any alleged misrepresentations.  MTD at 4-7.  The Court agrees.  As 
22   discussed at the hearing, So does not allege an affirmative misrepresentation made to consumers.  
23   The alleged misrepresentations identified in the Complaint, in which HP indicated printer owners 
24   could use HP cartridges or third-party cartridges, were in investor materials and a strategic plan.  
25   See Compl. ¶¶ 44, 46.  And So does not plead reliance on an affirmative misrepresentation, as he 
26   does not allege that he saw any of the alleged misrepresentations.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">id.</span><pre class="inline">  He alleges that he 
27   “rightfully believed” he could use a third-party cartridge based on his experience with another HP 
1   agree that So cannot allege reliance on the printer error messages, see Compl. ¶¶ 68, 72, as they 
2   did not appear until after purchase.  MTD at 10-11; Opp. at 12-13. The Court agrees that because 
3   the error messages appeared after So purchased the printer, there is no reliance. 
4        Therefore, the motion to dismiss any fraud-based claims based on an affirmative 
5   misrepresentation is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.                     
6              b.  Omission                                               
7        HP further argues that any fraud claims based on omission also fail because So does not 
8   allege HP concealed a defect central to the printers’ function.  MTD at 8-10.  To allege fraud by 
9   omission, the omission must either (1) “be contrary to a representation actually made by the 
10   defendant,” or (2) “an omission of a fact the defendant was obliged to disclose.”  Anderson v. 
11   Apple Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">500 F. Supp. 3d 993</span><pre class="inline">, 1012 (N.D. Cal. 2020) (quoting Hodsdon v. Mars, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4277741"><a href="/opinion/4500488/santos-guaman-v-sessions/#857" aria-description="Citation for case: Santos Guaman v. Sessions">891 F.3d 
12   857, 861</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018)).  Here, the first prong is not alleged, so Plaintiff needs to show that HP 
13   had a duty to disclose prior to sale that it would send out firmware that would make its printers 
14   incompatible with third-party cartridges.  The law about a defendant’s duty to disclose in 
15   fraudulent omission cases is “marked by general disarray,” largely because there are at least two 
16   tests to determine whether a defendant has a duty to disclose.  Hammerling v. Google LLC, No. 
17   21-cv-09004-CRB, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 2812188</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (N.D. Cal. July 18, 2022) (quoting In re Toyota 
18   RAV4 Hybrid Fuel Tank Litig., </pre><span class="citation no-link">534 F. Supp. 3d 1067</span><pre class="inline">, 1101-02 (N.D. Cal. 2021)).  The Court 
19   agrees with the recent Hammerling decision as to the appropriate test.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 2812188</span><pre class="inline">, at 
20   *8 (collecting Ninth Circuit and Northern District of California cases adopting this test).  So must 
21   allege that HP’s omission “either (1) relates to an unreasonable safety hazard or (2) is material, 
22   ‘central to the product’s function,’ and meets one of the four LiMandri factors.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> (quoting 
23   Hodson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4277741"><a href="/opinion/4500488/santos-guaman-v-sessions/#863" aria-description="Citation for case: Santos Guaman v. Sessions">891 F.3d at 863</a></span><pre class="inline">).  “The LiMandri factors are (1) the defendant is in a fiduciary 
24   relationship with the plaintiff; (2) the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not 
25   known to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; or (4) 
26   the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4277741"><a href="/opinion/4500488/santos-guaman-v-sessions/" aria-description="Citation for case: Santos Guaman v. Sessions">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at *7 
27   (citing LiMandri v. Judkins, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2267506"><a href="/opinion/2267506/limandri-v-judkins/#336" aria-description="Citation for case: LiMandri v. Judkins">52 Cal. App. 4th 326, 336</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1997)).       
1   inability to use a third-party cartridge is “material, central to the product’s function, and meets one 
2   of the four LiMandri factors.”  Plaintiff must also allege facts showing that HP intended to send 
3   the firmware updates at the time of purchase.  Plaintiff has not done so.  See Compl.  Therefore, 
4   the motion to dismiss any fraud-based claims based on omission is GRANTED WITH LEAVE 
5   TO AMEND.

7        HP’s motion to dismiss the fraud-based claims brought under the fraud prong of the UCL, 
8   FAL, and CLRA, as well as the common law, is thus GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND. 
9            3.  California CCDAFA Fraud Claim                            
10        So brings a claim under the California Comprehensive Computer Data Access and Fraud 
11   Act.  Compl. ¶¶ 144-159; see </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Penal Code § 502</span><pre class="inline">.  He asserts claims under subsections (1)-(5) 
12   of </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Penal Code § 502</span><pre class="inline">(c).  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">id. ¶ 152</span><pre class="inline">.  HP argues that So’s claim under subsection (c)(1) 
13   should be dismissed to the extent it is based on fraud.  MTD at 14-15.  Subsection (c)(1) imposes 
14   liability on any person who “[k]nowingly accesses and without permission alters, damages, 
15   deletes, destroys, or otherwise uses any data, computer, computer system, or computer network in 
16   order to either (A) devise or execute any scheme or artifice to defraud, deceive, or extort, or (B) 
17   wrongfully control or obtain money, property, or data.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Penal Code § 502</span><pre class="inline">(c)(1).  So argues 
18   that HP violated subsection (c)(1) by causing the Class Printers to display the false error messages, 
19   as these constituted “a scheme to defraud, deceive, and extort Plaintiff and Class Members to 
20   purchase new HP Original cartridges from HP.”  Compl. ¶ 152(a).       
21        HP argues that the claim should fail because So “fails to plead the underlying fraudulent 
22   conduct with requisite particularity” required by Rule 9(b).  MTD at 14-15.  Because the claim 
23   sounds in fraud, Rule 9(b) applies.  And the Court agrees that, for the reasons discussed above, 
24   Plaintiff has not pled fraud with particularity.                      
25        The motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s claims under Cal. Penal Code § (c)(1 is GRANTED 
26   WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

11              a.  UCL unfair claim                                       
12        So brings a claim under the unfair prong of the UCL.  Compl. ¶¶ 169-179 (Count 4).  HP 
13   asserts that the unfair claim is based on the same “unified course of fraudulent conduct” 
14   underlying the other fraud claims, and it should therefore be dismissed on the same basis as those 
15   claims.  MTD at 11.  HP further argues that if So’s unfair claim is grounded on anything other 
16   than fraud, namely on the basis that HP requires its printers to be used with compatible cartridges, 
17   it must be dismissed because other technology companies sell products that are only compatible 
18   with certain other of their products.  Id. at 11-12.  Plaintiff counters that he has sufficiently 
19   pleaded a unified course of fraudulent conduct.  Opp. at 13-14.  He identifies several “unfair 
20   business practices and acts” that he alleged in the Complaint, including, among others, HP’s sale 
21   and promotion of the Class Printers with the knowledge that they could lose functionality; false 
22   advertising; firmware update and resulting loss in functionality; and collection of usage data and 
23   customer information.  Id. at 14 (citing Compl. ¶ 173).  Finally, Plaintiff tries to distinguish the 
24   practices of other technology companies on the basis that those companies make clear to 
25   consumers at the time of purchase that their products are only compatible with certain other of 
26   their products.  Id.                                                  
27        To the extent So’s allegations and arguments rely on a “unified course of fraudulent 
1   Rule 9(b).  See Vess, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1103" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. 4   2014) (applying Rule 9(b) to unfair UCL claim based on “unified course of fraudulent conduct”).  
5   As discussed above, the Court finds Plaintiff has not met this standard.  Cf. Eidmann v. Walgreen 
6   Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">522 F. Supp. 3d 634</span><pre class="inline">, 647 (N.D. Cal. 2021) (“In this District, when plaintiff’s claim under the 
7   unfair prong overlaps entirely with the conduct alleged in the fraudulent and unlawful prongs of 
8   the UCL, ‘the unfair prong of the UCL cannot survive if the claims under the other two prongs . . . 
9   do not survive.’” (quoting Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1105" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1105</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 
10   2017))).  The Court therefore dismisses any allegations under the unfair prong that stem from “a 
11   unified course of fraudulent conduct” by HP.                          
12        The motion to dismiss the unfair claim under the UCL as to allegations that sound in fraud 
13   is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.                                       
14              b.  UCL unlawful claim                                     
15        Plaintiff also brings a claim under the unlawful prong of the UCL.  Compl. ¶¶ 160-168 
16   (Count 3).  HP does not seek to dismiss this claim.  See MTD.         
17      B.  CLRA notice requirement                                        
18        HP also seeks to dismiss the CLRA claim for lack of notice.  MTD at 12.  The CLRA 
19   requires a plaintiff to provide notice to a defendant 30 days or more prior to the commencement of 
20   an action for damages.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1782</span><pre class="inline">(a).  There is no such notice requirement for 
21   injunctive relief claims under the CLRA.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> § 1782(d).  Further, the CLRA provides that not less 
22   than 30 days after commencing an action for injunctive relief and providing notice, a consumer 
23   may amend their complaint to include a request for damages.  Id.  Here, Plaintiff provided notice 
24   to HP on March 28, 2022, and he filed his Complaint just 17 days later, seeking both injunctive 
25   relief and damages under the CLRA.  See Compl. ¶¶ 234, 236, Ex. B.    
26        HP argues that the Court should dismiss the CLRA claims for damages on this basis 
27   without leave to amend.  MTD at 12.  Plaintiff admits that the original request for damages under 
1   he provided notice, he asks for leave to amend his CLRA claim to seek damages.  Opp. at 15.  The 
2   Court agrees that the original claim for damages under the CLRA did not comply with notice 
3   requirements, and therefore it should be dismissed.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1782</span><pre class="inline">(a).  But because 
4   the statute specifically allows for amendment, the Court will grant leave to amend.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">id.</span><pre class="inline"> § 
5   1782(d); see also, e.g., Deitz v. Comcast Corp., No. C 06-06352 WHA, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2006 WL 3782902</span><pre class="inline">, at *5-
6   6 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 21, 2006).                                          
7        The motion to dismiss the CLRA for lack of notice is GRANTED WTH LEAVE TO 
8   AMEND.                                                                
9      C.  CFAA                                                           
10        So brings a claim under the CFAA, citing three subsections: </pre><span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. §§ 1030</span><pre class="inline">(a)(5)(A), 
11   1030(a)(2)(C), and 1030(a)(4).  Compl. ¶¶ 118-143.  HP argues that the CFAA claims should be 
12   dismissed because So does not allege the required statutory damages of $5,000.  MTD at 13.  The 
13   CFAA requires So to allege $5,000 in damages.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 1030</span><pre class="inline">(g).  He did not do so.  See 
14   Compl.  Under the CFAA, a class of plaintiffs may aggregate damages “so long as those damages 
15   arose from the same act by a defendant.”  In re Apple &amp; AT &amp; TM Antitrust Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2515927"><a href="/opinion/2515927/davis-v-filip/#1288" aria-description="Citation for case: Davis v. Filip">596 F. Supp. 2d 
16   1288, 1308</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2008); see also In re Toys R Us, Inc. Privacy Litig., No. 00-CV-2746, 2001 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">17   WL 34517252</span><pre class="inline">, at *11 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 9, 2001).  So may plead aggregated damages as long as they 
18   stem from a single act by HP.  The motion to dismiss the CFAA claim for failure to plead 
19   statutory damages is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.                     
20        HP further argues that the claim under subsection (a)(4) should be dismissed because it 
21   sounds in fraud, and the fraud is inadequately pled under Rule 9(b).  MTD at 14.  The statue 
22   forbids “knowingly and with intent to defraud, access[ing] a protected computer without 
23   authorization, or exceed[ing] authorized access, and by means of such conduct further[ing] the 
24   intended fraud and obtain[ing] anything of value . . . .”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 1030</span><pre class="inline">(a)(4).  Because the 
25   claim sounds in fraud, Rule 9(b) applies.  And the Court agrees that Plaintiff has not pled fraud 
26   with particularity.  The motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s claim under § 1030(a)(4) is GRANTED 
27   WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

2

Defenders of Wildlife">504 U.S. 555</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
3

4

5                                                                         
    judicial decision.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citing Lujan, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/#560" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. 6

</pre><span class="citation no-link">7                                                                         
    Dallas, 493</span><pre class="inline"> U.S. 215, 231 (1990)).  HP makes three arguments as to standing. 
8                                                                         
       A.  Standing for Out-of-State Class Members                        
9                                                                         
         HP argues that So does not have standing to assert consumer protection and fraud claims 
10                                                                         
    on behalf of out of state class members.  MTD at 15-22.  Plaintiff asserts his UCL and FAL claims 
11                                                                         
    on behalf of the “device owner class,” Compl. ¶¶ 160-205, which is a nationwide class, </pre><span class="citation no-link">id. ¶ 106</span><pre class="inline">.  
12                                                                         
    He asserts his CLRA claim on behalf of the “state consumer subclass,” </pre><span class="citation no-link">id. ¶¶ 222-236</span><pre class="inline">, which 
13                                                                         
    includes “[a]ll persons and entities residing in California and States with a similar consumer 
14                                                                         
    protection statute to [the CLRA],” </pre><span class="citation no-link">id. ¶ 106</span><pre class="inline">.  Finally, Plaintiff brings his fraud by omission claim 
15                                                                         
    “on behalf of the Device Owner Class under California law or, alternatively, the State Consumer 
16                                                                         
    Subclass(es) under the law of the state in which each respective Plaintiff purchased a Class 
17                                                                         
    Printer.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶ 207</span><pre class="inline">.                                                 
18                                                                         
         HP first asserts that So does not have standing to assert any consumer protection or fraud 
19                                                                         
    claims under the laws of any state other than California.  MTD at 16.  HP also argues that 
20                                                                         
    California law cannot be applied to out-of-state class members.  MTD at 17-22.  So argues that he 
21                                                                         
    has standing and that California law can be applied to out-of-state class members.  Opp. at 17-20. 
22                                                                         
         HP’s first argument, that So does not have standing to assert consumer protection or fraud 
23                                                                         
    claims under the laws of any state other than California, applies only to So’s fraud by omission 
24                                                                         
    claim, as that is the only claim in which So seeks to apply non-California law.  See Compl. ¶ 207 
25                                                                         
    (seeking to bring claim “on behalf of the Device Owner Class under California law or, 
26                                                                         
    alternatively, the State Consumer Subclass(es) under the law of the state in which each respective 
27                                                                         
    Plaintiff purchased a Class Printer”).  “Courts in the Ninth Circuit have consistently held that a 
1   plaintiff in a putative class action lacks standing to assert claims under the laws of states other than 
2   those where the plaintiff resides or was injured.”  Jones v. Micron Tech. Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">400 F. Supp. 3d 897</span><pre class="inline">, 
3   908 (N.D. Cal. 2019) (collecting cases).  Because Plaintiff resides in and was injured in California, 
4   see Compl. ¶¶ 91-103, he does not have standing to assert claims under the laws of any other 
5   states.  Therefore, Plaintiff cannot bring his fraud by omission claim under the common law of any 
6   state other than California.                                          
7        HP’s second argument gets to whether Plaintiff can bring his California consumer 
8   protection and fraud claims under the UCL, FAL, CLRA, and common law fraud by omission on 
9   behalf of a nationwide class.  See MTD at 17-22.  HP cites to Mazza v. American Honda Motor 
10   Co., in which the Ninth Circuit rejected a nationwide class because “variances in state law 
11   overwhelm common issues and preclude predominance for a single nationwide class.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8598712"><a href="/opinion/8619472/united-states-v-pelletier/#581" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Pelletier">666 F.3d 
12   581, 596</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012), overruled on other grounds by Olean Wholesale Grocery Coop., Inc. v. 
13   Bumble Bee Foods LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">31 F.4th 651</span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2022).  As the court explained, “California law 
14   may only be used on a classwide basis if ‘the interests of other states are not found to outweigh 
15   California’s interest in having its law applied.’”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> at 590 (quoting Wash. Mut. Bank, FA v. 
16   Superior Ct., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2585839"><a href="/opinion/2585839/wash-mut-bank-v-superior-court-of-orange-cty/#921" aria-description="Citation for case: Wash. Mut. Bank v. Superior Court of Orange Cty.">24 Cal. 4th 906, 921</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2001)).  The Mazza court then went on to conduct a detailed 
17   choice of law analysis, including examining the differences between California’s consumer 
18   protection laws and those of other states in which class members bought cars.  Id. at 590-94.  It 
19   ultimately concluded that “under the facts and circumstances of [the] case, . . . each class 
20   member’s consumer protection claim should be governed by the consumer protection laws of the 
21   jurisdiction in which the transaction took place.”  Id. at 594.  HP argues that under the Mazza 
22   analysis, California law cannot apply on a nationwide basis.  MTD at 17-22.  HP also argues that 
23   choice of law analysis is proper at this stage because it is a purely legal issue and So cannot show 
24   further discovery is necessary for the analysis.  Id. at 22.  So argues that California law can be 
25   applied to a nationwide class because HP’s actions occurred in California.  Opp. at 17-20. 
26        The Court agrees that under Mazza, choice of law issues might ultimately preclude a 
27   nationwide class.  But as Mazza demonstrates, a detailed and fact-intensive inquiry is necessary to 
1   not decide this issue at this stage of the case.  See Cullen v. Shutterfly Lifetouch, LLC, No. 20-cv-
2   06040-BLF, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 2000247</span><pre class="inline">, at *9 (N.D. Cal. May 19, 2021) (declining to address choice of 
3   law at “early stage of the case”); see also Wallace v. SharkNinja Operating, LLC, No. 18-cv-
4   05221-BLF, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2020 WL 1139649</span><pre class="inline">, at *14-15 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 9, 2020); Clancy v. The Bromley Tea 
5   Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8773417"><a href="/opinion/8789488/clancy-v-bromley-tea-co/#572" aria-description="Citation for case: Clancy v. Bromley Tea Co.">308 F.R.D. 564, 572</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2013); cf. Cimoli v. Alacer Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">587 F. Supp. 3d 978</span><pre class="inline">, 986-
6   87 (N.D. Cal 2022) (deciding choice of law at the pleading stage and distinguishing Cullen on the 
7   basis that the case at hand had already had an initial motion to dismiss, extensive discovery, and a 
8   first amended complaint).                                             
9        The motion to dismiss for lack of standing on behalf of classes bringing claims under the 
10   laws of states other than California is GRANTED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND and the 
11   motion to dismiss for lack of standing on behalf of a nationwide class under California law is 
12   DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.                                             
13      B.  Standing as to Other Printers                                  
14        HP argues that So does not have standing for printers he did not purchase.  MTD at 23-24.  
15   The Court takes the “substantially similar” approach to analyzing standing challenges, under 
16   which “a plaintiff may have standing to assert claims for unnamed class members based on 
17   products he or she did not purchase so long as the products and alleged misrepresentations are 
18   substantially similar.”  Romero v. HP, Inc., No. 16-cv-05415-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 386237</span><pre class="inline">, at *7 (N.D. 
19   Cal. Jan. 27, 2017) (quoting Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8704589"><a href="/opinion/8721396/miller-v-ghirardelli-chocolate-co/#869" aria-description="Citation for case: Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co.">912 F. Supp. 2d 861, 869</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">20   Cal. 2012</span><pre class="inline">)).  But a plaintiff cannot “simply provide[] a list of products with a bare allegation that 
21   the misrepresentations are the same as for the [product] Plaintiff purchased.”  Id. at *8; see also 
22   Arroyo v. TP-Link USA Corp., No. 5:14-cv-04999-EJD, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2015 WL 5698752</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 
23   29, 2015).                                                            
24        Here, So simply provides a list of Class Printers and conclusory allegations that the 
25   misrepresentations are the same for all of them.  See Compl. ¶¶ 89-90.  This is not sufficient.  
26   Plaintiff must provide more specific factual allegations, such as “facts that indicate what 
27   misrepresentations [or omissions] were made with respect to each printer, and whether the 
1   Romero, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 386237</span><pre class="inline">, at *8.                                        
2        The motion to dismiss claims for other printers is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO 
3   AMEND.                                                                
4      C.  Standing for Injunctive Relief                                 
5        HP also argues that So lacks standing to pursue injunctive relief.  MTD at 24-25.  
6   “Standing for injunctive relief requires a plaintiff to establish a ‘real and immediate threat of 
7   repeated injury.’”  Strojnik v. IA Lodging Napa First LLC, No. 19-CV-03983-DMR, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2020 WL 
8</span><pre class="inline">   2838814, at *5 (N.D. Cal. June 1, 2020) (quoting Fortyune v. Am. Multi-Cinema, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="200958"><a href="/opinion/200958/united-states-v-winn/#1075" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Winn">364 F.3d 
9   1075, 1081</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2004)).                                          
10        Plaintiff seeks an injunction requiring HP to (1) “reverse the effects of its malware 
11   transmissions” to make once-compatible third-party cartridges work again; (2) “cease accessing 
12   the Class Printers to obtain customer data without permission”; and (3) stop “sending such 
13   transmissions in the future” without owner consent.  Compl. Prayer for Relief ¶ F.  As to the first 
14   and third requests, HP asserts that because Plaintiff already purchased HP cartridges, he cannot 
15   show he will be wronged by future firmware updates, as he alleges they only affect printers using 
16   third-party cartridges.  MTD at 25.  As to the second request, HP asserts that So does not allege 
17   HP ever collected data from his printer specifically.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="200958"><a href="/opinion/200958/united-states-v-winn/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Winn">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  So relies on two cases from this district in 
18   arguing that the threat of future firmware updates combined with his alleged desire to go back to 
19   using third-party cartridges, is sufficient to allege standing for injunctive relief.  Opp. at 21-22. 
20        In Parziale v. HP, the court was confronted with a similar factual scenario to that at issue 
21   here.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">445 F. Supp. 3d 435</span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2020).  The court distinguished Davidson, a Ninth Circuit 
22   case holding that a plaintiff can establish standing to pursue injunctive relief against false labeling 
23   or advertising by alleging that they desire to purchase the product at issue in the future, on the 
24   basis that Parziale alleged he still owned the printers and raised claims not based on false 
25   advertising, such as a CFAA claim, in which the conduct is capable of repetition without 
26   purchasing another printer.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> at 451-52 (citing Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015730"><a href="/opinion/9022504/davidson-v-kimberly-clark-corp/#27" aria-description="Citation for case: Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.">889 F.3d 956 
27</a></span><pre class="inline">   (9th Cir. 2018)).  The Parziale court determined that “the threat of a future firmware update that 
1   standing, even in the absence of any allegations as to Plaintiff’s desire to purchase another 
2   printer.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015730"><a href="/opinion/9022504/davidson-v-kimberly-clark-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citing Spokeo, 578 U.S. at 339-40).  The court found the injury was “particularized” 
3   because it would affect Plaintiff in a “personal and individual way.”  Id. (internal quotation marks 
4   and citation omitted).   Further, the injury was “sufficiently concrete, considering that Plaintiff 
5   alleges a history of lawsuits based on similar misconduct by HP.”  Id.   
6        The Court here agrees with the approach in Parziale.  Plaintiff has standing to seek 
7   injunctive relief because he could be harmed by future firmware updates from HP.  While Plaintiff 
8   has purchased HP cartridges, he alleges he would like to use third-party cartridges again in the 
9   future.  Compl. ¶ 103; see Mobile Emergency Hous. Corp. v. HP, Inc., No. 20-cv-09157-SVK, 
10   Dkt. 52, at *22-23 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 15, 2021) (noting plaintiffs’ allegations that they would like to 
11   use third-party cartridges in the future in deciding there was standing for injunctive relief under 
12   Parziale).                                                            
13        HP’s motion to dismiss So’s claims for injunctive relief for lack of standing is thus 
14   DENIED.
