11                         STANDARD OF REVIEW                                 

12       A.  Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6)                         
13       The Court&#x27;s review of a motion to dismiss under FRCP 12(b)(6) is limited to the 
14  complaint and documents incorporated into the complaint by reference. Khoja v. 
15  Orexigen Therapeutics Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#998" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d 988, 998</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018); Lee v. City of Los 
16  Angeles, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6998020"><a href="/opinion/7092482/lee-v-city-of-los-angeles/#688" aria-description="Citation for case: Lee v. City of Los Angeles">250 F.3d 668, 688</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001). A motion to dismiss may be granted only if 
17  plaintiff&#x27;s complaint, with all factual allegations accepted as true, fails to “raise a right to 
18  relief above the speculative level.” Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544</a></span><pre class="inline">, 545 
19  (2007).                                                                   
20                                                                            
        To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual 
        matter, accepted as true, to “state a claim to relief that is plausible on its 
21                                                                            
        face.” A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual 
22       content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the 
        defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged. The plausibility standard is 
23       not akin to a probability requirement, but it asks for more than a sheer 
        possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully.                   
24                                                                            
1                                                                            
   Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#556" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 556, 570</a></span><pre class="inline">). 
2                                                                            
        A complaint must contain a “short and plain statement of the claim showing that 
3                                                                            
   the pleader is entitled to relief.” FRCP 8(a)(2). “Specific facts are not necessary; the 
4                                                                            
   statement need only give the defendant fair notice of what the ... claim is and the 
5                                                                            
   grounds upon which it rests.” Erickson v. Pardus, et al., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435053"><a href="/opinion/145722/erickson-v-pardus/#93" aria-description="Citation for case: Erickson v. Pardus">551 U.S. 89, 93</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007) (internal 
6                                                                            
   citations omitted). However, the pleading must be more than an “unadorned, the-
7                                                                            
   defendant-unlawfully-harmed-me accusation.” Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. at 678</a></span><pre class="inline">.       
8                                                                            
        While the Court must accept all the allegations contained in the complaint as 
9                                                                            
   true, the Court does not accept as true a “legal conclusion couched as a factual 
10                                                                            
   allegation.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> “Threadbare recitals of the elements of a cause of action, supported by 
11                                                                            
   mere conclusory statements, do not suffice.” Id.; Jones v. Community Development 
12                                                                            
   Agency, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.2d/733/646/">733 F.2d 646</a></span><pre class="inline">, 649 (9th Cir. 1984). “A district court abuses its discretion by 
13                                                                            
   denying leave to amend unless amendment would be futile or the plaintiff has failed to 
14                                                                            
   cure the complaint’s deficiencies despite repeated opportunities.” AE ex rel. Hernandez 
15                                                                            
   v. County of Tulare, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="621643"><a href="/opinion/621643/ae-ex-rel-hernandez-v-county-of-tulare/#636" aria-description="Citation for case: AE Ex Rel. Hernandez v. County of Tulare">666 F.3d 631, 636</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012).                   
16                                                                            
        To state a claim for relief under </pre><span class="citation no-link">42 U.S.C. § 1983</span><pre class="inline">, a plaintiff must show: (1) they 
17                                                                            
   suffered a violation of rights protected by the Constitution or created by federal statute, 
18                                                                            
   and (2) the violation was proximately caused by a person acting under color of state 
19                                                                            
   law. See Crumpton v. Gates, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="571121"><a href="/opinion/571121/john-crumpton-iv-v-daryl-gates-tom-bradley-tom-reddin-ed-davis-herbert/#1420" aria-description="Citation for case: John Crumpton, IV v. Daryl Gates Tom Bradley Tom Reddin...">947 F.2d 1418, 1420</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1991). The first step in a § 
20                                                                            
   1983 claim is therefore to identify the specific constitutional or statutory right allegedly 
21                                                                            
   infringed. Albright v. Oliver, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432926"><a href="/opinion/112924/albright-v-oliver/#271" aria-description="Citation for case: Albright v. Oliver">510 U.S. 266, 271</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1994). To satisfy the second prong, a 
22                                                                            
   plaintiff must allege facts showing how individually named defendants caused, or 
23                                                                            
24                                                                            
1  personally participated in causing, the harm alleged in the complaint. See Arnold v. IBM, 
2  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="385885"><a href="/opinion/385885/wolfgang-arnold-v-international-business-machines-corporation/#1355" aria-description="Citation for case: Wolfgang Arnold v. International Business MacHines...">637 F.2d 1350, 1355</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1981).                                      
3                             DISCUSSION                                     

4                                                                            
     A.  Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8                                   

5                                                                            
     Rule 8(a)(2) requires that the claim for relief contain “a short and plain statement of 
6                                                                            
   the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” Similarly, Rule 8(d)(1) requires 
7                                                                            
   “[e]ach allegation [to] be simple, concise, and direct.” In considering whether a 
8                                                                            
   complaint conforms to Rule 8, the Ninth Circuit has explained, “a dismissal for a 
9                                                                            
   violation under Rule 8(a)(2), is usually confined to instances in which the complaint is so 
10                                                                            
   ‘verbose, confused and redundant that its true substance, if any, is well disguised.’ ” 
11                                                                            
   Gillibeau v. City of Richmond, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8881595"><a href="/opinion/8895114/gillibeau-v-city-of-richmond/#431" aria-description="Citation for case: Gillibeau v. City of Richmond">417 F.2d 426, 431</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1969) (quoting Corcoran v. 
12                                                                            
   Yorty, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="268344"><a href="/opinion/268344/patrick-j-corcoran-v-mayor-samuel-w-yorty/#223" aria-description="Citation for case: Patrick J. Corcoran v. Mayor Samuel W. Yorty">347 F.2d 222, 223</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1965)).                                
13                                                                            
     But “verbosity or length is not by itself a basis for dismissing a complaint based on 
14                                                                            
   Rule 8(a).” Hearns v. San Bernardino Police Dep&#x27;t, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9553185"><a href="/opinion/1189534/hearns-v-san-bernardino-police-department/#1131" aria-description="Citation for case: Hearns v. San Bernardino Police Department">530 F.3d 1124, 1131</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008). 
15                                                                            
   To warrant dismissal under Rule 8(a), a complaint would have allegations that are 
16                                                                            
   overly confusing, ambiguous, vague, conclusory, redundant, or irrelevant -- obfuscating 
17                                                                            
   the substance of the core allegations. See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9553185"><a href="/opinion/1189534/hearns-v-san-bernardino-police-department/#1130" aria-description="Citation for case: Hearns v. San Bernardino Police Department">id. at 1130-32</a></span><pre class="inline"> (collecting cases). 
18                                                                            
     Defendants have not shown Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint violates the 
19                                                                            
   requirements of Fed. R. Civ. P. 8. Defendants argue that the First Amended Complaint, 
20                                                                            
   which includes 190 pages of “extraneous exhibits” should be dismissed because it is 
21                                                                            
   “not simple, concise, and direct” and does not contain and “short and plain statement of 
22                                                                            
   the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” Dkt. 38 at 6-7. But the motion 
23                                                                            
24                                                                            
1  does not demonstrate the First Amended Complaint in and of itself violates Fed. R. Civ. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">2  P. 8</span><pre class="inline">.                                                                     
3    At its root, Defendants challenge is directed at the length of the First Amended 
4  Complaint and its attachments, rather than arguing that the Plaintiffs have made 

5  confusing allegations. Yet, sheer length and verbosity are not reasons to strike the First 
6  Amended Complaint and order it repleaded. See Hearns, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9553185"><a href="/opinion/1189534/hearns-v-san-bernardino-police-department/#1130" aria-description="Citation for case: Hearns v. San Bernardino Police Department">530 F.3d at 1130-32</a></span><pre class="inline">.   
7    With respect to the Defendants’ challenge to the attachments that accompany the 
8  First Amended Complaint -- when assessing the sufficiency of a complaint under Rule 
9  12(b)(6), generally the Court may not consider material outside the pleadings. Lee v. 
10  City of Los Angeles, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6998020"><a href="/opinion/7092482/lee-v-city-of-los-angeles/#688" aria-description="Citation for case: Lee v. City of Los Angeles">250 F.3d 668, 688</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(d). 
11  “There are two exceptions to this rule: the incorporation-by-reference doctrine, and 
12  judicial notice under Federal Rule of Evidence 201.” Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, 
13  Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#998" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d 988, 998</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018); see also Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues &amp; Rts., 
14  Ltd., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435031"><a href="/opinion/145709/tellabs-inc-v-makor-issues-rights-ltd/#322" aria-description="Citation for case: Tellabs, Inc. v. Makor Issues &amp; Rights, Ltd.">551 U.S. 308, 322</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007) (“[C]ourts must consider the complaint in its entirety, as 

15  well as other sources courts ordinarily examine when ruling on Rule 12(b)(6) motions to 
16  dismiss, in particular, documents incorporated into the complaint by reference, and 
17  matters of which a court may take judicial notice.”).                     
18    Rule 201 of the Federal Rules of Evidence provides that courts may take judicial 
19  notice of “adjudicative fact[s]” that are “not subject to reasonable dispute,” meaning the 
20  fact “can be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot 
21  reasonably be questioned.” Fed. R. Evid. 201(a)–(b). But Courts are not allowed to take 
22  judicial notice of disputed facts contained in documents that contain other facts 
23                                                                            

24                                                                            
1  susceptible to judicial notice. See Khoja, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#999" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 999</a></span><pre class="inline">. The Court must “clearly 
2  specify” the fact or facts being judicially noticed. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">                  
3    “Unlike rule-established judicial notice, incorporation-by-reference is a judicially 
4  created doctrine that treats certain documents as though they are part of the complaint 

5  itself.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id. at 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">. The doctrine is designed to prevent plaintiffs “from selecting only 
6  portions of documents that support their claims, while omitting portions of those very 
7  documents that weaken—or doom—their claims.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">                          
8    A document may be incorporated by reference into a complaint if it either: 
9    •   “forms the basis of [a] plaintiff&#x27;s claim” or                       
10    •  is referred to “extensively” by the plaintiff. United States v. Ritchie, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="783479"><a href="/opinion/783479/united-states-v-donald-lawrence-ritchie-heather-horner/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Donald Lawrence Ritchie, Heather Horner,...">342 F.3d 903</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
11       908 (9th Cir. 2003). For a reference to be sufficiently “extensive,” a document 
12       should be referred to “more than once.” Khoja, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1003" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 1003</a></span><pre class="inline">. But “a single 
13       reference” could, in theory, satisfy the standard if the reference is “relatively 
14       lengthy.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">                                                        

15    Generally, and unlike judicial notice, district courts “may assume [an incorporated 
16  document&#x27;s] contents are true for purposes of a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6).” 
17  Marder v. Lopez, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="794585"><a href="/opinion/794585/marder-v-lopez/#448" aria-description="Citation for case: Marder v. Lopez">450 F.3d 445, 448</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2006) (quoting Ritchie, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="783479"><a href="/opinion/783479/united-states-v-donald-lawrence-ritchie-heather-horner/#908" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Donald Lawrence Ritchie, Heather Horner,...">342 F.3d at 908</a></span><pre class="inline">). It 
18  is improper, however, for courts “to assume the truth of an incorporated document if 
19  such assumptions only serve to dispute facts stated in a well-pleaded complaint.” Khoja, 
20  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1003" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 1003</a></span><pre class="inline">. Thus, courts must be cautious when drawing inferences from 
21  incorporated documents. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">

17    B.  Claims against Individual Defendants                                
18    Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint should be dismissed 
19  because the individual Defendants were not mentioned in any of Plaintiffs’ causes of 
20  action.                                                                   
21    To state a claim for relief against individual defendants under </pre><span class="citation no-link">42 U.S.C. § 1983</span><pre class="inline">, 
22  Plaintiff must allege that each defendant personally participated in the deprivation of  
23                                                                            

24                                                                            
1  constitutional rights. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#673" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. at 673</a></span><pre class="inline">; Colwell v. Bannister, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9802856"><a href="/opinion/2718116/john-colwell-v-robert-bannister/" aria-description="Citation for case: John Colwell v. Robert Bannister">763 F.3d 1060</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1070 
2  (9th Cir. 2014).                                                          
3    Liability may not be imposed on supervisory personnel for the acts or omissions of 
4  their subordinates under the theory of respondeat superior. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#672" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. at 672-673</a></span><pre class="inline">; 

5  Jones v. Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7012309"><a href="/opinion/7106340/jones-v-williams/#934" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Williams">297 F.3d 930, 934</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2002). A supervisor can be held liable in 
6  their individual capacity under § 1983 only if (1) they personally participated in the

4    D. Plaintiffs’ Free Exercise Claim                                      

5    The First Amendment states that “Congress shall make no law respecting an 
6  establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof.” This prohibition 
7  applies to the states through the 14th Amendment. Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, 
8  Inc. v. City of Hialeah, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432833"><a href="/opinion/112881/church-of-the-lukumi-babalu-aye-inc-v-city-of-hialeah/#531" aria-description="Citation for case: Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. 9  any “law [that] discriminates against some or all religious beliefs or regulates or prohibits 
10  conduct because it is undertaken for religious reasons.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432833"><a href="/opinion/112881/church-of-the-lukumi-babalu-aye-inc-v-city-of-hialeah/#532" aria-description="Citation for case: Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah">Id. at 532</a></span><pre class="inline">.      
11    To state a claim under the Free Exercise Clause, a plaintiff must show the 
12  challenged government action “substantially burdens a religious practice and either is 
13  not justified by a substantial state interest or is not narrowly tailored to achieve that 
14  interest.” Sabra v. Maricopa Cnty. Cmty. Coll. Dist., </pre><span class="citation no-link">44 F.4th 867</span><pre class="inline">, 890 (9th Cir. 2022). “A 

15  substantial burden ... place[s] more than an inconvenience on religious exercise; it must 
16  have a tendency to coerce individuals into acting contrary to their religious beliefs or 
17  exert substantial pressure on an adherent to modify his behavior and to violate his 
18  beliefs.” Jones v. Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2812255"><a href="/opinion/2812255/clarence-jones-v-max-williams/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clarence Jones v. Max Williams">791 F.3d 1023</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1031–32 (9th Cir. 2015). Notably, “the right 
19  of free exercise does not relieve an individual of the obligation to comply with a ‘valid 
20  and neutral law of general applicability on the ground that the law proscribes (or 
21  prescribes) conduct that his religion prescribes (or proscribes).’ ” Doe v. San Diego 
22  Unified Sch. Dist., </pre><span class="citation no-link">19 F.4th 1173</span><pre class="inline">, 1180 (9th Cir. 2021), reconsideration en banc denied, 
23  </pre><span class="citation no-link">22 F.4th 1099</span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2022) (citation omitted).

11    F.  Plaintiffs’ Public Policy Tort Claim                                
12    Plaintiffs allege the public policies at issue are “employment discrimination without 
13  just cause” (Dkt. 34 at ¶525) and violation of “employment agreements” that prohibit 
14  termination without just cause (Dkt. 34 at ¶524).                         

15    To satisfy the elements of a claim of wrongful discharge in violation of public policy, 
16  the “plaintiff must prove (1) the existence of a clear public policy (clarity element); (2) 
17  that discouraging the conduct in which [he or she] engaged would jeopardize the public 
18  policy (jeopardy element); and (3) that the public-policy-linked conducted caused the 
19  dismissal (causation element).” Hubbard v. Spokane County, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4712263"><a href="/opinion/4907805/hubbard-v-spokane-county/#707" aria-description="Citation for case: Hubbard v. Spokane County">146 Wash.2d 699, 707</a></span><pre class="inline">, 50 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">20  P.3d 602</span><pre class="inline"> (2002). Then, (4) “the defendant must not be able to offer an overriding 
21  justification for the dismissal (absence of justification element).”      
22    Courts have generally limited public policy tort actions to situations in which the 
23  employee is discharged for (1) refusing to commit an illegal act; (2) performing a public 

24                                                                            
1  duty or obligation; (3) exercising a legal right or privilege; or (4) engaging in 
2  “whistleblowing” activity. Dicomes v. State, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1147623"><a href="/opinion/1147623/dicomes-v-state/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dicomes v. State">113 Wash.2d 612</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1147623"><a href="/opinion/1147623/dicomes-v-state/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dicomes v. State">782 P.2d 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1006–07 
3  (1989). If an employee&#x27;s public policy tort action falls into one of the four Dicomes 
4  categories, they establish a prima facie case of wrongful discharge in violation of public 

5  policy by showing that (1) their discharge may have been motivated by reasons that 
6  contravene a clear mandate of public policy; and (2) their public-policy-linked conduct 
7  was a significant factor in the decision to discharge him. Martin v. Gonzaga Univ., 191 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="4002067"><a href="/opinion/4225765/l-romano-engineering-corp-v-state/" aria-description="Citation for case: L. Romano Engineering Corp. v. State">8  Wash.2d 712</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4312149"><a href="/opinion/4534896/martin-v-gonzaga-univ/#844" aria-description="Citation for case: Martin v. Gonzaga Univ.">425 P.3d 837, 844</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2018). The McDonnell Douglas burden-shifting 
9  framework applies. Martin, 425 P.3d at 844–45.                            
10    Defendants argue Plaintiffs should be precluded from pursing a wrongful discharge 
11  in violation of public policy claim where other statutes, such as the WLAD, provide an 
12  adequate statutory remedy for the alleged violation of public policy. See Dkt. 38 at 20-
13  21.                                                                       
14    Between 2002 and 2011, the Washington Supreme Court followed a tangential line 

15  of reasoning regarding the jeopardy element of the wrongful discharge tort, concluding 
16  that a plaintiff could not prove that his or her conduct was necessary for the effective 
17  enforcement of a public policy (i.e., that the public policy was in jeopardy) unless they 
18  proved that no other statutory provision existed that would adequately protect that 
19  policy. See Rose v. Anderson Hay and Grain Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4715819"><a href="/opinion/4910117/rose-v-anderson-hay-grain-co/#275" aria-description="Citation for case: Rose v. Anderson Hay &amp; Grain Co.">184 Wn.2d 268, 275-280</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2015) 
20  (tracing the evolution of the tort in Washington).                        
21    In a trio of cases decided in 2015, the Supreme Court recognized that requiring 
22  plaintiff to show the inadequacy of alternative statutory remedies was a departure from 
23  its earlier precedents that had created confusion and harmfully deprived aggrieved 

24                                                                            
1  litigants of a rightful tort claim without any corresponding benefit to defendants. Rose, 
2  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4715819"><a href="/opinion/4910117/rose-v-anderson-hay-grain-co/#281" aria-description="Citation for case: Rose v. Anderson Hay &amp; Grain Co.">184 Wn.2d at 281-82</a></span><pre class="inline">. The requirement was therefore disavowed, and the contrary line 
3  of cases was overruled. Courts no longer reject a wrongful discharge in violation of 
4  public policy because there are adequate statutory remedies: rather, dismissal of the 

5  tort claim is appropriate only where the alternative statutory remedy is intended to be 
6  exclusive. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4715819"><a href="/opinion/4910117/rose-v-anderson-hay-grain-co/#285" aria-description="Citation for case: Rose v. Anderson Hay &amp; Grain Co.">Id. at 285-86</a></span><pre class="inline">. Defendants have not shown this.                 
7    Plaintiffs’ conduct arguably falls into one of the four Dicomes categories, i.e., 
8  asserting a legal right, and Defendants have not shown that any alternative statutory 
9  remedy is intended to be exclusive. Plaintiffs state a sufficient public policy tort claim at 
10  this stage. The Court DENIES Defendants’ motion to dismiss this claim.    
11    G. Plaintiffs’ Takings Clause claim                                     
12    The Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment, which applies to local governments 
13  through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides: “[N]or shall private property be taken for 
14  public use, without just compensation.” To prove a taking, each plaintiff must establish a 

15  protected property interest under Washington law. See Vandevere v. Lloyd, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="215896"><a href="/opinion/215896/united-states-v-booker/#957" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Booker">644 F.3d 
16  957, 963</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011).                                                 
17       Plaintiffs allege they “each were deprived of wages, pension rights, and other 
18  contractual benefits of employment by the wrongful actions of Defendants,” and 
19  “intangible property rights, including valid contracts, are protected by the Takings 
20  Clause.” Dkt. 34 at ¶539-546. Plaintiffs, however, miss a fundamental element of the 
21  Takings Clause –the private property must have been used for public use. Plaintiffs fail 
22  to allege that their wages, pension rights and “other contractual benefits of employment” 
23  were used for public use.                                                 

24                                                                            
1       Therefore, Plaintiffs have failed to state a claim under the Takings Clause and 
2  Defendants’ motion to dismiss this claim is GRANTED.                      
3    H. Plaintiffs’ Wage Theft Claim                                         
4    Under the first paragraph of RCW 49.48.010 (2020) (amended 2022), “[w]hen any 

5  employee shall cease to work for an employer, whether by discharge or by voluntary 
6  withdrawal, the wages due him or her on account of his or her employment shall be paid 
7  to him or her at the end of the established pay period.” The second paragraph provides: 
8  “It shall be unlawful for any employer to withhold or divert any portion of an employee&#x27;s 
9  wages unless the deduction is” required by law, agreed to by employer and employee, 
10  or for medical services. RCW 49.48.010.                                   
11    Plaintiffs have not plausibly alleged a violation of the statute. While lost wages may 
12  be an element of the damages for Plaintiffs’ employment claims, Plaintiffs have not 
13  alleged that they have been unlawfully deprived of wages other than as damages 
14  resulting from other claims. To assert a plausible wage theft claim, Plaintiffs would need 

15  to assert a separate cause of action alleging, for example, that Defendants did not pay 
16  Plaintiffs for all hours worked before they were terminated from their employment or 
17  illegally deducted an amount from their paychecks.                        
18    Thus, Plaintiffs’ have failed to state a wage theft claim, and Defendants’ motion to 
19  dismiss this claim is GRANTED.                                            
20    I.  Plaintiffs’ Breach of Contract Claim                                
21    In Washington, “[a] breach of contract is actionable only if the contract imposes a 
22  duty, the duty is breached, and the breach proximately causes damage to the claimant.” 
23                                                                            

24                                                                            
1  C 1031 Properties, Inc. v. First Am. Title Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4761664"><a href="/opinion/4953557/c-1031-properties-inc-v-first-american-title-insurance/" aria-description="Citation for case: C 1031 Properties, Inc. v. First American Title Insurance">175 Wash. App. 27</a></span><pre class="inline">, 33–34, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9823377"><a href="/opinion/5309018/levier-v-department-of-workforce-services-workforce-appeals-board/#500" aria-description="Citation for case: Levier v. Department of Workforce Services, Workforce...">301 P.3d 
2  500</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2013).                                                               
3    Failing to identify a breached contractual provision dooms a breach of contract 
4  claim. See, e.g., Shroyer v. New Cingular Wireless Services, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="175568"><a href="/opinion/175568/shroyer-v-new-cingular-wireless-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Shroyer v. New Cingular Wireless Services, Inc.">622 F.3d 1035</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1041-42 

5  (9th Cir. 2010) (although the first amended complaint did not point to the specific 
6  contractual provisions that Plaintiff alleges were violated, Plaintiff identified the relevant 
7  contractual provisions in his opposition to the 12(b)(6) motion, thereby successfully 
8  stating a breach of contract claim); Ogorsolka v. Residential Credit Sols., Inc., No. 2:14-
9  CV-00078-RSM, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 2860742</span><pre class="inline">, at *7 (W.D. Wash. June 23, 2014) (concluding that 
10  because the complaint “failed to identify any relevant portion of a contract that 
11  Defendants breached,” the plaintiffs failed to state a cognizable breach of contract 
12  claim); BP W. Coast Prod., LLC v. Shalabi, No. C11-1341-MJP, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 441155</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 
13  (W.D. Wash. Feb. 10, 2012) (“A breach of contract claim must point to a provision of the 
14  contract that was breached.”).                                            

15    Plaintiffs allege there “existed a binding contract between each Plaintiff and the 
16  Department” and the Department breached this contract by terminating Plaintiffs without 
17  just cause. Dkt. 34 at ¶470-480. Plaintiffs further allege Defendants’ actions violated 
18  Plaintiffs’ pension rights, which were established by contract. </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. at ¶477</span><pre class="inline">. Plaintiffs, 
19  however, failed to identify the specific provisions allegedly breached by Defendants in 
20  both their First Amended Complaint and in their response to Defendants’ motion to 
21  dismiss. The Court does not expect Plaintiffs to attach copies of all their challenged 
22  contracts to the First Amended Complaint; yet, Plaintiffs are required to plead their 
23  breach of contract claim with more specificity to clarify, for example, what were the 

24                                                                            
1  terms of the “just cause” portion of their employment contract that was allegedly 
2  breached by the Defendants, whether Plaintiffs’ pension rights were specified in a 
3  separate contract, and what were the terms of those pension rights the Defendants 
4  allegedly breached.                                                       

5    Thus, Plaintiffs have not sufficiently pled a breach of contract claim at this time. 
6  Defendants’ motion to dismiss this claim is GRANTED.                      
7    J.  Plaintiffs’ Failure to Accommodate under Washington Law Against Discrimination 
8    Plaintiffs claim Defendants failed to accommodate their sincerely held religious 
9  beliefs in violation of the Washington Law Against Discrimination (WLAD), RCW 49.60. 
10    Under the WLAD, employers may not refuse to hire, discharge, bar from   
11  employment, or discriminate against in compensation or other terms of employment any 
12  person because of their religion. RCW § 49.60.180; see Kumar v. Gate Gourmet, Inc., 
13  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4710729"><a href="/opinion/4906934/kumar-v-gate-gourmet-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Kumar v. Gate Gourmet, Inc.">180 Wash.2d 481</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4710729"><a href="/opinion/4906934/kumar-v-gate-gourmet-inc/#203" aria-description="Citation for case: Kumar v. Gate Gourmet, Inc.">325 P.3d 193, 203</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2014) (en banc). The WLAD creates a cause of 
14  action for failure to reasonably accommodate an employee&#x27;s religious practices. Kumar, 

15  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4710729"><a href="/opinion/4906934/kumar-v-gate-gourmet-inc/#203" aria-description="Citation for case: Kumar v. Gate Gourmet, Inc.">325 P.3d at 203</a></span><pre class="inline">. To plead a WLAD failure-to-accommodate claim, Plaintiffs must 
16  plausibly allege substantially the same elements as a Title VII failure-to-accommodate 
17  claim. See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4710729"><a href="/opinion/4906934/kumar-v-gate-gourmet-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Kumar v. Gate Gourmet, Inc.">id.</a></span><pre class="inline">                                                            
18    A plaintiff establishes a prima facie claim of failure to accommodate a religious belief 
19  or practice by showing that (1) he or she has a bona fide religious belief, the practice of 
20  which conflicts with employment duties; (2) he or she informed the employer of the 
21  beliefs and the conflict; and (3) the employer responded by subjecting the employee to 
22  threatened or actual discriminatory treatment. See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4710729"><a href="/opinion/4906934/kumar-v-gate-gourmet-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Kumar v. Gate Gourmet, Inc.">id.</a></span><pre class="inline">                    
23                                                                            

24                                                                            
1    “Once an employee establishes a prima facie case of failure to accommodate 
2  religion, the burden shifts to the employer to show ‘either that it initiated good faith 
3  efforts to accommodate reasonably the employee&#x27;s religious practices or that it could 
4  not reasonably accommodate the employee without undue hardship.’ ” Bolden-Hardge 

5  v. Off. of Cal. State Controller, </pre><span class="citation no-link">63 F.4th 1215</span><pre class="inline">, 1224 (9th Cir. 2023) (quoting Tiano v. 
6  Dillard Dep&#x27;t Stores, Inc., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/139/679/">139 F.3d 679</a></span><pre class="inline">, 681 (9th Cir. 1998)).           
7    Regarding establishing a bona fide religious belief in conflict with an employment 
8  duty, “[a] religious belief need not be consistent or rational to be protected under Title 
9  VII, and an assertion of a sincere religious belief is generally accepted.” Keene v. City &amp; 
10  Cnty. of San Francisco, No. 22-16567, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 3451687</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (9th Cir. May 15, 2023) 
11  (citing Thomas v. Rev. Bd., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9428253"><a href="/opinion/110440/thomas-v-review-board-of-the-indiana-employment-security-division/#714" aria-description="Citation for case: Thomas v. Review Board of the Indiana Employment Security...">450 U.S. 707, 714</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1981)). Indeed, the court may not 
12  “question the legitimacy of [an individual&#x27;s] religious beliefs regarding COVID-19 
13  vaccinations.” Doe v. San Diego Unified Sch. Dist., </pre><span class="citation no-link">19 F.4th 1173</span><pre class="inline">, 1176 n. 3 (9th Cir. 
14  2021) (citing Masterpiece Cakeshop, Ltd. v. Colorado C.R. Comm&#x27;n, </pre><span class="citation no-link">584 U.S. 617</span><pre class="inline"> 

15  (2018)).                                                                  
16    Plaintiffs here have stated a claim for failure to reasonably accommodate their 
17  religious practices. Their First Amended Complaint alleges that (1) they hold a sincere 
18  religious belief that conflicted with Defendants’ vaccine mandate; (2) they informed 
19  Defendants of this conflict; and (3) Defendants failed to consider the accommodations 
20  proposed by Plaintiffs, leaving Plaintiffs to choose between being terminated or taking a 
21  vaccine in direct violation of their sincerely held religious beliefs. See Dkt. 34 at ¶481-
22  491.                                                                      
23                                                                            

24                                                                            
1    Plaintiffs have met their burden to establish a prima facie religious accommodation 
2  claim. Berry v. Dep&#x27;t of Soc. Serv., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="794284"><a href="/opinion/794284/daniel-m-berry-v-department-of-social-services-tehama-county-bill/#655" aria-description="Citation for case: Daniel M. Berry v. Department of Social Services, Tehama...">447 F.3d 642, 655</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir.2006). Thus, Defendants’ 
3  motion to dismiss Plaintiffs’ failure to accommodate claim is DENIED.     
4                             CONCLUSION                                     

5       For the foregoing reasons, the Court DENIES Defendants’ motion to dismiss the 
6  following claims: (1) procedural due process, (2) First Amendment Free Exercise, Due 
7  Process, and Equal Protection regarding the Defendants’ unwritten customs or 
8  practices, or an as-applied challenge (3) arbitrary and capricious action; (4) public 
9  policy; (5) and failure to accommodate.                                   
10       The Court GRANTS Defendants’ motion to dismiss: (1) Plaintiffs’ Takings Clause 
11  claim under the Fifth Amendment, (2) wage theft claim, (3) claim under the First 
12  Amendment Free Exercise Clause regarding the facial validity of the Defendants’ formal 
13  policy, and (4) breach of contract claim.                                 
14       Where a motion to dismiss is granted, a district court should provide leave to 

15  amend unless it is clear that the complaint could not be saved by any     
16  amendment. See Eminence Capital, LLC v. Aspeon, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9495724"><a href="/opinion/780573/eminence-capital-llc-and-jay-spechler-v-aspeon-inc-richard-p-stack/#1052" aria-description="Citation for case: Eminence Capital, Llc, and Jay Spechler v. Aspeon, Inc....">316 F.3d 1048, 1052</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
17  2003). Even after a complaint has been amended or a responsive pleading has been 
18  served, the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure provide that “[t]he court should freely give 
19  leave [to amend] when justice so requires.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a)(2). The Ninth Circuit 
20  requires that this policy favoring amendment be applied with “extreme     
21  liberality.” Morongo Band of Mission Indians v. Rose, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="534877"><a href="/opinion/534877/morongo-band-of-mission-indians-plaintiff-counter-defendantappellant-v/#1079" aria-description="Citation for case: Morongo Band of Mission Indians,...">893 F.2d 1074, 1079</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
22  1990). “In determining whether leave to amend is appropriate, the district court 
23  considers ‘the presence of any of four factors: bad faith, undue delay, prejudice to the 

24                                                                            
1  opposing party, and/or futility.’ ” Owens v. Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">244 F.3d 
2</span><pre class="inline">  708, 712 (9th Cir. 2001) (quoting Griggs v. Pace Am. Group, Inc., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/170/877/">170 F.3d 877</a></span><pre class="inline">, 880 
3  (9th Cir. 1999)).                                                         
4       In assessing Plaintiffs’ Takings Clause claim, wage theft claim, Free Exercise 

5  claim regarding whether the formal policy of the City and Fire Department was facially 
6  valid under the First Amendment, and breach of contract claim, the Court finds it is 
7  appropriate to allow Plaintiffs to file a motion for leave to amend their breach of contract 
8  claim only. The Court finds allowing Plaintiffs to amend the remaining claims to be futile. 
9       As discussed above, Plaintiffs repeatedly mention employment and pension 
10  contracts in the First Amended Complaint but fail to specify any of the provisions that 
11  were allegedly breached by Defendants. It appears that this claim potentially could be 
12  supported if Plaintiffs choose to again amend their complaint and specify the provisions 
13  they allege to have been breached.
