18    I.  BACKGROUND                                                       
19        On August 1, 2023, Plaintiff Mark Hayden (“Plaintiff”) filed a putative class action 
20   complaint against Bob’s Red Mill Natural Foods, Inc. (“Defendant”) concerning its allegedly 
21   deceptive marketing of its Whole Ground Flaxseed Meal and Golden Flaxseed Meal, in both non-
22   organic and organic varieties (“the Products”).  Dkt. No. 1 (“Compl.”) ¶ 12.  Specifically, Plaintiff 
23   alleges that the Products’ front label contains representations such as “To Your Good Health,” 
24   “You Can See Our Quality,” “Non-GMO,” and “Gluten Free.”  The back label allegedly also 
25   states (among other things) that the Products “contain a wealth of nutrients” and “give[] your body 
26   access to the amazing nourishment,” and note that there “is a general agreement among experts of 
27   all sorts that folks should consume more omega-3 fatty acids to promote good health” 
1   Representations give the “net-effect or net-impression . . . that the Products do not contain any 
2   potentially harmful ingredients like high levels of cadmium,” and that this impression is 
3   misleading because the Products do contain high levels of cadmium.  Id. ¶ 12.  According to 
4   testing performed by ConsumerLabs.com, for example, the (non-organic) Whole Ground Flaxseed 
5   Meal tested positive for 6.1 micrograms of cadmium per serving and the (organic) Whole Golden 
6   Flaxseed Meal product tested positive for 5.5 micrograms of cadmium per serving.  Compl. ¶¶ 17, 
7   21.  Plaintiff further alleges that the cadmium found in the Products – a “known human carcinogen 
8   linked to a myriad of health issues” – is “unsafe and unlawful” and should be disclosed.  Id. ¶ 2.   
9        In light of Defendant’s allegedly deceptive labeling, Plaintiff brings three causes of action 
10   for violations of California’s Consumers Legal Remedies Act (“CLRA”) (</pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1750</span><pre class="inline"> 
11   et seq.) and Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”) (</pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200</span><pre class="inline"> et seq.), and for 
12   breach of implied warranties.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> ¶¶ 55–86.  On September 28, 2024, Defendant moved to dismiss 
13   Plaintiff’s complaint in its entirety, arguing that Plaintiff lacks standing and has failed to plead any 
14   plausible claim for relief.  Dkt. No. 17 (“Mot.”).  Defendant also filed a request for judicial notice.  
15   Dkt. No. 18 (“RJN”).  The matter is now fully briefed and ready for disposition.  See Dkt. No. 20 
16   (“Opp.”), 25 (“Reply”).                                               
17    II.  LEGAL STANDARD                                                  
18        A.  Rule 12(b)(1)                                                
19        Federal Rule of Civil Procedure Rule 12(b)(1) allows a party to move to dismiss for lack of 
20   subject matter jurisdiction.  See Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1).  The issue of Article III standing is 
21   jurisdictional and is therefore “properly raised in a motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil 
22   Procedure 12(b)(1).”  White v. Lee, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6987266"><a href="/opinion/7082005/white-v-lee/#1242" aria-description="Citation for case: White v. Lee">227 F.3d 1214, 1242</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2000).  To meet his burden of 
23   establishing standing, a plaintiff must show he has “(1) suffered an injury in fact, (2) that is fairly 
24   traceable to the challenged conduct of the defendant, and (3) that is likely to be redressed by a 
25   favorable judicial decision.”  Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, </pre><span class="citation no-link">578 U.S. 330</span><pre class="inline">, 338 (2016), as revised (May 
26   24, 2016).  And where a plaintiff seeks injunctive relief, he must also demonstrate a “real and 
27   immediate threat of repeated injury.”  Chapman v. Pier 1 Imports (U.S.) Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9438879"><a href="/opinion/182444/chapman-v-pier-1-imports-us-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Chapman v. Pier 1 Imports (U.S.) Inc.">631 F.3d 939</a></span><pre class="inline">, 946 
1        If a plaintiff fails to establish standing or any other aspect of subject matter jurisdiction, 
2   “the court, on having the defect called to its attention or on discovering the same, must dismiss the 
3   case, unless the defect be corrected by amendment.”  Tosco Corp. v. Communities for a Better 
4   Env’t, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6991164"><a href="/opinion/7085822/tosco-corp-v-communities-for-a-better-environment/#499" aria-description="Citation for case: Tosco Corp. v. Communities for a Better Environment">236 F.3d 495, 499</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001), abrogated on other grounds by Hertz Corp. v. Friend, 
5   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1735"><a href="/opinion/1735/hertz-corp-v-friend/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hertz Corp. v. Friend">559 U.S. 77</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2010).                                                   
6        B.  Rule 12(b)(6)                                                
7        Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(a) requires that a complaint contain “a short and plain 
8   statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2).  A 
9   defendant may move to dismiss a complaint for failing to state a claim upon which relief can be 
10   granted under Rule 12(b)(6).  “Dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) is appropriate only where the 
11   complaint lacks a cognizable legal theory or sufficient facts to support a cognizable legal theory.”  
12   Mendiondo v. Centinela Hosp. Med. Ctr., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1300996"><a href="/opinion/1300996/mendiondo-v-centinela-hospital-medical-center/#1104" aria-description="Citation for case: Mendiondo v. Centinela Hospital Medical Center">521 F.3d 1097, 1104</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).  To survive a Rule 
13   12(b)(6) motion, a plaintiff need only plead “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible 
14   on its face.”  Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007).  A claim is facially plausible 
15   when a plaintiff pleads “factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that 
16   the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009). 
17        In reviewing the plausibility of a complaint, courts “accept factual allegations in the 
18   complaint as true and construe the pleadings in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.”  
19   Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1194077"><a href="/opinion/1194077/manzarek-v-st-paul-fire-marine-insurance/#1031" aria-description="Citation for case: Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Insurance">519 F.3d 1025, 1031</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).  Nevertheless, 
20   courts do not “accept as true allegations that are merely conclusory, unwarranted deductions of 
21   fact, or unreasonable inferences.”  In re Gilead Scis. Secs. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1400355"><a href="/opinion/1400355/in-re-gilead-sciences-securities-litigation/#1055" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Gilead Sciences Securities Litigation">536 F.3d 1049, 1055</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
22   2008) (quoting Sprewell v. Golden State Warriors, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7006046"><a href="/opinion/7100340/sprewell-v-golden-state-warriors/#988" aria-description="Citation for case: Sprewell v. Golden State Warriors">266 F.3d 979, 988</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001)). 
23        Rule 9(b) imposes a heightened pleading standard where fraud is an essential element of a 
24   claim.  See Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b) (“In alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with particularity 
25   the circumstances constituting fraud or mistake.”); see also Vess v. Ciba–Geigy Corp. USA, 317 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="672148"><a href="/opinion/672148/michael-rene-pardue-v-larry-burton/#1107" aria-description="Citation for case: Michael Rene Pardue v. Larry Burton">26   F.3d 1097, 1107</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2003).  A plaintiff must identify “the who, what, when, where, and how” 
27   of the alleged conduct, so as to provide defendants with sufficient information to defend against 
1   knowledge, and other conditions of a person’s mind may be alleged generally.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 
2   Rule 9(b).                                                            
3        Even if the court concludes that a 12(b)(6) motion should be granted, the “court should 
4   grant leave to amend even if no request to amend the pleading was made, unless it determines that 
5   the pleading could not possibly be cured by the allegation of other facts.”  Lopez v. Smith, 203 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">6   F.3d 1122</span><pre class="inline">, 1127 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (quotation omitted).

8      A.  Request for Judicial Notice                                    
9        Before turning to the substance of Defendant’s motion, the Court considers its requests for 
10   judicial notice, which are unopposed.  Dkt. No. 18.  The Court will grant some of the requests but

12           i.  Legal Standard                                            
13        In Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, the Ninth Circuit clarified the judicial notice rule and 
14   incorporation by reference doctrine.  See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d 988</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018).  Under Federal Rule of 
15   Evidence 201, a court may take judicial notice of a fact “not subject to reasonable dispute because 
16   it … can be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be 
17   questioned.”  Fed. R. Evid. 201(b)(2).  Accordingly, a court may take “judicial notice of matters of 
18   public record,” but “cannot take judicial notice of disputed facts contained in such public records.”  
19   Khoja, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#999" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 999</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citation and quotations omitted).  The Ninth Circuit has clarified that if a 
20   court takes judicial notice of a document, it must specify what facts it judicially noticed from the 
21   document.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#999" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id. at 999</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Further, “[j]ust because the document itself is susceptible to judicial notice 
22   does not mean that every assertion of fact within that document is judicially noticeable for its 
23   truth.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  As an example, the Ninth Circuit held that for a transcript of a conference call, the 
24   court may take judicial notice of the fact that there was a conference call on the specified date, but 
25   may not take judicial notice of a fact mentioned in the transcript, because the substance “is subject 
26   to varying interpretations, and there is a reasonable dispute as to what the [document] establishes.”  
27   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 999–1000.                                                      
1   allows a court to consider certain documents as though they were part of the complaint itself.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> 
2   at 1002.  This is to prevent plaintiffs from cherry-picking certain portions of documents that 
3   support their claims, while omitting portions that weaken their claims.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Incorporation by 
4   reference is appropriate “if the plaintiff refers extensively to the document or the document forms 
5   the basis of plaintiff’s claim.”  Khoja, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">.  However, “the mere mention of the 
6   existence of a document is insufficient to incorporate the contents” of a document.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id. at 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">.  
7   And while a court “may assume [an incorporated document’s] contents are true for purposes of a 
8   motion to dismiss … it is improper to assume the truth of an incorporated document if such 
9   assumptions only serve to dispute facts stated in a well-pleaded complaint.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">

22        B.  Motion to Dismiss                                            
23           i.  Standing                                                  
24        Defendant argues that Plaintiff lacks standing to bring his claims, which is a jurisdictional 
25   challenge assessed under Rule 12(b)(1).  Specifically, Defendant argues that Plaintiff (1) did not 
26   suffer an injury-in-fact, (2) does not have standing to sue for products he did not purchase, and (3) 
27   has not adequately alleged a risk of future harm to justify his request for injunctive relief.  The 
1   case on standing grounds.                                             
2              a.  Injury-in-Fact                                         
3        First, Defendant suggests Plaintiff has not adequately alleged injury-in-fact, and therefore 
4   lacks Article III standing.  Mot. at 13–16.  The Court disagrees.     
5        Injury is one of the three “irreducible minimum” components of standing.  Lujan v. Defs. 
6   of Wildlife, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/#590" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">504 U.S. 555, 590</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">112 S.Ct. 2130</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">119 L.Ed.2d 351</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1992).  “A ‘quintessential injury-
7   in-fact’ occurs when plaintiffs allege that they ‘spent money that, absent defendants’ actions, they 
8   would not have spent.”  Gagetta v. Walmart, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">646 F. Supp. 3d 1164</span><pre class="inline">, 1172 (N.D. Cal. 2022) 
9   (citing cases).  Here, Plaintiff alleges that “[a]t the time of [his] purchases, he did not know the 
10   Products contained high levels of cadmium,” and that he “would not have purchased the Product 
11   had he known the Product contains high levels of cadmium, a substance which is known to be 
12   hazardous to human health.”  Compl. ¶¶ 28, 30.  Plaintiff alleges that as a result, “[he] suffered in 
13   fact when he spent money to purchase the Product he would not have purchased absent 
14   Defendant’s misconduct.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶ 28</span><pre class="inline">.  Plaintiff further alleges that “[h]ad [he] and putative class 
15   members known about the high levels of cadmium, they would not have purchased the Products or 
16   would have paid less for the Products.”  While Defendant frames this allegation as “threadbare,” 
17   Mot. at 15, Plaintiff’s allegation that he “would not have bought the product but for the 
18   misrepresentation . . . is sufficient to allege causation . . . [and] to allege economic injury.”  
19   Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015730"><a href="/opinion/9022504/davidson-v-kimberly-clark-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.">889 F.3d 956</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018) (quoting Kwikset Corp. v. 
20   Superior Court, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/#330" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">51 Cal.4th 310, 330</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011)).                          
21        Defendant’s arguments to the contrary are misplaced.  Defendant maintains that because 
22   Plaintiff “was not promised anything that he did not receive” and was not “exposed to false 
23   information that lured him into buying the product,” he cannot have been injured.  Reply at 7.  But 
24   Plaintiff’s entire suit is premised on the notion that he was promised something he did not receive 
25   (i.e. flaxseed products without high levels of cadmium), and that he was exposed to false 
26   information on the product packaging (i.e. representations that created the net impression that 
27   Products were healthy and did not contain toxic ingredients like cadmium).  Whether Plaintiff’s 
1   negative), but Plaintiff has pled that Defendant made material misrepresentations and omissions 
2   about the product that ultimately caused him to “[pay] more than he . . . actually valued the 
3   product.”  Kwikset Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/#330" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">51 Cal.4th at 330</a></span><pre class="inline">.                          
4        Since this is sufficient for articulating economic injury under our authorities, the Court 
5   DENIES Defendant’s motion to dismiss on this ground.2                 
6              b.  Standing for Products Plaintiff Did Not Purchase       
7        The complaint makes clear that Plaintiff only purchased one of the four Products at issue – 
8   the Organic Whole Ground Flaxseed Meal – on approximately two occasions.  Compl. ¶ 28.  
9   Defendant argues that Plaintiff lacks standing for the Products that he did not personally purchase 
10   (the Organic Golden Flaxseed Meal, Whole Ground Flaxseed Meal, and Golden Flaxseed Meal) 
11   (collectively, the “Unpurchased Products”) because he cannot have been economically injured by 
12   products he did not purchase.  Mot. at 16–17.  The Court again disagrees. 
13        In the Ninth Circuit, “[t]here is no controlling authority on whether [p]laintiffs have 
14   standing for products they did not purchase.”  In re S.C. Johnson &amp; Son, Inc. Windex Non-Toxic 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">15   Litig., 20</span><pre class="inline">-CV-03184-HSG, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 3191733</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (N.D. Cal. July 28, 2021) (citing Miller v. 
16   Ghirardelli Chocolate Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8704589"><a href="/opinion/8721396/miller-v-ghirardelli-chocolate-co/#868" aria-description="Citation for case: Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co.">912 F. Supp. 2d 861, 868</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2012)).  “The majority of the 
17   courts that have carefully analyzed the question hold that a plaintiff may have standing to assert 
18   claims for unnamed class members based on products he or she did not purchase so long as the 
19   products and alleged misrepresentations are substantially similar.”  Miller, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8704589"><a href="/opinion/8721396/miller-v-ghirardelli-chocolate-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co.">912 F. Supp. 2d 861</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 
20   869; see also Papasan v. Dometic Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 4865602</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 27, 2017); 
21   Werdebaugh v. Blue Diamond Growers, No. 12-CV-02724-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 WL 5487236</span><pre class="inline">, at *12–13 
22   (N.D. Cal. Oct. 2, 2013).  If the products are sufficiently similar, “any concerns regarding material 
23   differences in the products can be addressed at the class certification stage.”  Anderson v. Jamba 
24   Juice Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8701764"><a href="/opinion/8718571/anderson-v-jamba-juice-co/#1006" aria-description="Citation for case: Anderson v. Jamba Juice Co.">888 F. Supp. 2d 1000, 1006</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2012).  However, “[w]here the alleged

6              a.  Breach of Implied Warranty                             
7        Defendant contends that Plaintiff fails to state a claim for breath of implied warranties, and 
8   the Court agrees.                                                     
9        “In cases involving human food, a party can plead that a product violates the implied 
10   warranty of merchantability through allegations that the product was unsafe for consumption, 
11   contaminated, or contained foreign objects.”  Barnes v. Nat. Organics, Inc., No. 
12   EDCV22314JGBPLAX, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 4283779</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (C.D. Cal. Sept. 13, 2022) (citing Thomas v.

14        For the reasons previously stated, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not plausibly pled that 
15   the Products are unsafe for consumption because Plaintiff fails to allege that the level of cadmium 
16   allegedly present in the Products constitute a level at which the complained-of health risks occur.  
17   Plaintiff’s repeated incantation of “high,” “unsafe,” and “unlawful” levels of cadmium is not a 
18   sufficient substitute for factual allegations tying the level of cadmium in the Products to the 
19   supposed negative health risks and outcomes flowing from elevated exposure.  The Court 
20   consequently cannot conclude that Plaintiff has pled that the Products were “unsafe for 
21   consumption.”                                                         
22        Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s breach of 
23   implied warranty claim.

3        Finally, Plaintiff alleges various violations under the unlawful prong of the UCL.  Compl. 
4   ¶ 71.  Specifically, he alleges violations of California Civil Code sections 1709, 1711, 1770(a)(5),

6   Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986 (“Proposition 65”), Cal. Health &amp; Safety 
7   Code § 25249.5, et seq), and common law breach of implied warranties.  Id.  None of these 
8   violations are actionable as pled.  First, Plaintiff makes no allegations specific to California Civil 
9   Code sections 1573, 1709, and 1711, or California Business &amp; Professions Code section 17500 et 
10   seq.  Plaintiff identifies these provisions for the first and only time Paragraph 71, when he 
11   summarily lists the predicate violations without alleging their elements or how Defendant’s 
12   conduct meets them.  As a result, the Court finds these violations inadequately pled.  Second, 
13   Plaintiff cannot rely on his warranty claim or CLRA claim as predicate violations of the UCL’s 
14   unlawful prong given that he has not pled sufficient facts to support those claims.  See Stewart v. 
15   Kodiak Cakes, LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">537 F. Supp. 3d 1103</span><pre class="inline">, 1151 (S.D. Cal. 2021) (“When a statutory claim fails, a 
16   derivative UCL claim also fails.”) (internal quotations omitted).

18   violating Proposition 65 must fail, since “[a] plaintiff cannot sidestep [Proposition 65’s] [notice]
