<center><h1>RINARD<br>
v.<br>
BICZAK</h1></center>
<center>Docket No. 101702.</center>
<center><p><b>Michigan Court of Appeals.</b></p></center>
<center>Decided June 5, 1989.</center>
<p><i>Landman, Latimer, Clink &amp; Robb</i> (by <i>Steven C. Kohl</i> and <i>Bennett C. Rosene</i>), for plaintiffs.</p>
<p><i>Bremer, Wade, Nelson &amp; Alt</i> (by <i>Michael D. Wade</i> and <i>Phillip J. Nelson</i>), for defendant.</p>
<p>Before: DANHOF, C.J., and HOOD and R.L. OLZARK,<sup>[*]</sup> JJ.</p>
<p>DANHOF, C.J.</p>
<p>Defendant appeals as of right from a Muskegon Circuit Court jury verdict which awarded damages to plaintiffs Gordon and Stephanie Rinard for the costs of raising their daughter Christine's child, which they adopted. We reverse.</p>
<p>In May of 1983, Stephanie Rinard took fifteen-year-old Christine to see defendant because she discovered that Christine was sexually active. Christine was not pregnant at this time. On December 5, 1983, Christine was again taken to defendant who examined her regarding her possible pregnancy. Christine replied negatively when defendant asked her if she had engaged in sexual intercourse since the last time that he saw her. Actually, she had been having sexual relations with her stepbrother. Defendant incorrectly concluded that Christine was not pregnant at this time. On July 9, 1984, Christine gave birth to a normal, healthy baby which Gordon and Stephanie Rinard adopted.</p>
<p>Plaintiffs Gordon, Stephanie and Christine Rinard brought this medical malpractice action against defendant based on his failure to diagnose Christine's pregnancy in December of 1983. Christine's claim was dismissed with prejudice on motion of her own counsel prior to trial. Plaintiffs testified that Christine probably would have had an abortion if defendant had informed them that <span class="star-pagination">*290</span> she was pregnant in December of 1983. The jury awarded damages to Gordon and Stephanie Rinard for the costs of raising the child. The lower court denied defendant's post-trial motions.</p>
<p>We conclude that the child's mother was the only plaintiff who could have properly brought a cause of action based on defendant's failure to diagnose her pregnancy. She could have recovered the costs of pregnancy and birth, and related damages, but not the costs of raising the child. The mother's parents could not maintain this cause of action for the costs of raising their daughter's child, whom they adopted. Nobody, including natural and adoptive parents, can recover the costs of raising a normal, healthy child because those costs are outweighed by the benefits of that child's life.</p>
<p>We resolve the primary issues in this case by addressing three questions: (1) Can a cause of action be maintained in Michigan for failure to diagnose pregnancy? (2) What recovery is available based on this cause of action? and (3) Can the child's grandparents, who are also the adoptive parents in this case, bring this cause of action?</p>
<p>A cause of action can be maintained in Michigan for failure to diagnose pregnancy. In <i>Proffitt v Bartolo,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#46" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">162 Mich App 35, 46-47</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">412 NW2d 232</a></span> (1987), lv den <span class="citation no-link">430 Mich 860</span> (1988), this Court stated that as long as abortion remains an option allowed by law, physicians owe a duty to furnish patients with adequate information for them to be able to decide whether to choose that course of action. <i>Proffitt</i> was a wrongful birth case. The term "wrongful birth" is a shorthand name given to actions brought by the parents of a child born with severe defects against a physician, or other responsible party, who negligently fails to inform them in a timely fashion of the risk that the mother will give birth to such a child, effectively <span class="star-pagination">*291</span> precluding an informed decision as to whether the pregnancy should be avoided or terminated. <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">162 Mich App 40</a></span>. The instant case does not involve a wrongful birth cause of action. However, the claim asserted in this case is more analogous to that cause of action than to other causes of action such as wrongful life or wrongful pregnancy which Michigan courts have addressed. See <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#40" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><i>Proffitt, supra,</i> pp 40-41</a></span>. This Court impliedly recognized a cause of action for failure to diagnose pregnancy in <i>Clapham v Yanga,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 47</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">300 NW2d 727</a></span> (1980), lv gtd <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1981), dis by stipulation <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1982). Our Supreme Court granted leave to appeal in that case and directed the parties to brief the issues of whether the defendant should be liable for damages for failure to diagnose pregnancy and, if so, how the damages should be measured. The appeal was dismissed upon stipulation by the parties.</p>
<p>We turn to the question of what recovery is available based on a cause of action for wrongful failure to diagnose pregnancy. The lower court instructed the jury to determine damages by applying the "benefits rule" which was set forth in <i>Troppi v Scarf,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">31 Mich App 240</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">187 NW2d 511</a></span> (1971), lv den <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/Mich/385/753/">385 Mich 753</a></span> (1971). Michigan courts have applied the benefits rule on numerous occasions. However, for the reasons set forth below, we conclude that the benefits rule should not be used to determine damages in this type of case.</p>
<p>Under the benefits rule, the trier of fact determines the amount of damages to be awarded by evaluating the benefits of having the child according to the circumstances of the case and subtracting the dollar value of those benefits from the dollar, value of the injury to the plaintiff caused by the defendant's tortious conduct. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><i>Troppi, supra,</i> pp 254-257</a></span>. In <i>Troppi,</i> the defendant pharmacist negligently <span class="star-pagination">*292</span> failed to fill a woman's prescription for birth control pills, resulting in the birth of a normal, healthy child. Under <i>Troppi,</i> the costs of raising a normal, healthy, but unwanted, child, is an element of gross damages. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">31 Mich App 261</a></span>. See also <i>Green v Sudakin,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/#547" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">81 Mich App 545, 547</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">265 NW2d 411</a></span> (1978), lv den <span class="citation no-link">403 Mich 855</span> (1978). The <i>Troppi</i> Court noted that, in the great majority of cases, the birth of a healthy child confers so substantial a benefit as to outweigh the expenses of his or her birth and support. However, the Court felt that it could not say, as a matter of law, that a healthy child always confers such an overriding benefit. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><i>Troppi, supra,</i> p 254</a></span>.</p>
<p>Michigan is among the minority of states which allow the recovery of the costs of raising a child as an element of damages, offset by the benefits received by the parents from the parent-child relationship. In a substantially greater number of jurisdictions, courts have denied the recovery of child-rearing costs. See <i>Morris v Sanchez,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#186" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez">746 P2d 184, 186-187</a></span> (Okla, 1987); <i>Cockrum v Baumgartner,</i> 95 Ill 2d 193, 197; 69 Ill Dec 168; <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#387" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner">447 NE2d 385, 387-388</a></span> (1983), cert den <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/US/464/846/">464 US 846</a></span>; <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/S%20Ct/104/149/">104 S Ct 149</a></span>; <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/L%20Ed%202d/78/139/">78 L Ed 2d 139</a></span> (1983), and cases cited therein.</p>
<p>Courts have not allowed the recovery of the costs of raising a normal, healthy child as an element of damages for many reasons. <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#187" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez"><i>Morris, supra,</i> pp 187-188</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#198" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner"><i>Cockrum, supra,</i> pp 198-199</a></span>. We consider the best reason to be that the costs of raising such a child are outweighed by the value of that child's life. In <i>Rohm v Stroud,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2121827"><a href="/opinion/2121827/rohm-v-stroud/#696" aria-description="Citation for case: Rohm v. Stroud">386 Mich 693, 696</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2121827"><a href="/opinion/2121827/rohm-v-stroud/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rohm v. Stroud">194 NW2d 307</a></span> (1972), our Supreme Court stated that the value of a minor child's services to a parent is at least as great as the amount expended by the parent on the child's support, maintenance and education. In that wrongful death case, our Supreme Court further stated that parents <span class="star-pagination">*293</span> are at least entitled to the presumption that a child is worth his keep, and the negligent act which snuffs out their child's life deprives them of services at least equal to the amount of their pecuniary outlay. <span class="citation" data-id="2121827"><a href="/opinion/2121827/rohm-v-stroud/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rohm v. Stroud">386 Mich 697</a></span>.</p>
<p>The instant case does not involve a wrongful death claim. However, allowing the costs of raising a child as an element of damages logically requires the conclusion that the nonexistence of that child would be a benefit. <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#188" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez"><i>Morris, supra,</i> p 188</a></span>. We agree with the reasoning of the Illinois Court of Appeals which stated:</p>
<blockquote>The existence of a normal, healthy life is an esteemed right under our laws, rather than a compensable wrong. [<i>Wilczynski v Goodman,</i> 73 Ill App 3d 51, 62; 29 Ill Dec 216; <span class="citation" data-id="2126504"><a href="/opinion/2126504/wilczynski-v-goodman/#487" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilczynski v. Goodman">391 NE2d 479, 487</a></span> (1979).</blockquote>
<p>In a proper hierarchy of values, the benefit of life should not be outweighed by the expense of supporting it. <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#201" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner"><i>Cockrum, supra,</i> p 201</a></span>. A court "`has no business declaring that among the living are people who never should have been born.'" <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#51" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><i>Proffitt, supra,</i> p 51</a></span>, quoting <i>Smith v Cote,</i> 128 NH 231, 249; <span class="citation" data-id="8050079"><a href="/opinion/8090040/smith-v-cote/#353" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Cote">513 A2d 341, 353</a></span> (1986).</p>
<p>Another reason for not allowing the recovery of child-rearing costs as an element of damages is that, to maximize their recovery under the benefits rule, parents must demonstrate that they did not want their child and that the child is of minimal value to them. Michigan should not allow "`the unseemly spectacle of parents disparaging the "value" of their children or the degree of their affection for them in open court.'" <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#202" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner"><i>Cockrum, supra,</i> p 202</a></span>, quoting <i>Public Health Trust v Brown,</i> 388 So 2d 1084, 1086, n 4 (Fla App, 1980). A related concern is for the child who may learn <span class="star-pagination">*294</span> that his parents did not want him to exist and sued to have the person who made his existence possible provide for his support. <i>Wilbur v Kerr,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="9760215"><a href="/opinion/2367900/wilbur-v-kerr/#242" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilbur v. Kerr">275 Ark 239, 242-244</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9760215"><a href="/opinion/2367900/wilbur-v-kerr/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilbur v. Kerr">628 SW2d 568, 570-571</a></span> (1982).</p>
<p>We do not dispute the fact that child rearing is a costly enterprise. We simply believe that the benefits of raising a normal, healthy child must be conclusively presumed to be greater than the costs of raising that child. As a matter of public policy, parents should not be able to recover the costs of raising a normal, healthy child as an element of damages. We would limit the parents' recovery for a physician's failure to diagnose pregnancy to the costs of pregnancy and birth, and related damages for pain and suffering, medical complications caused by the pregnancy, mental distress, lost wages, and loss of consortium. See <span class="citation" data-id="2126504"><a href="/opinion/2126504/wilczynski-v-goodman/#63" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilczynski v. Goodman"><i>Wilczynski, supra,</i> p 63</a></span>. These damages should not be offset with what the factfinder speculates to be the benefits of a child. The benefits rule should not be used to measure damages in this type of case.</p>
<p>We turn to the question of whether the child's grandparents, who are also the adoptive parents in this case, can bring this cause of action. They should not be able to recover child-rearing costs as an element of damages for the same reasons that the child's parents should not be able to recover those costs. Plaintiffs' reliance on cases which have extended a physician's duty to third parties is misplaced. Regardless of the existence of a special relationship and the foreseeability that the doctor's failure to diagnose the pregnancy would result in someone's having to provide for the child, the grandparents should not be able to recover damages because the benefits that they gain from raising the child outweigh the child-rearing costs. Further, making a defendant doctor liable to people <span class="star-pagination">*295</span> who voluntarily choose to raise a child, based on a determination that their choice was foreseeable, opens the door to claims in future cases by relatives or other people who could argue that they were foreseeable adoptive parents. There are many potential parents who would gladly adopt a normal, healthy child and not claim that they are injured because they must raise that child. Grandparents should not be allowed to adopt a cause of action by adopting their daughter's child.</p>
<p>In <i><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">Clapham, supra,</a></span></i> a panel of this Court set forth the proposition that the only person who would profit from a holding that the grandparents are not entitled to damages would be the defendant doctor who would escape the consequences of his own negligence. <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 60</a></span>. We disagree. The appropriate consequences for a doctor who negligently fails to diagnose a pregnancy resulting in a normal, healthy child are to pay for the costs of pregnancy and birth, and related damages. The <i>Clapham</i> Court set forth the above-described proposition after explaining that guardianship with the grandparents was in the best interests of the child in that case. <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 59</a></span>-60. We recognize that in some cases such as <i>Clapham,</i> which involved an older child and different circumstances, adoption by the grandparents might be in the child's best interests. However, the instant case involves a young child who will grow up in a household where her adoptive parents' children are her natural parents. We question whether this situation is really in the child's best interests.</p>
<p>Our decision that this case must be reversed makes it unnecessary for us to address defendant's remaining claims concerning jury instructions and the admission of evidence.</p>
<p>We recognize that portions of this opinion could be interpreted as being contrary to portions of this <span class="star-pagination">*296</span> Court's opinions in cases such as <i><span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">Proffitt, supra,</a></span></i> and <i>Eisbrenner v Stanley,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">106 Mich App 357</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">308 NW2d 209</a></span> (1981), lv den <span class="citation no-link">414 Mich 875</span> (1982). Both <i>Proffitt and Eisbrenner</i> allowed wrongful birth causes of action for children who were born with severe birth defects. We will continue to allow parents who successfully maintain a wrongful birth cause of action to recover for their extraordinary medical expenses and the extraordinary costs of raising the child, as well as the emotional harm they have suffered. <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#46" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><i>Proffitt, supra,</i> p 46</a></span>. If changes are to be made in this area of the law, then they should be made by the Legislature or our Supreme Court. <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#58" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><i>Proffitt, supra,</i> pp 58-59</a></span>. We question whether the benefits rule should ever be used when it requires that a value be placed on human life.<sup>[1]</sup> We invite our Supreme Court to grant leave to resolve the issues raised in this case.</p>
<p>Reversed.</p>
<p>R.L. OLZARK, J., concurred.</p>
<p>HOOD, J. <i>(dissenting).</i></p>
<p>I agree that a cause of action can be maintained for failure to diagnose Christine's pregnancy. However, I would also find on the facts of this case that the grandparents can recover child-rearing costs and that their damages are not precluded as a matter of law but are to be determined by the trier of fact.</p>
<p>In May, 1983, Mrs. Rinard took Christine, who was then fifteen years old, to see Dr. Biczak. Mrs. Rinard chose Biczak because he had previously conducted Mrs. Rinard's yearly physicals. The visit was prompted when Mrs. Rinard discovered that <span class="star-pagination">*297</span> Christine had been sexually active and feared she might be pregnant. The pregnancy tests performed at that time were negative.</p>
<p>Late in November, Mrs. Rinard again called defendant because Christine had not had a period since September 7, 1983. An appointment was made and Mrs. Rinard took Christine to defendant's office on December 5, 1983. At that examination, Biczak questioned Christine concerning whether she had engaged in sexual intercourse since the last appointment. Christine's response was negative. Actually, Christine had been having sexual relations with her stepbrother, but Christine testified that he had never penetrated her and at the time of the examination she believed that there had to be penetration in order for there to be "sexual intercourse."</p>
<p>Biczak concluded that Christine was not pregnant. He believed she was simply going through a phase of irregular menses which is common for teenagers. No pregnancy tests were taken. Christine was in fact pregnant at that time. Mrs. Rinard brought Christine to see Biczak again in April, 1984. Christine again said that she had not had a menstrual period since September, 1983, and that she had not had sexual intercourse since May, 1983. Biczak diagnosed secondary amenorrhea. He reached this conclusion because Christine was having no menstrual periods as opposed to menstrual irregularity and because he had eliminated pregnancy as a source of Christine's problems. Biczak believed that the abdominal bloating Christine was then experiencing was due to gastrointestinal problems.</p>
<p>Mrs. Rinard then decided to take her daughter to a gynecologist who informed Mrs. Rinard that Christine was pregnant. The child was delivered in July, 1984, by emergency Caesarean section because <span class="star-pagination">*298</span> of a seizure Christine experienced while in labor. The child was born healthy and full term. The Rinards adopted the child in August, 1984.</p>
<p>The complaint alleged that Biczak's negligence in failing to timely diagnose her pregnancy deprived Christine of the opportunity to terminate the unplanned pregnancy. Only the Rinards sought recovery for the expenses incurred for Christine's pregnancy care and for the support and maintenance of the child.</p>
<p>I agree with the majority's holding that a cause of action can be maintained for failure to diagnose Christine's pregnancy. In <i>Clapham v Yanga,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 47</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">300 NW2d 727</a></span> (1980), lv gtd <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1981), dis by stipulation <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1982), this Court impliedly recognized a cause of action for failure to diagnose a pregnancy. The patient was fourteen years old when her mother took her to the doctor for a medical examination. Both the daughter and the grandparents were awarded damages. This Court did not address the viability of the claim, but decided a damages issue with reference to principles generally applicable in medical malpractice actions.</p>
<p>In arguing that plaintiffs' claim must be denied despite <i>Clapham,</i> defendant relies on <i>Rieck v Medical Protective Co of Fort Wayne, Indiana,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">64 Wis 2d 514</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">219 NW2d 242</a></span> (1974). In <i><span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">Rieck</a></span>,</i> the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that a complaint alleging that a clinic and an obstetrician failed to determine and timely inform the mother that she was pregnant so that the pregnancy could be terminated did not state a cause of action. The Wisconsin court reasoned that, even where negligence can be established as the cause in fact of certain resulting damages, recovery could be denied on public policy grounds.</p>
<p>Some of the same public policy arguments raised <span class="star-pagination">*299</span> by the <i><span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">Rieck</a></span></i> court were considered by this Court in <i>Troppi v Scarf,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">31 Mich App 240</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">187 NW2d 511</a></span> (1971), in determining that a pharmacist could be liable for negligently failing to supply a prescribed oral contraceptive. The <i>Troppi</i> Court found the birth of a healthy child to be a compensable injury. Since <i>Troppi,</i> the Michigan courts have continued to treat other claims involving unplanned births as malpractice claims. <i>Eisbrenner v Stanley,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">106 Mich App 357</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">308 NW2d 209</a></span> (1981)(physician's failure to diagnose rubella); <i>Stephens v Spiwak,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">61 Mich App 647</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">233 NW2d 124</a></span> (1975)(negligently performed tubal ligation); <i><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">Clapham, supra</a></span></i> (misdiagnosis of pregnancy).</p>
<p>Similarly, the only other court which has directly addressed the wrongful diagnosis claim viewed the case basically as one for malpractice in which the person responsible must respond for the resulting damages. <i>Ziemba v Sternberg,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="5793024"><a href="/opinion/5934790/ziemba-v-sternberg/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ziemba v. Sternberg">45 AD2d 230</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="5793024"><a href="/opinion/5934790/ziemba-v-sternberg/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ziemba v. Sternberg">357 NYS2d 265</a></span> (1974). See also <i>Cockrum v Baumgartner,</i> 95 Ill 2d 193; <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner">447 NE2d 385</a></span> (1983), in which the Illinois Supreme Court addressed only the issue of damages, but treated the claim as one for malpractice.</p>
<p>The Michigan cases have continued to follow <i>Troppi</i> and to view the unplanned pregnancy or birth claim as a question of the physician's duty to ascertain and advise the parent so that available options can be exercised. In the absence of any directive from the Legislature or the Supreme Court that this type of claim should not be allowed, the wrongful diagnosis claim should also be recognized. See <i>Proffitt v Bartolo,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#46" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">162 Mich App 35, 46</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">412 NW2d 232</a></span> (1987).</p>
<p>Having determined that this is a viable cause of action, the next question is whether it can be brought by the grandparents of the child. Defendant would preclude plaintiffs from recovery on <span class="star-pagination">*300</span> the basis that any duty he owed the daughter did not extend to her parents since they were not his patients. He would distinguish <i>Clapham</i> and its award to the grandparents because in <i>Clapham</i> the natural parent was also a plaintiff.</p>
<p>The extension of the physician's duty to third parties has been recognized in limited situations. In <i>Duvall v Goldin,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="9670923"><a href="/opinion/1708803/duvall-v-goldin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Duvall v. Goldin">139 Mich App 342</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9670923"><a href="/opinion/1708803/duvall-v-goldin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Duvall v. Goldin">362 NW2d 275</a></span> (1984), this Court recognized that the plaintiff, injured in an auto accident, could bring a claim against a doctor based on his negligence in treating a patient who caused the accident.</p>
<p>The question is whether the facts establish a relationship between the parties such that the community will impose a legal obligation or duty upon the defendant for the benefit of plaintiffs. <i>Id.,</i> p 347. A defendant's special relationship with a nonparty can form the basis for a cause of action where it was foreseeable that the defendant's conduct would create a risk of harm to the plaintiff and where policy considerations do not preclude recognition of the asserted duty. <i>Id.,</i> p 349.<sup>[1]</sup> See also <i>Shepard v Redford Community Hospital,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2138970"><a href="/opinion/2138970/shepard-v-redford-community-hospital/" aria-description="Citation for case: Shepard v. Redford Community Hospital">151 Mich App 242</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2138970"><a href="/opinion/2138970/shepard-v-redford-community-hospital/" aria-description="Citation for case: Shepard v. Redford Community Hospital">390 NW2d 239</a></span> (1986). <i>Welke v Kuzilla,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="9703718"><a href="/opinion/1976104/welke-v-kuzilla/" aria-description="Citation for case: Welke v. Kuzilla">144 Mich App 245</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9703718"><a href="/opinion/1976104/welke-v-kuzilla/" aria-description="Citation for case: Welke v. Kuzilla">375 NW2d 403</a></span> (1985).</p>
<p>The doctor-patient relationship between Biczak and Christine supplies the special relationship. It was foreseeable that a doctor's failure to diagnose a pregnancy could result in someone's having to provide for the child. The likelihood in this case <span class="star-pagination">*301</span> that the expense would fall on the grandparents is not so rare or unusual an occurrence as to be considered unforeseeable. <span class="citation" data-id="9670923"><a href="/opinion/1708803/duvall-v-goldin/#352" aria-description="Citation for case: Duvall v. Goldin"><i>Duvall, supra,</i> p 352</a></span>. Having found the harm to plaintiffs foreseeable, I find no policy reason which would preclude recovery. The only person who would profit from a holding that the grandparents are not entitled to damages would be the defendant doctor who would be allowed to escape the consequences of his own negligence. <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/#60" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga"><i>Clapham, supra,</i> p 60</a></span>. As noted in <i><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">Clapham, supra,</a></span></i> p 61:</p>
<blockquote>The only thing novel about this case is our holding that the grandparents of a minor child's baby may obtain damages where they provide for the infant's care, just as the baby's parents might have. See <i><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi, supra</a></span></i><i>; </i><i>Stephens v Spiwak,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">61 Mich App 647</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2183335"><a href="/opinion/2183335/stephens-v-spiwak/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephens v. Spiwak">233 NW2d 124</a></span> (1975), lv den <span class="citation no-link">395 Mich 761</span> (1975); <i>Green v Sudakin,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">81 Mich App 545</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">265 NW2d 411</a></span> (1978), lv den <span class="citation no-link">403 Mich 855</span> (1978). We believe this is a sound and minor extension of existing precedent. A child's grandparents cannot be treated like total strangers in respect to a claim such as the one presented here.</blockquote>
<p>Nor would I find that somehow Christine's parents are precluded from receiving damages because they voluntarily assumed legal responsibility of the child through adoption. See <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/#58" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga"><i>Clapham, supra,</i> p 58</a></span>. Once the determination is made that liability extends to these plaintiffs in these circumstances, a change in their legal status does not change their right to recover.</p>
<p>The final question is what recovery is available. The majority would bar recovery of damages for the emotional and economic expenses of raising the child and thereby discard the "benefits rule" set forth in <i><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">Troppi, supra</a></span></i><i>. Troppi</i> approached the recovery of expenses arising from an unplanned <span class="star-pagination">*302</span> pregnancy as coming under the general tort rule that the wrongdoer is liable for all injuries directly resulting from the wrongful acts. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#246" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><i>Troppi, supra,</i> pp 246, 252</a></span>. Although recognizing that benefits may naturally flow from the birth of a healthy child, the <i>Troppi</i> Court refused to find as a matter of law that these benefits would always outweigh the unexpected costs associated with raising the unplanned child. By allowing the finder of fact to determine the final award by subtracting the benefits from the assessed damages, the Court sought to provide a rule with sufficient flexibility to allow the trier of fact to evaluate all the circumstances of a case and arrive at a reasonable amount of damages. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><i>Troppi, supra,</i> pp 254-257</a></span>.</p>
<p>Defendant argues that the benefits rule is a minority opinion among the jurisdictions which have considered this type of recovery and that we should now reject it and align ourselves with the majority view which precludes recovery for the costs of raising the child. I would decline to do so.</p>
<p>To a great extent, those courts which have rejected the <i>Troppi</i> approach, see, e.g., <i><span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner">Cockrum, supra</a></span></i><i>; </i><i>Morris v Sanchez,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez">746 P2d 184</a></span> (Okla, 1987), echo the reasoning of <i><span class="citation" data-id="1696546"><a href="/opinion/1696546/rieck-v-medical-protective-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rieck v. Medical Protective Co.">Rieck, supra,</a></span></i> and raise issues similar to those addressed in <i>Troppi</i> when the rule was adopted. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><i>Troppi, supra,</i> pp 254-262</a></span>. In general, those courts which would deny recovery begin with the premise that the parents cannot be damaged by the birth of a normal, healthy baby since human life is so valued that the benefits associated with birth and life will always outweigh any claimed damages.<sup>[2]</sup> Those courts which align with <i>Troppi</i> in general take the view that compensation <span class="star-pagination">*303</span> is sought as a direct result of the physician's negligence and is recoverable in the same manner as other malpractice awards.<sup>[3]</sup></p>
<p>I do not agree that allowing plaintiffs to be compensated for expenditures suffered as a result of the physician's negligence somehow disparages the value of human life or ignores the potential benefits of parenthood.</p>
<p>The injury here is not the child, but the economic reality associated with the unplanned expenses which are the consequences of defendant's negligence. As noted in <i>Jones v Malinowski,</i> 299 Md 257, 270; <span class="citation" data-id="2379505"><a href="/opinion/2379505/jones-v-malinowski/#436" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Malinowski">473 A2d 429, 436</a></span> (1984), a negligent sterilization case:</p>
<blockquote>The parents seek damages, not because they do not love and want to keep the unplanned child, but because the direct, foreseeable and natural consequences of the physician's negligence has forced upon them burdens which they sought and had a right to avoid....</blockquote>
<p>By allowing recovery I would join with the court in <i>Ochs v Borrelli,</i> 187 Conn 253, 259; <span class="citation" data-id="2079084"><a href="/opinion/2079084/ochs-v-borrelli/#885" aria-description="Citation for case: Ochs v. Borrelli">445 A2d 883, 885-886</a></span> (1982), and reject the</p>
<blockquote>premise that a recognition of the economic costs of parenthood is necessarily a negative judgment on the child who occasions them. We may take judicial notice of the fact that raising a child from birth to maturity is a costly enterprise, and hence injurious, although it is an experience that abundantly recompenses most parents with intangible rewards. There can be no affront to public policy in our recognition of these costs and no inconsistency in our view that parental pleasure softens but does not eradicate economic reality.</blockquote>
<p><span class="star-pagination">*304</span> The unplanned birth in this case has caused plaintiffs to expend both money and emotional energy to discharge a familial responsibility. See <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#192" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez"><i>Morris, supra,</i> p 192</a></span> (Kauger, J., concurring and dissenting in part). The finder of fact presumably has determined that the Rinards would not otherwise have experienced these expenditures except for the defendant's negligence. In these circumstances, the benefits rule continues to be an appropriate means of determining damages.</p>
<p>Defendant also appeals the trial court's refusal to give four requested nonstandard jury instructions. The requested instructions either concern the use and qualifications of an expert witness, issues more properly the concern of the court than the jury, or they relate to the weight to be given the expert's testimony. The trial court gave the Standard Jury Instructions on credibility of witnesses and professional negligence which adequately and fairly represented the applicable law to the jury. <i>Beadle v Allis,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1894469"><a href="/opinion/1894469/beadle-v-allis/#525" aria-description="Citation for case: Beadle v. Allis">165 Mich App 516, 525-526</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1894469"><a href="/opinion/1894469/beadle-v-allis/" aria-description="Citation for case: Beadle v. Allis">418 NW2d 906</a></span> (1987).</p>
<p>Furthermore, a trial court may properly refuse an instruction where the SJI Committee recommends against an instruction, unless the instruction is necessary to accurately state the law and the matter is not otherwise adequately covered in the instructions. MCR 2.516(D)(3); <i>Siirila v Barrios,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="9659804"><a href="/opinion/1619071/siirila-v-barrios/#595" aria-description="Citation for case: Siirila v. Barrios">398 Mich 576, 595-597</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9659804"><a href="/opinion/1619071/siirila-v-barrios/" aria-description="Citation for case: Siirila v. Barrios">248 NW2d 171</a></span> (1976). The SJI Committee recommends that no instruction on the weighing of expert testimony be given (SJI2d 4.10). I agree with the committee commentary that matters related to weighing expert testimony are best left to the argument of counsel. The requested instructions did not set forth considerations relevant to the jury's deliberation and the court did not abuse its discretion in rejecting them. MCR 2.516(D)(4).</p>
<p><span class="star-pagination">*305</span> Defendant's final argument is that the trial court erred in admitting evidence over his objection. The decision to admit certain evidence rests within the sound discretion of the trial court and that decision will not be set aside absent an abuse of discretion. <i>Hadley v Trio Tool Co,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1655998"><a href="/opinion/1655998/hadley-v-trio-tool-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Trio Tool Co.">143 Mich App 319</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1655998"><a href="/opinion/1655998/hadley-v-trio-tool-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Trio Tool Co.">372 NW2d 537</a></span> (1985). The court allowed testimony regarding an office visit when the patient was six months pregnant as relevant to the claim of malpractice and the defendant's lack of medical knowledge. Testimony regarding the patient's seizures at the time of delivery was admitted as relevant to plaintiffs' claim. Given the nature of the claim in this case, the court did not abuse its discretion in allowing the testimony.</p>
<p>Defendant's post-trial motions for summary disposition, judgment notwithstanding the verdict and a new trial all basically claimed that the Rinards could not have a claim against Biczak because he did not owe them any legal duty. In reviewing the trial court's denial of these motions, this Court views the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and decides if judgment for the nonmoving party is precluded as a matter of law. This Court will not reverse the decision of the trial court absent an abuse of its discretion. <i>Ritchie v Michigan Consolidated Gas Co,</i> <span class="citation" data-id="1966176"><a href="/opinion/1966176/ritchie-v-michigan-consolidated-gas-co/#367" aria-description="Citation for case: Ritchie v. Michigan Consolidated Gas Co.">163 Mich App 358, 367-368</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1966176"><a href="/opinion/1966176/ritchie-v-michigan-consolidated-gas-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ritchie v. Michigan Consolidated Gas Co.">413 NW2d 796</a></span> (1987). Having found that plaintiffs' claims were not precluded as a matter of law, I would affirm the trial court's denial of defendant's motions and affirm the jury verdict for plaintiffs.</p>
