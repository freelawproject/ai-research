    I.   BACKGROUND                                                       
26                                                                         
         Simpson is a California corporation that designs, manufacturers, and sells structural 
27                                                                         
    connectors for use in building construction.  Dkt. No. 1 ¶¶ 2, 9-10.  Simpson assigns individual 
1   alphanumeric product names for each of its products.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 12-13</span><pre class="inline">.  Each product name includes a 
2   “part name” consisting of a letter or combination of letters designating the product line, and a 
3   “model number” consisting of additional numbers and letters appended to the part name to 
4   distinguish between various models of a particular part with different attributes.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  According to 
5   Simpson, it invests considerable time and effort in creating part names and model numbers.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> ¶ 
6   14.  Simpson uses its product names on its website and product packaging, as well as in catalogs, 
7   publications, and other advertising materials, including its Wood Construction Connectors 
8   Catalog.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 16-20, 22</span><pre class="inline">.  The Wood Construction Connectors Catalog contains an alphabetical 
9   product index and various charts specifying various attributes of Simpson’s products, listed by 
10   product names.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 19-20</span><pre class="inline">.  Simpson has registered copyrights in the Wood Construction 
11   Connectors Catalog and its supplements dating between 2000 and 2020.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶ 21</span><pre class="inline">.   
12        MiTek is a Missouri corporation that manufacturers and sells construction products, 
13   including products that compete with Simpson’s.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 3, 24-25</span><pre class="inline">.  Simpson alleges that MiTek’s 
14   products are “knock-offs or close copies” of Simpson’s products that are not equivalent to or 
15   substitutes for Simpson’s products due to the patented nature of some of Simpson’s technology.  
16   </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 24-26</span><pre class="inline">.  According to Simpson, MiTek uses Simpson product names as MiTek’s own 
17   product names and “stock numbers,” or as “reference numbers” or “reference series” on MiTek’s 
18   website and in its mobile phone application, catalogs, labels, and other materials.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 27-49</span><pre class="inline">, Ex. 
19   C.  In particular, Simpson says, MiTek’s 2020 Catalog uses Simpson product names as a basis for 
20   MiTek’s own product names and includes an alphabetical reference index using Simpson product 
21   names as reference numbers.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 42-44</span><pre class="inline">.  Simpson says that MiTek’s use of Simpson products 
22   names in this manner deceives consumers into believing that the companies’ products are 
23   equivalent and interchangeable when they are not, or that MiTek’s products are actually 
24   Simpson’s products.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 50-54</span><pre class="inline">.                                    
25   II.  LEGAL STANDARD                                                   
26        “A motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a 
27   claim upon which relief can be granted ‘tests the legal sufficiency of a claim.’”  Conservation 
1   729, 732 (9th Cir. 2001)).  When determining whether a claim has been stated, the Court accepts 
2   as true all well-pled factual allegations and construes them in the light most favorable to the 
3   plaintiff.  Reese v. BP Exploration (Alaska) Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="219976"><a href="/opinion/219976/reese-v-bp-exploration-alaska-inc/#690" aria-description="Citation for case: Reese v. BP Exploration (Alaska) Inc.">643 F.3d 681, 690</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011).  While a 
4   complaint need not contain detailed factual allegations, it “must contain sufficient factual matter, 
5   accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">6   U.S. 662</span><pre class="inline">, 678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007)).  A claim is 
7   facially plausible when it “allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is 
8   liable for the misconduct alleged.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">                              
9        The Court is not required to “‘assume the truth of legal conclusions merely because they 
10   are cast in the form of factual allegations.’”  Prager Univ. v. Google LLC (“Prager I”), No. 17-
11   CV-06064-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WL 1471939</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 26, 2018) (quoting Fayer v. Vaughn, 
12   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="216101"><a href="/opinion/216101/fayer-v-vaughn/#1064" aria-description="Citation for case: Fayer v. Vaughn">649 F.3d 1061, 1064</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (per curiam)).  Nor does the Court accept allegations that 
13   contradict documents attached to the complaint or incorporated by reference, Gonzalez v. Planned 
14   Parenthood of L.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2685239"><a href="/opinion/2685239/p-victor-gonzalez-v-planned-parenthood-of-la/#1115" aria-description="Citation for case: P. Victor Gonzalez v. Planned Parenthood of La">759 F.3d 1112, 1115</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014), or that rest on “allegations that are 
15   merely conclusory, unwarranted deductions of fact, or unreasonable inferences.”  In re Gilead 
16   Scis. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1400355"><a href="/opinion/1400355/in-re-gilead-sciences-securities-litigation/#1055" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Gilead Sciences Securities Litigation">536 F.3d 1049, 1055</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).               
17        A court generally may not consider any material beyond the pleadings when ruling on a 
18   Rule 12(b)(6) motion.  If matters outside the pleadings are considered, “the motion must be treated 
19   as one for summary judgment under Rule 56.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(d).  However, documents 
20   appended to the complaint, incorporated by reference in the complaint, or which properly are the 
21   subject of judicial notice may be considered along with the complaint when deciding a Rule 
22   12(b)(6) motion.  Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#998" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d 988, 998</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018); see also Hal 
23   Roach Studios, Inc. v. Richard Feiner &amp; Co., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="537184"><a href="/opinion/537184/hal-roach-studios-inc-a-delaware-corporation-v-richard-feiner-and/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hal Roach Studios, Inc., a Delaware Corporation v....">896 F.2d 1542</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1555 n.19 (9th Cir. 1990).  
24   Likewise, a court may consider matters that are “capable of accurate and ready determination by 
25   resort to sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned.”  Roca v. Wells Fargo Bank, 
26   N.A., No. 15-cv-02147-KAW, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2016 WL 368153</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 1, 2016) (quoting Fed. R. 
27   Evid. 201(b)).                                                        
    III.  DISCUSSION                                                      
1                                                                         
         A.   Federal Claims                                              
2                                                                         
              1.   False advertising (Lanham Act, </pre><span class="citation no-link">15 U.S.C. § 1125</span><pre class="inline">(a)(1)(B)) 
3                                                                         
         To establish a claim for false advertising under </pre><span class="citation no-link">15 U.S.C. § 1125</span><pre class="inline">(a)(1)(B), a plaintiff must 
4                                                                         
    plausibly allege that the defendant made a false or misleading representation of fact in commercial 
5                                                                         
    advertising or promotion about the defendant’s own or the plaintiff’s goods, services, or 
6                                                                         
    commercial activities.  Prager Univ. v. Google LLC (“Prager III”), </pre><span class="citation no-link">951 F.3d 991</span><pre class="inline">, 999 (9th Cir. 
7                                                                         
    2020) (citing Southland Sod Farms v. Stover Seed Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6945094"><a href="/opinion/7041988/southland-sod-farms-v-stover-seed-co/#1139" aria-description="Citation for case: Southland Sod Farms v. Stover Seed Co.">108 F.3d 1134, 1139</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1997)).  The 
8                                                                         
    Ninth Circuit has explained that “commercial advertising or promotion” within the meaning of 
9                                                                         
    § 1125(a)(1)(B) requires the following:                               
10                                                                         
              (1) commercial speech; (2) by a defendant who is in commercial 
11             competition with plaintiff; (3) for the purpose of influencing 
              consumers to buy defendant’s goods or services.  While the  
12             representations need not be made in a “classic advertising  
              campaign,” but may consist instead of more informal types of 
13             “promotion,” the representations (4) must be disseminated   
              sufficiently to the relevant purchasing public to constitute 
14             “advertising” or “promotion” within that industry.          
15   Coastal Abstract Serv. Inc. v. First Am. Title Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="763256"><a href="/opinion/763256/coastal-abstract-service-inc-v-first-american-title-insurance-company/#735" aria-description="Citation for case: Coastal Abstract Service, Inc. v. First American Title...">173 F.3d 725, 735</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1999).   
16        MiTek argues that Simpson has not pled an actionable statement, i.e., one that is capable of 
17   being proven true or false.  Dkt. No. 23 at 3–4.  MiTek says that a product name cannot be proven 
18   true or false, and that Simpson has not pled sufficient facts explaining how MiTek’s use of 
19   Simpson’s alphabetical index in MiTek’s reference index is a false advertisement or statement.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="763256"><a href="/opinion/763256/coastal-abstract-service-inc-v-first-american-title-insurance-company/" aria-description="Citation for case: Coastal Abstract Service, Inc. v. First American Title...">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  
20   Simpson responds that false advertising extends to statements that are impliedly false, and that 
21   MiTek’s use of Simpson’s product names falsely implies that MiTek’s products are the same as or 
22   equivalent to Simpson’s.  Dkt. No. 31 at 5–9.                         
23        “[A] false advertising cause of action under the [Lanham] Act is not limited to literal 
24   falsehoods; it extends to false representations made by implication or innuendo.”  Cook, Perkiss &amp; 
25   Liehe, Inc. v. N. Cal. Collection Serv., Inc., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.2d/911/242/">911 F.2d 242</a></span><pre class="inline">, 245 (9th Cir. 1990).  A false advertising 
26   claim based on implied statements “must be both specific and communicated as to deceive[] a 
27   significant portion of the recipients.”  Prager III, 951 F.3d at 1000 (quoting William H. Morris Co. 
1   Simpson does not plead that the false statements at issue are merely its product names.  Rather, 
2   Simpson pleads that it is MiTek’s use of Simpson’s product names as reference numbers situated 
3   next to MiTek’s own similar product names that creates the false impression that the parties’ 
4   products are equivalent or otherwise interchangeable.  Dkt. No. 1 ¶¶ 25, 29-54.  Simpson supports 
5   its allegations with cites to and images from MiTek’s website, catalogs, publications, packaging, 
6   and marketing materials showing the context in which MiTek has used Simpson product names as 
7   reference numbers.  Id.  These are sufficient facts to support a claim of implied false advertising. 
8        MiTek asserts that Simpson is required to plead express statements that creating the 
9   misleading context in addition to the implied falsehoods.  Dkt. No. 33 at 1–2.  But that is what 
10   Simpson has effectively done here: Simpson has pled that the placement of MiTek’s product 
11   numbers next to Simpson’s product numbers creates an overall false statement of equivalence.  
12   Accordingly, the Court denies MiTek’s motion to dismiss Simpson’s false advertising claim under 
13   the Lanham Act.                                                       
14             2.   Passing off (Lanham Act, </pre><span class="citation no-link">15 U.S.C. § 1125</span><pre class="inline">(a)(1)(A))    
15        MiTek contends that Simpson has failed to state a claim for passing off under the Lanham 
16   Act because Simpson has not pled that its product names possess the necessary inherent 
17   distinctiveness or acquired distinctiveness through secondary meaning required for protection 
18   under the Lanham Act.  Dkt. No. 23 at 7–9.  In fact, MiTek says, the complaint indicates that 
19   Simpson’s product names have specific descriptive and functional meanings and perhaps have 
20   even reached generic status, removing them from Lanham Act protection altogether.  Simpson 
21   responds that pleading distinctiveness is required only for trademark infringement claims, whereas 
22   the Lanham Act protects even generic marks from “false designation of origin.”  Dkt. No. 31 at 
23   14–15.                                                                
24        To establish a claim for false designation of origin under § 1125(a)(1)(A), a plaintiff must 
25   show that the defendant (1) used in commerce (2) any word, false designation of origin, false or 
26   misleading description, or representation of fact, which (3) is likely to cause confusion or mistake, 
27   or to deceive, as to sponsorship, affiliation, or the origin of the goods or services in question.  
1   Network, Inc. v. Oey, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1384069"><a href="/opinion/1384069/freecycle-network-inc-v-oey/" aria-description="Citation for case: Freecycle Network, Inc. v. Oey">505 F.3d 898</a></span><pre class="inline">, 902–04 (9th Cir. 2007); Int’l Order of Job’s Daughters v. 
2   Lindeburg &amp; Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="383718"><a href="/opinion/383718/international-order-of-jobs-daughters-v-lindeburg-and-company/#917" aria-description="Citation for case: International Order of Job&#x27;s Daughters v. Lindeburg and...">633 F.2d 912, 917</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1980)).  The Ninth Circuit recognizes that 
3   misdesignation of origin, or “passing off,” constitutes a violation of Section 43(a) of the Lanham 
4   Act.  OTR Wheel Eng’g, Inc. v. W. Worldwide Servs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4297031"><a href="/opinion/4519778/otr-wheel-engineering-v-west-worldwide-services/#1016" aria-description="Citation for case: Otr Wheel Engineering v. West Worldwide Services">897 F.3d 1008, 1016</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018); 
5   Zito v. Steeplechase Films, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2574191"><a href="/opinion/2574191/zito-v-steeplechase-films-inc/#1028" aria-description="Citation for case: Zito v. Steeplechase Films, Inc.">267 F. Supp. 2d 1022, 1028</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2003) (citing Lamothe v. 
6   Atl. Recording Corp., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.2d/847/1403/">847 F.2d 1403</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1406 (9th Cir. 1988)).  Passing off involves selling a good or 
7   service of one person’s creation as that of another.  Zito, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2574191"><a href="/opinion/2574191/zito-v-steeplechase-films-inc/#1028" aria-description="Citation for case: Zito v. Steeplechase Films, Inc.">267 F. Supp. 2d at 1028</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Passing off may 
8   be either express or implied.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2574191"><a href="/opinion/2574191/zito-v-steeplechase-films-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Zito v. Steeplechase Films, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Express passing off occurs when a business labels its goods or 
9   services with a mark identical to that of another enterprise, whereas implied passing off involves, 
10   for example, the use of a competitor’s advertising material or a sample or photograph of the 
11   competitor’s product to impliedly represent that the product being sold is made by the competitor.  
12   Id.; see also Dastar Corp. v. Twentieth Century Fox Film. Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="130141"><a href="/opinion/130141/dastar-corp-v-twentieth-century-fox-film-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dastar Corp. v. Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp.">539 U.S. 23</a></span><pre class="inline">, 27 n.1 (2003) 
13   (“Passing off (or palming off, as it is sometimes called) occurs when a producer misrepresents his 
14   own goods or services as someone else’s.  ‘Reverse passing off,’ as its name implies, is the 
15   opposite: The producer misrepresents someone else’s goods or services as his own.”) (internal 
16   quotation marks and citations omitted).                               
17        Even a generic mark may serve as the basis for a claim for passing off under 
18   § 1125(a)(1)(A).  Blinded Veterans Ass’n v. Blinded Ams. Veterans Found., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8969607"><a href="/opinion/8977849/blinded-veterans-assn-v-blinded-american-veterans-foundation/" aria-description="Citation for case: Blinded Veterans Ass&#x27;n v. Blinded American Veterans...">872 F.2d 1035</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1042–
19   48 (D.C. Cir. 1989) (holding that plaintiff’s name was not a protectable trademark because the 
20   term “blinded veterans” is generic, but remanding for consideration of a passing off claim); see 
21   also Nissan Motor Co., Ltd. v. Nissan Computer Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8732874"><a href="/opinion/8749605/nissan-motor-co-v-nissan-computer-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Nissan Motor Co. v. Nissan Computer Corp.">204 F.R.D. 460</a></span><pre class="inline">, 467 n.11 (C.D. Cal. 2001) 
22   (describing defendant’s argument that a generic term cannot serve as the basis for a passing off 
23   claim as “misguided” and citing Blinded Veterans).  Here, Simpson alleges that (1) MiTek’s use of 
24   Simpson product names as MiTek’s own product names falsely identifies MiTek products as 
25   Simpson products, and (2) MiTek’s use of Simpson’s product names as reference numbers for 
26   MiTek’s own products in MiTek sales and marketing literature creates a false impression that 
27   MiTek’s products are in some way connected to or associated with Simpson.  Dkt. No. 1 ¶¶ 25, 
1   misdesignation of origin, or passing off, under the Lanham Act.       
2             3.   Copyright infringement                                 
3        To withstand a motion to dismiss on a claim of direct copyright infringement, the plaintiff 
4   must (1) “show ownership of the allegedly infringed material” and (2) “demonstrate that the 
5   alleged infringer violates at least one exclusive right granted to copyright holders under 17 U.S.C. 
6   § 106.”  Disney Enters., Inc. v. VidAngel, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4198721"><a href="/opinion/4421468/disney-enterprises-inc-v-vidangel-inc/#856" aria-description="Citation for case: Disney Enterprises, Inc. v. Vidangel, Inc.">869 F.3d 848, 856</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2017) (citing Perfect 
7   10, Inc. v. Amazon.com, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1228127"><a href="/opinion/1228127/perfect-10-inc-v-amazon-com-inc/#1159" aria-description="Citation for case: Perfect 10, Inc. v. Amazon. Com, Inc.">508 F.3d 1146, 1159</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2007)).  A copyright holder has the 
8   exclusive rights to reproduce, distribute, publicly display, perform, and create derivative works of 
9   the copyrighted work.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">17 U.S.C. § 106</span><pre class="inline">.                               
10        The parties do not dispute that Simpson owns valid copyrights in its Wood Construction 
11   Connectors Catalog.  However, MiTek contends that the portions of the catalog that Simpson 
12   alleges MiTek has infringed—namely, Simpson product names and the alphabetical index of 
13   Simpson products—are not protectable works.  Dkt. No. 23 at 9–12.  In particular, MiTek argues 
14   that copyright protection does not extend to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of 
15   operation, concept, principle, or discovery, and that words and short phrases—including names—
16   and the mere listing of ingredients or contents are not protected.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> at 10 (quoting </pre><span class="citation no-link">17 U.S.C. § 
17</span><pre class="inline">   102(b); </pre><span class="citation no-link">37 C.F.R. § 202.1</span><pre class="inline">(a)).  Furthermore, the mere arrangement of facts in alphabetical order is 
18   not protected.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> at 11–12 (citing Feist Publ’ns, Inc. v. Rural Tel. Serv. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="112568"><a href="/opinion/112568/feist-publications-inc-v-rural-telephone-service-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.">499 U.S. 340</a></span><pre class="inline">, 348 
19   (1991)).                                                              
20        In response, Simpson argues that the Ninth Circuit has stated that originality is a question 
21   of fact that cannot be decided on a motion to dismiss.  Dkt. No.31 at 17–18 (citing Dezendorf v. 
22   Twentieth Century-Fox Film Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1544353"><a href="/opinion/1544353/dezendorf-v-twentieth-century-fox-film-corporation/#851" aria-description="Citation for case: Dezendorf v. Twentieth Century-Fox Film Corporation">99 F.2d 850, 851</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1938)).  However, the Ninth Circuit 
23   has also held that “when the copyrighted work and the alleged infringement are both before the 
24   court, capable of examination and comparison, non-infringement can be determined on a motion 
25   to dismiss.”  Christianson v. West Pub’lg Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3010728"><a href="/opinion/3010728/pennzoil-products-co-v-colelli-associates-inc/#203" aria-description="Citation for case: Pennzoil Products Co. v. Colelli &amp; Associates, Inc.">149 F.3d 202, 203</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1945); see also 
26   Rentmeester v. Nike, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4248559"><a href="/opinion/4471306/jacobus-rentmeester-v-nike-inc/#1118" aria-description="Citation for case: Jacobus Rentmeester v. Nike, Inc.">883 F.3d 1111, 1118, 1125</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018) (affirming district court’s 
27   dismissal of copyright suit where plaintiff’s original work and defendant’s allegedly infringing 
1   the two works, it must “filter out” unprotectable elements of the plaintiff’s work), overruled on 
2   other grounds, Skidmore as Trustee for Randy Craig Wolfe Trust v. Led Zeppelin, </pre><span class="citation no-link">952 F.3d 1051</span><pre class="inline"> 
3   (9th Cir. 2020).                                                      
4        The Court is unable to find any case law reconciling Dezendorf with Christianson.  Neither 
5   case is particularly helpful here.  The Ninth Circuit has cited Dezendorf only once in recent 
6   decades, but that opinion was amended specifically to delete the portion relying on Dezendorf.  
7   Hall v. Swift, </pre><span class="citation no-link">782 F. App’x 639</span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2019), amended, </pre><span class="citation no-link">786 F. App’x 711</span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2019).  
8   Christianson does not directly address the question of whether a court may determine originality 
9   at the pleading stage.  Rather, that case concerned the question of substantial similarity on a 
10   motion to dismiss.  Justice Wardlaw recently noted in a concurring opinion that Christianson and 
11   Rentmeester are outliers as dismissals for lack of substantial similarity on a motion to dismiss.  
12   Astor-White v. Strong, </pre><span class="citation no-link">733 F. App’x 407</span><pre class="inline">, 408–09, n.2 (9th Cir. 2018) (Wardlaw, J. concurring). 
13        In any event, neither Dezendorf nor Christianson stands for the proposition that the Court 
14   must resolve the question of whether Simpson’s product names and alphabetical index of product 
15   names are sufficiently original to warrant copyright protection at the pleading stage.  While the 
16   Court agrees that MiTek raises serious questions about the protectability of Simpson’s product 
17   names and alphabetical index, the Court would benefit from a fuller record before deciding the 
18   issue.  The Court is not prepared to say at this time that Simpson’s names and index are not 
19   sufficiently original as a matter of law.                             
20        Accordingly, the Court denies the motion to dismiss Simpson’s copyright claim. 
21        B.   State Law Claims                                            
22             1.   Unfair competition (</pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200</span><pre class="inline">)    
23        California’s Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”) “prohibits any ‘fraudulent business act or 
24   practice.’”  Williams v. Gerber Prods. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d 934, 938</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008) (quoting Cal. Bus. &amp; 
25   Prof. Code § 17200).  MiTek argues that Simpson has failed to state a UCL claim for multiple 
26   reasons.  First, MiTek argues that Simpson has not alleged an actionable statement.  Dkt. No. 23 at 
27   3–4.  This is the same argument MiTek makes concerning Simpson’s Lanham Act false 
1   III.A.1.                                                              
2        Second, MiTek contends that Simpson has not adequately pled standing to pursue a UCL 
3   claim because it does not allege reliance on MiTek’s misrepresentations and resulting injury.  Dkt. 
4   No. 23 at 6–7.  Instead, MiTek says, the complaint only alleges consumers’ reliance, whereas 
5   Simpson must plead its own reliance.  Id.  Additionally, MiTek contends that Simpson must allege 
6   an economic injury in that it had prior possession or legal vested interest in the claimed lost money 
7   or property.  Dkt. No. 33 at 3–4.                                     
8        “[U]nder the UCL, standing extends to ‘a person who has suffered injury in fact and has 
9   lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition’ ([Cal. Civ. Code] § 17204), while 
10   under the false advertising law, in materially identical language, standing extends to ‘any person 
11   who has suffered injury in fact and has lost money or property as a result of a violation of this 
12   chapter’ ([Cal. Civ. Code] § 17535).”  Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">51 Cal. 4th 310</a></span><pre class="inline">, 321–22 
13   (2011).  To satisfy the standing requirement, a party must “(1) establish a loss or deprivation of 
14   money or property sufficient to qualify as injury in fact, i.e., economic injury, and (2) show that 
15   that economic injury was the result of, i.e., caused by, the unfair business practice or false 
16   advertising that is the gravamen of the claim.”  Id. at 885 (emphases original).  District courts 
17   within the Ninth Circuit are split on the question of whether a competitor plaintiff must plead its 
18   own reliance on a defendant’s allegedly misleading statement.  Lona’s Lil Eats, LLC v. DoorDash, 
19   Inc., No. 20-cv-06703-TSH, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 151978</span><pre class="inline">, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 18, 2021).  MiTek notes 
20   correctly that the majority of district courts have required a plaintiff to plead its own reliance.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  
21   However, the Court finds the views and analysis described in Lona’s Lil Eats more persuasive 
22   given the nature of Simpson’s allegations.                            
23        MiTek principally relies on Kwikset and In re Tobacco II Cases, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9685293"><a href="/opinion/1801796/in-re-tobacco-ii-cases/" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Tobacco II Cases">46 Cal. 4th 298</a></span><pre class="inline">, 314 
24   (2009), but those cases involved false advertising claims by consumer plaintiffs, not a competitor 
25   plaintiff like Simpson.  Lona’s Lil Eats, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 151978</span><pre class="inline">, at *11.  Additionally, the Kwikset court 
26   acknowledged that reliance is not the only way to plead causation; rather, a plaintiff may plead a 
27   causal connection between the acts complained of and the plaintiff’s injury.  Kwikset, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2368488"><a href="/opinion/2368488/dana-point-safe-harbor-collective-v-superior-court/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dana Point Safe Harbor Collective v. Superior Court">51 Cal. 4th 
1</a></span><pre class="inline">   competition or a violation of the false advertising law.  (§§ 17204, 17535.)  The phrase ‘as a result 
2   of’ in its plain and ordinary sense means ‘caused by’ and requires a showing of a causal 
3   connection or reliance on the alleged misrepresentation.”) (emphasis added); see also Lona’s Lil 
4   Eats, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 151978</span><pre class="inline">, at *11.  As MiTek acknowledged at the hearing, strict adherence to the 
5   rule requiring pleading reliance would create an absurd result where a plaintiff could never bring a 
6   UCL claim against its competitor for false or misleading advertising.  Dkt. No. 42 at 8:15-22 
7   (“THE COURT: So how do we answer the question that was posed by Judge Hixson, which is . . . 
8   under what circumstances would a competitor ever be able to assert a claim under the FAL or the 
9   UCL when there is an alleged misrepresentation?  MS. SCHEIPETER: Well, and I’m not sure 
10   under the existing case law that they could.”).  Such a result would defeat the very purpose of the 
11   UCL, which is “to protect both consumers and competitors” alike.  Kwikset, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/#883" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">51 Cal. 4th at 883</a></span><pre class="inline">.   
12        With respect to MiTek’s argument that Simpson must allege an economic injury in that it 
13   had prior possession or legal vested interest in the claimed lost money or property in order to plead 
14   standing under the UCL, the Court does not agree.  Simpson does not seek to recover damages 
15   under the UCL and is thus not required to plead such possession or interest.  Finelite, Inc. v. 
16   Ledalite Architect. Prods., C-10-1276 MMC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2010 WL 3385027</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 26, 2010).  
17   Here, Simpson alleges that because of MiTek’s misleading use of Simpson product names, 
18   Simpson lost sales.  Dkt. No. 1 ¶¶ 50-54, 61.  While Simpson could have provided more detail 
19   concerning the lost sales, the Court finds that it has adequately pled standing under the UCL. 
20        Finally, MiTek argues that Simpson has not adequately pled a claim under the 
21   “fraudulent,” “unfair,” or “unlawful” prongs of the UCL statute because the complaint does not 
22   specify which theory or theories of unfair competition Simpson asserts.  Dkt. No. 23 at 5–6.  
23   Simpson responds that its UCL claim incorporates and relies on allegations regarding false 
24   advertising and passing off under both the Lanham Act and FAL, and therefore it states a claim 
25   under all three prongs.  Dkt. No. 31 at 9–11.                         
26        Section 17200’s unlawful prong “borrows violations of other laws and treats them as 
27   unlawful practices that the unfair competition law makes independently actionable.”  Davis v. 
1   claim under the unlawful prong of the UCL, a plaintiff must sufficiently plead a predicate 
2   violation.”  MacDonald v. Ford Motor Co., 37 F. Supp. 3d. 1087, 1097 (N.D. Cal. 2014).  The 
3   “unfair” prong of the UCL “prohibit[s] not only advertising which is false, but also advertising 
4   which[,] although is true, is either actually misleading or which has a capacity, likelihood or 
5   tendency to deceive or confuse the public.”  Brady v. Bayer Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6106822"><a href="/opinion/6239892/brady-v-bayer-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brady v. Bayer Corp.">26 Cal. App. 5th 1156</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1173 
6   (2018) (citation omitted).  Alleged violations of the Lanham Act and the FAL can serve as a 
7   predicate basis for a claim of unlawful and unfair practices under the UCL.  Lona’s Lil Eats, 2021 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">8   WL 151978</span><pre class="inline">, at *13; Kasky v. Nike, Inc., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/Cal.%204th/27/939/">27 Cal. 4th 939</a></span><pre class="inline">, 950 (2002).  Moreover, a violation of the 
9   FAL generally is also a violation of the “fraudulent” prong of the UCL.  Hadley v. Kellogg Sales 
10   Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1089" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1089</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017).  Because Simpson has stated claims for false 
11   advertising under the Lanham Act and the FAL, it has also stated a UCL claim under all three 
12   prongs.                                                               
13          Accordingly, the Court denies the motion to dismiss the UCL claim. 
14             2.   False advertising (</pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17500</span><pre class="inline">)     
15        California’s False Advertising Law (“FAL”) “prohibits any ‘unfair, deceptive, untrue, or 
16   misleading advertising.’”  Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d at 938</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17500</span><pre class="inline">).  
17   MiTek argues that Simpson has failed to state a FAL claim for two reasons.  First, MiTek argues 
18   that Simpson has not alleged an actionable statement.  Dkt. No. 23 at 3–4.  This is the same 
19   argument MiTek made concerning Simpson’s Lanham Act false advertisement claim, and it is not 
20   persuasive for the reasons stated above.  See supra Section III.A.1.   
21        Second, MiTek raises the same argument concerning standing as it did for Simpson’s UCL 
22   claim.  Dkt. No. 23 at 6–7.  For the same reasons explained above, the Court finds that Simpson 
23   has adequately pled standing for its FAL claim.  See supra Section III.B.1; Lona’s Lil Eats, 2021 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">24   WL 151978</span><pre class="inline">, at *10–12.                                                 
25        Accordingly, the Court denies the motion to dismiss the FAL claim.
