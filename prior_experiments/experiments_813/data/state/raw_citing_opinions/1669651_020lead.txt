<opinion type="majority">
<author id="b301-8">Danhof, C.J.</author>
<p id="AeI">Defendant appeals as of right from a Muskegon Circuit Court jury verdict which awarded damages to plaintiffs Gordon and Stephanie Rinard for the costs of raising their daughter Christine’s child, which they adopted. We reverse.</p>
<p id="b301-9">In May of 1983, Stephanie Rinard took fifteen-year-old Christine to see defendant because she discovered that Christine was sexually active. Christine was not pregnant at this time. On December 5, 1983, Christine was again taken to defendant who examined her regarding her possible pregnancy. Christine replied negatively when defendant asked her if she had engaged in sexual intercourse since the last time that he saw her. Actually, she had been having sexual relations with her stepbrother. Defendant incorrectly concluded that Christine was not pregnant at this time. On July 9, 1984, Christine gave birth to a normal, healthy baby which Gordon and Stephanie Rinard adopted.</p>
<p id="b301-10">Plaintiffs Gordon, Stephanie and Christine Rinard brought this medical malpractice action against defendant based on his failure to diagnose Christine’s pregnancy in December of 1983. Christine’s claim was dismissed with prejudice on motion of her own counsel prior to trial. Plaintiffs testified that Christine probably would have had an abortion if defendant had informed them that <page-number citation-index="1" label="290">*290</page-number>she was pregnant in December of 1983. The jury awarded damages to Gordon and Stephanie Rinard for the costs of raising the child. The lower court denied defendant’s post-trial motions.</p>
<p id="b302-6">We conclude that the child’s mother was the only plaintiff who could have properly brought a cause of action based on defendant’s failure to diagnose her pregnancy. She could have recovered the costs of pregnancy and birth, and related damages, but not the costs of raising the child. The mother’s parents could not maintain this cause of action for the costs of raising their daughter’s child, whom they adopted. Nobody, including natural and adoptive parents, can recover the costs of raising a normal, healthy child because those costs are outweighed by the benefits of that child’s life.</p>
<p id="b302-7">We resolve the primary issues in this case by addressing three questions: (1) Can a cause of action be maintained in Michigan for failure to diagnose pregnancy? (2) What recovery is available based on this cause of action? and (3) Can the child’s grandparents, who are also the adoptive parents in this case, bring this cause of action?</p>
<p id="b302-8">A cause of action can be maintained in Michigan for failure to diagnose pregnancy. In <em>Proffitt v Bartolo, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#46" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">162 Mich App 35, 46-47</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">412 NW2d 232</a></span> (1987), lv den <span class="citation no-link">430 Mich 860</span> (1988), this Court stated that as long as abortion remains an option allowed by law, physicians owe a duty to furnish patients with adequate information for them to be able to decide whether to choose that course of action. <em>Proffitt </em>was a wrongful birth case. The term "wrongful birth” is a shorthand name given to actions brought by the parents of a child born with severe defects against a physician, or other responsible party, who negligently fails to inform them in a timely fashion of the risk that the mother will give birth to such a child, effectively <page-number citation-index="1" label="291">*291</page-number>precluding an informed decision as to whether the pregnancy should be avoided or terminated. <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">162 Mich App 40</a></span>. The instant case does not involve a wrongful birth cause of action. However, the claim asserted in this case is more analogous to that cause of action than to other causes of action such as wrongful life or wrongful pregnancy which Michigan courts have addressed. See <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#40" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><em>Proffitt, supra, </em>pp 40-41</a></span>. This Court impliedly recognized a cause of action for failure to diagnose pregnancy in <em>Clapham v Yanga, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 47</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">300 NW2d 727</a></span> (1980), lv gtd <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1981), dis by stipulation <span class="citation no-link">412 Mich 889</span> (1982). Our Supreme Court granted leave to appeal in that case and directed the parties to brief the issues of whether the defendant should be liable for damages for failure to diagnose pregnancy and, if so, how the damages should be measured. The appeal was dismissed upon stipulation by the parties.</p>
<p id="b303-6">We turn to the question of what recovery is available based on a cause of action for wrongful failure to diagnose pregnancy. The lower court instructed the jury to determine damages by applying the "benefits rule” which was set forth in <em>Troppi v Scarf, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">31 Mich App 240</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">187 NW2d 511</a></span> (1971), lv den <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/Mich/385/753/">385 Mich 753</a></span> (1971). Michigan courts have applied the benefits rule on numerous occasions. However, for the reasons set forth below, we conclude that the benefits rule should not be used to determine damages in this type of case.</p>
<p id="b303-7">Under the benefits rule, the trier of fact determines the amount of damages to be awarded by evaluating the benefits of having the child according to the circumstances of the case and subtracting the dollar value of those benefits from the dollar value of the injury to the plaintiff caused by the defendant’s tortious conduct. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><em>Troppi, supra, </em>pp 254-257</a></span>. In <em>Troppi, </em>the defendant pharmacist neg<page-number citation-index="1" label="292">*292</page-number>ligently failed to fill a woman’s prescription for birth control pills, resulting in the birth of a normal, healthy child. Under <em>Troppi, </em>the costs of raising a normal, healthy, but unwanted, child, is an element of gross damages. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf">31 Mich App 261</a></span>. See also <em>Green v Sudakin, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/#547" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">81 Mich App 545, 547</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1720144"><a href="/opinion/1720144/green-v-sudakin/" aria-description="Citation for case: Green v. Sudakin">265 NW2d 411</a></span> (1978), lv den <span class="citation no-link">403 Mich 855</span> (1978). The <em>Troppi </em>Court noted that, in the great majority of cases, the birth of a healthy child confers so substantial a benefit as to outweigh the expenses of his or her birth and support. However, the Court felt that it could not say, as a matter of law, that a healthy child always confers such an overriding benefit. <span class="citation" data-id="2185063"><a href="/opinion/2185063/troppi-v-scarf/#254" aria-description="Citation for case: Troppi v. Scarf"><em>Troppi, supra, </em>p 254</a></span>.</p>
<p id="b304-6">Michigan is among the minority of states which allow the recovery of the costs of raising a child as an element of damages, offset by the benefits received by the parents from the parent-child relationship. In a substantially greater number of jurisdictions, courts have denied the recovery of child-rearing costs. See <em>Morris v Sanchez, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#186" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez">746 P2d 184, 186-187</a></span> (Okla, 1987); <em>Cockrum v Baumgartner, </em>95 Ill 2d 193, 197; 69 Ill Dec 168; <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#387" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner">447 NE2d 385, 387-388</a></span> (1983), cert den <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/US/464/846/">464 US 846</a></span>; <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/S%20Ct/104/149/">104 S Ct 149</a></span>; <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/L%20Ed%202d/78/139/">78 L Ed 2d 139</a></span> (1983), and cases cited therein.</p>
<p id="b304-7">Courts have not allowed the recovery of the costs of raising a normal, healthy child as an element of damages for many reasons. <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#187" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez"><em>Morris, supra, </em>pp 187-188</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#198" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner"><em>Cockrum, supra, </em>pp 198-199</a></span>. We consider the best reason to be that the costs of raising such a child are outweighed by the value of that child’s life. In <em>Rohm v Stroud, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2121827"><a href="/opinion/2121827/rohm-v-stroud/#696" aria-description="Citation for case: Rohm v. Stroud">386 Mich 693, 696</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="2121827"><a href="/opinion/2121827/rohm-v-stroud/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rohm v. Stroud">194 NW2d 307</a></span> (1972), our Supreme Court stated that the value of a minor child’s services to a parent is at least as great as the amount expended by the parent on the child’s support, maintenance and education. In that wrongful death case, our Supreme Court further stated that par<page-number citation-index="1" label="293">*293</page-number>ents are at least entitled to the presumption that a child is worth his keep, and the negligent act which snuffs out their child’s life deprives them of services at least equal to the amount of their pecuniary outlay. <span class="citation" data-id="2121827"><a href="/opinion/2121827/rohm-v-stroud/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rohm v. Stroud">386 Mich 697</a></span>.</p>
<p id="b305-6">The instant case does not involve a wrongful death claim. However, allowing the costs of raising a child as an element of damages logically requires the conclusion that the nonexistence of that child would be a benefit. <span class="citation" data-id="9612672"><a href="/opinion/1384809/morris-v-sanchez/#188" aria-description="Citation for case: Morris v. Sanchez"><em>Morris, supra, </em>p 188</a></span>. We agree with the reasoning of the Illinois Court of Appeals which stated:</p>
<blockquote id="b305-7">The existence of a normal, healthy life is an esteemed right under our laws, rather than a compensable wrong. <em>[Wilczynski v Goodman, </em>73 Ill App 3d 51, 62; 29 Ill Dec 216; <span class="citation" data-id="2126504"><a href="/opinion/2126504/wilczynski-v-goodman/#487" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilczynski v. Goodman">391 NE2d 479, 487</a></span> (1979).</blockquote>
<p id="b305-8">In a proper hierarchy of values, the benefit of life should not be outweighed by the expense of supporting it. <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#201" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner"><em>Cockrum, supra, </em>p 201</a></span>. A court " 'has no business declaring that among the living are people who never should have been born.’ ” <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#51" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><em>Proffitt, supra, </em>p 51</a></span>, quoting <em>Smith v Cote, </em>128 NH 231, 249; <span class="citation" data-id="8050079"><a href="/opinion/8090040/smith-v-cote/#353" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Cote">513 A2d 341, 353</a></span> (1986).</p>
<p id="b305-9">Another reason for not allowing the recovery of child-rearing costs as an element of damages is that, to maximize their recovery under the benefits rule, parents must demonstrate that they did not want their child and that the child is of minimal value to them. Michigan should not allow " 'the unseemly spectacle of parents disparaging the "value” of their children or the degree of their affection for them in open court.’ ” <span class="citation" data-id="9734034"><a href="/opinion/2190432/cockrum-v-baumgartner/#202" aria-description="Citation for case: Cockrum v. Baumgartner"><em>Cockrum, supra, </em>p 202</a></span>, quoting <em>Public Health Trust v Brown, </em>388 So 2d 1084, 1086, n 4 (Fla App, 1980). A related concern is for the child who may learn <page-number citation-index="1" label="294">*294</page-number>that his parents did not want him to exist and sued to have the person who made his existence possible provide for his support. <em>Wilbur v Kerr, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9760215"><a href="/opinion/2367900/wilbur-v-kerr/#242" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilbur v. Kerr">275 Ark 239, 242-244</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="9760215"><a href="/opinion/2367900/wilbur-v-kerr/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilbur v. Kerr">628 SW2d 568, 570-571</a></span> (1982).</p>
<p id="b306-6">We do not dispute the fact that child rearing is a costly enterprise. We simply believe that the benefits of raising a normal, healthy child must be conclusively presumed to be greater than the costs of raising that child. As a matter of public policy, parents should not be able to recover the costs of raising a normal, healthy child as an element of damages. We would limit the parents’ recovery for a physician’s failure to diagnose pregnancy to the costs of pregnancy and birth, and related damages for pain and suffering, medical complications caused by the pregnancy, mental distress, lost wages, and loss of consortium. See <span class="citation" data-id="2126504"><a href="/opinion/2126504/wilczynski-v-goodman/#63" aria-description="Citation for case: Wilczynski v. Goodman"><em>Wilczynski, supra, </em>p 63</a></span>. These damages should not be offset with what the factfinder speculates to be the benefits of a child. The benefits rule should not be used to measure damages in this type of case.</p>
<p id="b306-7">We turn to the question of whether the child’s grandparents, who are also the adoptive parents in this case, can bring this cause of action. They should not be able to recover child-rearing costs as an element of damages for the same reasons that the child’s parents should not be able to recover those costs. Plaintiffs’ reliance on cases which have extended a physician’s duty to third parties is misplaced. Regardless of the existence of a special relationship and the foreseeability that the doctor’s failure to diagnose the pregnancy would result in someone’s having to provide for the child, the grandparents should not be able to recover damages because the benefits that they gain from raising the child outweigh the child-rearing costs. Further, making a defendant doctor liable to peo<page-number citation-index="1" label="295">*295</page-number>pie who voluntarily choose to raise a child, based on a determination that their choice was foreseeable, opens the door to claims in future cases by relatives or other people who could argue that they were foreseeable adoptive parents. There are many potential parents who would gladly adopt a normal, healthy child and not claim that they are injured because they must raise that child. Grandparents should not be allowed to adopt a cause of action by adopting their daughter’s child.</p>
<p id="b307-6">In <em><span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">Clapham, supra,</a></span> </em>a panel of this Court set forth the proposition that the only person who would profit from a holding that the grandparents are not entitled to damages would be the defendant doctor who would escape the consequences of his own negligence. <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 60</a></span>. We disagree. The appropriate consequences for a doctor who negligently fails to diagnose a pregnancy resulting in a normal, healthy child are to pay for the costs of pregnancy and birth, and related damages. The <em>Clapham </em>Court set forth the above-described proposition after explaining that guardianship with the grandparents was in the best interests of the child in that case. <span class="citation" data-id="2055749"><a href="/opinion/2055749/clapham-v-yanga/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clapham v. Yanga">102 Mich App 59</a></span>-60. We recognize that in some cases such as <em>Clapham, </em>which involved an older child and different circumstances, adoption by the grandparents might be in the child’s best interests. However, the instant case involves a young child who will grow up in a household where her adoptive parents’ children are her natural parents. We question whether this situation is really in the child’s best interests.</p>
<p id="b307-7">Our decision that this case must be reversed makes it unnecessary for us to address defendant’s remaining claims concerning jury instructions and the admission of evidence.</p>
<p id="b307-8">We recognize that portions of this opinion could be interpreted as being contrary to portions of this <page-number citation-index="1" label="296">*296</page-number>Court’s opinions in cases such as <em><span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo">Proffitt, supra,</a></span> </em>and <em>Eisbrenner v Stanley, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">106 Mich App 357</a></span>; <span class="citation" data-id="1854010"><a href="/opinion/1854010/eisbrenner-v-stanley/" aria-description="Citation for case: Eisbrenner v. Stanley">308 NW2d 209</a></span> (1981), lv den <span class="citation no-link">414 Mich 875</span> (1982). Both <em>Proffitt and Eisbrenner </em>allowed wrongful birth causes of action for children who were born with severe birth defects. We will continue to allow parents who successfully maintain a wrongful birth cause of action to recover for their extraordinary medical expenses and the extraordinary costs of raising the child, as well as the emotional harm they have suffered. <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#46" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><em>Proffitt, supra, </em>p 46</a></span>. If changes are to be made in this area of the law, then they should be made by the Legislature or our Supreme Court. <span class="citation" data-id="1302718"><a href="/opinion/1302718/proffitt-v-bartolo/#58" aria-description="Citation for case: Proffitt v. Bartolo"><em>Proffitt, supra, </em>pp 58-59</a></span>. We question whether the benefits rule should ever be used when it requires that a value be placed on human life.<footnotemark>1</footnotemark> We invite our Supreme Court to grant leave to resolve the issues raised in this case.</p>
<p id="b308-5">Reversed.</p>
