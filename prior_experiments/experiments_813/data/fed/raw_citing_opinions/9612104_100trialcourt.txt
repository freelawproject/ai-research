26    II.  LEGAL STANDARD                                                  
27        Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(a) requires that a complaint contain “a short and plain 
1   defendant may move to dismiss a complaint for failing to state a claim upon which relief can be 
2   granted under Rule 12(b)(6).  “Dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) is appropriate only where the 
3   complaint lacks a cognizable legal theory or sufficient facts to support a cognizable legal theory.”  
4   Mendiondo v. Centinela Hosp. Med. Ctr., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1300996"><a href="/opinion/1300996/mendiondo-v-centinela-hospital-medical-center/#1104" aria-description="Citation for case: Mendiondo v. Centinela Hospital Medical Center">521 F.3d 1097, 1104</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).  To survive a Rule 
5   12(b)(6) motion, a plaintiff need only plead “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible 
6   on its face.”  Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007).  A claim is facially plausible 
7   when a plaintiff pleads “factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that 
8   the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009). 
9        Rule 9(b) imposes a heightened pleading standard where fraud is an essential element of a 
10   claim.  See Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b) (“In alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with particularity 
11   the circumstances constituting fraud or mistake.”); see also Vess v. Ciba–Geigy Corp. USA, 317 
</pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/12/1097/">12   F.3d 1097</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1107 (9th Cir. 2003).  A plaintiff must identify “the who, what, when, where, and how” 
13   of the alleged conduct, so as to provide defendants with sufficient information to defend against 
14   the charge.  Cooper v. Pickett, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6964204"><a href="/opinion/7060249/cooper-v-pickett/#627" aria-description="Citation for case: Cooper v. Pickett">137 F.3d 616, 627</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1997).  However, “[m]alice, intent, 
15   knowledge, and other conditions of a person’s mind may be alleged generally.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 
16   Rule 9(b).                                                            
17        In reviewing the plausibility of a complaint, courts “accept factual allegations in the 
18   complaint as true and construe the pleadings in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.”  
19   Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1194077"><a href="/opinion/1194077/manzarek-v-st-paul-fire-marine-insurance/#1031" aria-description="Citation for case: Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Insurance">519 F.3d 1025, 1031</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).  Nevertheless, 
20   courts do not “accept as true allegations that are merely conclusory, unwarranted deductions of 
21   fact, or unreasonable inferences.”  In re Gilead Scis. Secs. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1400355"><a href="/opinion/1400355/in-re-gilead-sciences-securities-litigation/#1055" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Gilead Sciences Securities Litigation">536 F.3d 1049, 1055</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
22   2008) (quoting Sprewell v. Golden State Warriors, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7006046"><a href="/opinion/7100340/sprewell-v-golden-state-warriors/#988" aria-description="Citation for case: Sprewell v. Golden State Warriors">266 F.3d 979, 988</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001)). 
23        Even if the court concludes that a 12(b)(6) motion should be granted, the “court should 
24   grant leave to amend even if no request to amend the pleading was made, unless it determines that 
25   the pleading could not possibly be cured by the allegation of other facts.”  Lopez v. Smith, 203 
</pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/26/1122/">26   F.3d 1122</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1127 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (quotation omitted).

1   III.  MOTION TO DISMISS                                               
2        A.  Request for Judicial Notice                                  
3        Defendant moves the Court to take judicial notice of various materials, including (1) the 
4   labels for 13 of the Products (Dkt. No. 24-2), (2) dictionary definitions of “nourishment” (Mot. at 
5   25 n.10) and “nutrition” (Mot. at 27 n.13), (3) contents from the Federal Register (Mot. at 18–22), 
6   and (4) regulatory guidance from the FDA’s website (Mot. at 18–20).1  Dkt. No. 24-3.  The Court 
7   will GRANT Defendant’s requests, which are all unopposed.             
8           i.  Legal Standard                                            
9        In Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, the Ninth Circuit clarified the judicial notice rule and 
10   incorporation by reference doctrine.  See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d 988</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018).  Under Federal Rule of 
11   Evidence 201, a court may take judicial notice of a fact “not subject to reasonable dispute because 
12   it … can be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be 
13   questioned.”  Fed. R. Evid. 201(b)(2).  Accordingly, a court may take “judicial notice of matters of 
14   public record,” but “cannot take judicial notice of disputed facts contained in such public records.”  
15   Khoja, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#999" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 999</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citation and quotations omitted).  The Ninth Circuit has clarified that if a 
16   court takes judicial notice of a document, it must specify what facts it judicially noticed from the 
17   document.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#999" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id. at 999</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Further, “[j]ust because the document itself is susceptible to judicial notice 
18   does not mean that every assertion of fact within that document is judicially noticeable for its 
19   truth.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  As an example, the Ninth Circuit held that for a transcript of a conference call, the 
20   court may take judicial notice of the fact that there was a conference call on the specified date, but 
21   may not take judicial notice of a fact mentioned in the transcript, because the substance “is subject 
22   to varying interpretations, and there is a reasonable dispute as to what the [document] establishes.”  
23   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 999–1000.                                                      
24        Separately, the incorporation by reference doctrine is a judicially created doctrine that 
25   allows a court to consider certain documents as though they were part of the complaint itself.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> 
26   at 1002.  This is to prevent plaintiffs from cherry-picking certain portions of documents that 
27                                                                         
1   support their claims, while omitting portions that weaken their claims.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Incorporation by 
2   reference is appropriate “if the plaintiff refers extensively to the document or the document forms 
3   the basis of plaintiff’s claim.”  Khoja, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">.  However, “the mere mention of the 
4   existence of a document is insufficient to incorporate the contents” of a document.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id. at 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">.  
5   And while a court “may assume [an incorporated document’s] contents are true for purposes of a 
6   motion to dismiss … it is improper to assume the truth of an incorporated document if such 
7   assumptions only serve to dispute facts stated in a well-pleaded complaint.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">   
8          ii.  Analysis                                                  
9        The Court finds that it can properly consider the labels of the Products (Dkt. No. 24-2, 
10   Exhibits 1-8) under the incorporation by reference doctrine, since “[P]laintiff[s] refer[] extensively 
11   to the [labels]” in their complaint and “the [labels] form[] the basis of [P]laintiff[s’] claim.”  
12   Khoja, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4303222"><a href="/opinion/4525969/karim-khoja-v-orexigen-therapeutics-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: Karim Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc.">899 F.3d at 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">.  See also Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1087" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1087</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">13   Cal. 2017</span><pre class="inline">) (“[C]ourts addressing motions to dismiss product-labeling claims routinely take 
14   judicial notice of images of the product packaging.”) (internal quotations omitted)).  The Court 
15   also finds judicial notice of the two dictionary definitions, Federal Register excerpts, and FDA 
16   materials appropriate, since all of those materials are “not subject to reasonable dispute” and “can 
17   be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be 
18   questioned.”  Fed. R. Evid. 201(b)(2); see also Becerra v. Dr Pepper/Seven Up, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">945 F.3d 
19</span><pre class="inline">   1225, 1229 (9th Cir. 2019) (consulting dictionary definitions); Paralyzed Veterans of Am. v. 
20   McPherson, No. C064670SBA, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2008 WL 4183981</span><pre class="inline">, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 9, 2008) (taking judicial 
21   notice of “information on government agency websites, which have often been treated as proper 
22   subjects for judicial notice”).  Accordingly, Defendant’s request is GRANTED.  Dkt. No. 24-3.   
23        A.  Standing                                                     
24        Defendant argues that Plaintiffs lack both statutory standing for their consumer protection 
25   claims and Article III standing for their injunctive relief claims.  Mot. at 31–32.  The Court agrees 
26   with Plaintiffs that they have adequately pled statutory standing under consumer protection 
27   statutes, but determines that they have not sufficiently pled an entitlement to injunctive relief 
1           i.  Statutory Standing                                        
2        Defendant contends that Plaintiffs lack standing to pursue their California UCL, FAL, and 
3   CLRA claims, as well as their New York GBL claim, because they fail to properly allege 
4   economic injury.  See Mot. at 22-23.  The Court finds that Plaintiffs have pled sufficient facts at 
5   this juncture.                                                        
6        To have standing under the UCL or FAL, a plaintiff must “(1) establish a loss or 
7   deprivation of money or property sufficient to qualify as injury in fact, i.e., economic injury, and 
8   (2) show that the economic injury was the result of, i.e., caused by, the unfair business practice or 
9   false advertising that is the gravamen of the claim.”  Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2368488"><a href="/opinion/2368488/dana-point-safe-harbor-collective-v-superior-court/#310" aria-description="Citation for case: Dana Point Safe Harbor Collective v. Superior Court">51 Cal. 4th 
10   310, 322</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011) (emphasis in original).  In other words, “[a] consumer who relies on a product 
11   label and challenges a misrepresentation contained therein can satisfy the standing requirement of 
12   [the UCL and FAL] by alleging . . . that he or she would not have bought the product but for the 
13   misrepresentation.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2368488"><a href="/opinion/2368488/dana-point-safe-harbor-collective-v-superior-court/" aria-description="Citation for case: Dana Point Safe Harbor Collective v. Superior Court">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  The CLRA requires that a person suffered “damages as the result of” a 
14   violation of the statute in order to have standing, but does not require a showing of economic loss 
15   specifically.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1780</span><pre class="inline">(a).  And to establish standing under the GBL, a plaintiff must 
16   “allege that, on account of a materially misleading practice, she purchased a product and did not 
17   receive the full value of her purchase.”  Duchnik v. Tops Markets, LLC, No. 22CV399JLSHKS, 
18   </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 4827951</span><pre class="inline">, at *6 (W.D.N.Y. July 6, 2023), report and recommendation adopted, No. 
19   22CV399JLSHKS, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 4828141</span><pre class="inline"> (W.D.N.Y. July 26, 2023) (collecting cases).   
20        To have standing under all of these statutes, then, Plaintiffs must allege economic injury 
21   and reliance.  Here, Plaintiffs allege that they were “exposed to, read, and relied on” the 
22   challenged label statements, and identify the label statements on which they relied.  Compl. ¶¶ 
23   150, 152.  They further allege that they “would not have purchased the Products if they knew that 
24   the challenged labeling claims [on which they relied] were false and misleading” and “would only 
25   have been willing to pay less, or unwilling to purchase them at all, absent the false and misleading 
26   labeling” about which they complain.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 155, 158</span><pre class="inline">.  Plaintiffs also allege that the “Products 
27   cost more than similar products without misleading labeling and would have cost less absent 
1   controlling law, these allegations are sufficient to establish standing under the relevant statues 
2   given that Plaintiffs aver they overpaid (i.e. were economically injured) in reliance on Defendant’s 
3   challenged labeling.  See Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015730"><a href="/opinion/9022504/davidson-v-kimberly-clark-corp/#969" aria-description="Citation for case: Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.">889 F.3d 956, 969</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018) 
4   (“[A] consumer’s allegation that ‘she would not have bought the product but for the 
5   misrepresentation . . . is sufficient to allege causation . . . [and] to allege economic injury.’”) 
6   (omissions in the original).                                          
7        Though Defendant casts Plaintiffs’ allegations as “conclusory” and urges the Court to 
8   effectively apply a heightened standard in assessing them, no binding authority supports that 
9   approach.  For example, Defendant challenges Plaintiffs’ allegation that they paid a price premium 
10   for the Products on the grounds that they failed to allege the “amount of the alleged premium or 
11   the identity and pricing of non-premium products.”  Mot. at 32.  But Davidson instructs that to 
12   properly plead economic injury, a consumer must only allege that “she was exposed to false 
13   information about the product purchased, which caused the product to be sold at a higher price, 
14   and that she ‘would not have purchased the goods in question absent this misrepresentation.’”  
15   Davidson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015730"><a href="/opinion/9022504/davidson-v-kimberly-clark-corp/#966" aria-description="Citation for case: Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp.">889 F.3d at 966</a></span><pre class="inline"> (internal citations omitted).  Plaintiffs have done so: they plausibly 
16   allege that they were exposed to labels that supposedly touted the healthiness of the Products in a 
17   misleading manner, and that the perceived healthiness of the Products allowed Defendant to sell 
18   them at a higher price (because consumers are allegedly willing to pay more for healthy foods), 
19   and that Plaintiffs would not have purchased the Products or would have paid less for them absent 
20   the misleading branding. At this stage, it is enough that Plaintiffs allege a plausible theory for the 
21   premium (i.e. the label’s allegedly misleading invocation of healthiness), and causally connect that 
22   misleading labeling to their purchase.  See Ary v. Target Corp., No. 22-CV-02625-HSG, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 
23</span><pre class="inline">   2622142, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 23, 2023); Takahashi-Mendoza v. Coop. Regions of Organic 
24   Producer Pools, </pre><span class="citation no-link">673 F. Supp. 3d 1083</span><pre class="inline">, 1093–94 (N.D. Cal. 2023).       
25        Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have statutory standing under the UCL, FAL, 
26   CLRA and GBL to pursue their claims, and DENIES Defendant’s motion to dismiss on this basis.

1          ii.  Article III Standing                                      
2        Defendant also argues that Plaintiffs lack standing to seek injunctive relief.  On this point, 
3   the Court agrees.                                                     
4        To have standing to seek injunctive relief under Article III, a plaintiff must “demonstrate a 
5   real and immediate threat of repeated injury in the future.”  Chapman v. Pier 1 Imports (U.S.) Inc., 
6   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9438879"><a href="/opinion/182444/chapman-v-pier-1-imports-us-inc/#946" aria-description="Citation for case: Chapman v. Pier 1 Imports (U.S.) Inc.">631 F.3d 939, 946</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (internal quotation omitted).  Wronged plaintiffs are entitled to 
7   injunctive relief only if they can show that they face a ‘“real or immediate threat . . . that [they] 
8   will again be wronged in a similar way.”’  Mayfield v. United States, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="594"><a href="/opinion/594/mayfield-v-united-states/#970" aria-description="Citation for case: Mayfield v. United States">599 F.3d 964, 970</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
9   2010) (omission in original) (quoting City of Los Angeles v. Lyons, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9429162"><a href="/opinion/110916/city-of-los-angeles-v-lyons/#111" aria-description="Citation for case: City of Los Angeles v. Lyons">461 U.S. 95, 111</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1983)).  In 
10   the context of false advertising cases, the Ninth Circuit has confirmed that “a previously deceived 
11   consumer may have standing to seek an injunction against false advertising or labeling, even 
12   though the consumer now knows or suspects that the advertising was false at the time of the 
13   original purchase[.]”  Davidson, 889 F.3d at, 969.  Davidson identifies two ways a plaintiff may 
14   establish the required risk of future harm: (1) “the consumer’s plausible allegations that [they] will 
15   be unable to rely on the product’s advertising or labeling in the future, and so will not purchase the 
16   product although [they] would like to”; or (2) “the consumer’s plausible allegations that [they] 
17   might purchase the product in the future, despite the fact it was once marred by false advertising or 
18   labeling, as [they] may reasonably, but incorrectly, assume the product was improved.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9429162"><a href="/opinion/110916/city-of-los-angeles-v-lyons/" aria-description="Citation for case: City of Los Angeles v. Lyons">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 
19   969–70.                                                               
20        Here, Plaintiffs allege that they “still wish to purchase healthy packaged fruits and juices 
21   with nutritional benefits and continue to see the Products at stores when they shop,” and “would 
22   purchase the Products in the future if the Products were as represented, but unless Dole is enjoined 
23   . . . may not be able to rely on Dole’s health and wellness claims in the future.”  Compl. ¶ 163 
24   (emphasis added).  Plaintiffs’ allegations do not squarely satisfy Davidson: Plaintiffs do not allege 
25   that they will not purchase the product in the future because they cannot rely on the 
26   representations, and they do not allege that they might purchase the Products in the future 
27   assuming – reasonably, but incorrectly – that the product was improved.  Where Plaintiffs allege 
1   repeated injury does not appear “real and immediate.”  Plaintiffs try to rehabilitate the allegations 
2   in their Opposition by suggesting that they face a threat of injury because the Products could be 
3   reformulated (by, for example, processing raw fruit differently) to mitigate the presence of FA 
4   sugar and make the labels truthful without Plaintiffs being able to discern “whether the products 
5   have been reformulated or Dole is just continuing to misrepresent them.”  Opp. at 30.  However, 
6   that allegation (which seems to invoke Davidson’s second scenario) did not appear in the 
7   Complaint, and regardless, Plaintiffs do not explain how they would have trouble discerning the 
8   presence or absence of FA sugar in the Products in the future where they plead elsewhere that 
9   “[f]ree sugar excludes only sugars naturally occurring in intact fruits, vegetables, or dairy 
10   products.”  Compl. ¶ 44 (emphasis in original).  To the extent the Products continue consisting of 
11   anything other than just intact fruit, the Court is skeptical of the notion that Plaintiffs could 
12   “reasonably, but incorrectly” assume improvement of the Products.     
13        Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to dismiss Plaintiffs’ claims for 
14   injunctive relief.2

6           i.  Background                                                
7        The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (“FDCA”) sets out the standards that foods and their 
8   labels must meet to avoid being deemed misbranded.  Chacanaca v. Quaker Oats Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">752 F. 
9</span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 2d 1111, 1116 (N.D. Cal. 2010).  Some of these standards were established through 
10   amendment by the Nutrition Labeling and Education Act of 1990 (“NLEA”), which, among other 
11   things, also added a preemption clause prohibiting states from imposing labeling requirements that 
12   differ from certain of those contained in the FDCA.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 C.F.R. § 343-1</span><pre class="inline">(a)(3) (“[N]o State . . . 
13   may directly or indirectly establish . . . any requirement for the labeling of food . . . that is not 
14   identical to” the FDCA’s requirements).  Under this provision, courts have found labeling claims 
15   barred where plaintiffs seek a court order imposing labeling requirements that differ from or layer 
16   onto the FDCA’s requirements.  See, e.g., Greenberg v. Target Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">985 F.3d 650</span><pre class="inline">, 655 (9th Cir. 
17   2021) (“Thus, if the defendants’ biotin statement meets the FDCA’s three requirements for a 
18   structure/function claim, then any state law claims challenging that claim fall to the wayside.”); 
19   see also Chacanaca, 752 F. Supp. 2d at 1119.                          
20        Section 343(r) of the FDCA “governs all voluntary statements about nutrient content or 
21   health information a manufacturer chooses to include on a food label or packaging.”  Chacanaca, 
22   752 F. Supp. 2d at 1117.  In particular, Section 343(r) covers food labeling claims that “expressly 
23   or by implication” “characterize[] the level of any nutrient” or “characterize[] the relationship of 
24   any nutrient . . . to a disease or a health-related condition.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 U.S.C. § 343</span><pre class="inline">(r)(1)(A)-(B).  The 
25   FDA has promulgated regulations that categorize and govern the two types of claims covered by 
26   Section 343(r) into nutrient content claims (both implied and express) as well as health claims.  
27   See </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 C.F.R. §§ 101.13</span><pre class="inline"> (nutrient content claims), 101.14 (health claims).

18              b.  Antioxidant Statement                                  
19        The parties disagree about whether the back-label Antioxidant Statement (“Vitamin C is an 
20   antioxidant that helps support a healthy immune system”) is a nutrient content claim.  Under 21 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">21   C.F.R. § 101.13</span><pre class="inline">(b), a nutrient content claim “expressly or implicitly characterizes the level of a 
22   nutrient of the type required to be in nutrition labeling.”  An express nutrient content claim makes 
23   a direct statement about the “level (or range) of a nutrient in a food.”  Since the Antioxidant 
24   Statement does not expressly characterize the “level (or range)” of vitamin C in the products, it is 
25   not an express nutrient content claim.                                
26        The question is therefore whether it is an implied nutrient content claim.  The Court finds it 
27   is.  An implied nutrient content claim is one that “[d]escribes the food or an ingredient therein in a 
1   the food, because of its nutrient content, may be useful in maintaining healthy dietary practices 
2   and is made in association with an explicit claim or statement about a nutrient.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 C.F.R. § 
3</span><pre class="inline">   101.13(b)(2)(i)–(ii).  The Antioxidant Statement does not, as Defendant contends, satisfy the first 
4   definition because it does not “characterize[] the level of Vitamin C” in the applicable Products.  
5   Mot. at 17.  Instead, it simply describes the role that vitamin C plays in the body without stating or 
6   implying that it is “absent or present in a certain amount.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 C.F.R. § 101.13</span><pre class="inline">(b)(2)(i) (emphasis 
7   added).  However, the Court must consider whether the Antioxidant Statement meets the second 
8   definition by “[s]uggest[ing] that the food, because of its nutrient content, may be useful in 
9   maintaining healthy dietary practices and [are] made in association with an explicit claim or 
10   statement about a nutrient.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 C.F.R. § 101.13</span><pre class="inline">(b)(2)(i)(ii).  The regulations provide an example 
11   of such a statement: “Healthy, contains 3 grams (g) of fat.”  In this example, “Healthy” is the 
12   implied nutrient content claim made in association with the explicit statement “contains 3 grams 
13   (g) of fat.”                                                          
14        The key inquiry in this case is whether the challenged statement is made “in association 
15   with an explicit claim or statement about a nutrient.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  While it only addresses it in passing, 
16   Defendant argues that this requirement is satisfied because “[w]henever a Product highlights the 
17   presence of Vitamin C with the Antioxidant Statement – which appears on the back or side panel 
18   of the Product – the Product simultaneously states that the Product is an ‘excellent source’ or 
19   ‘good source’ of Vitamin C on the front panel.”  Mot. at 17.  In other words, Defendant “argues 
20   this latter, unchallenged statement renders the former, challenged statement a permitted implied 
21   nutrient content claim.”  Opp. at 13.                                 
22        While a close question, the Court finds that the Antioxidant Statement is made “in 
23   association with” the front-label “excellent source” and “good source” claims.  While Plaintiffs 
24   argue that the statements are not in close enough proximity to be clearly associated, “the ‘magic 
25   words’ that might create an implied nutrient claim do not need to be directly adjacent to the 
26   discussion of a nutrient to create an implied nutrient content claim . . . .”  See LeGrand v. Abbott 
27   Lab’ys, </pre><span class="citation no-link">655 F. Supp. 3d 871</span><pre class="inline">, 888 (N.D. Cal. 2023) (internal quotations omitted).  Instead, the 
1        Here, the words and the context of the label convince the Court that the statements are 
2   adequately associated, their placement notwithstanding.  First, the wording of the Antioxidant 
3   Statement and the front-label “excellent” or “good source” statements clearly indicate a shared 
4   subject matter – the nutrient vitamin C – that linguistically tethers the statements to one another.  
5   This is not always the case.  For example, in each of the implied content claims discussed in an 
6   FDA pamphlet from 2008 – such as “Nutritious, contains 3 grams of fiber,” “Best choice, contains

8   tethers the subject manner of the implied content claim (i.e. the clause before the comma) to the 
9   explicit statement about a nutrient (i.e. the clause after the comma) other than their adjacent 
10   placement.  See Guidance For Industry: A Labeling Guide For Restaurants and Other Retail 
11   Establishments Selling Away-from-home Foods, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2008 WL 2155726</span><pre class="inline">, at *10.  Take as an example 
12   the implied content claim “Nutritious, contains 3 grams of fiber.”  But for the adjacent placement

18   term “healthy” on cereal packaging was not an implied nutrient claim because it was not 
19   “combined with direct references to a governed nutrient” – such as sodium or fat or cholesterol.  
20   No. 16-CV-04958-WHO, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WL 1335867</span><pre class="inline">, at *5 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 15, 2018) [“Krommenhock

 E         advertising copy that likens the Products to sunshine — a patently fanciful analogy that is further

    18    this sentence pairing as anything other than a statement of corporate aspiration would be

   “0    Krommenhock I, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7243732"><a href="/opinion/7325818/krommenhock-v-post-foods-llc/#965" aria-description="Citation for case: Krommenhock v.         assess whether a statement is mere puffery, it must be considered in the context of the whole 
        label.”).  The clear sales jargon backdrop distinguishes this case from those relied upon by 
        Plaintiffs.  See, e.g., Chacanaca, 752 F. Supp. 2d at 1125-26 (holding that use of “wholesome” 
        claim was not puffery in context lacking aspirational sales promises and imagery). 
              Similarly, the challenged representation on Defendant’s Fruit Bowls that they “seal in 
         goodness and nutrition” follows the claim “FULL OF SUNSHINE” and “Bring sunshine with you 
         wherever you go.”  To state the obvious: the Products are not literally “full of sunshine,” and no 
   28 

1   consumer can “bring sunshine” with them “wherever” they go – whether by purchasing one of the 
2   Products or doing something else.  And the Representations’ pairing of “nutrition” with the 
3   nonspecific term “goodness” further incorporates an inherently subjective element.  See Weiss v. 
4   Kroger Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 5114608</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 8, 2014) (“[A] whole lot of goodness 
5   contained in each and every tiny sunflower seed” was “highly subjective non-actionable puffery” 
6   and therefore did not reasonably imply the product was healthy); Truxel, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2019 WL 3940956</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 
7   (“a world of goodness” was non-actionable puffery).  In short, the inclusion of the conceivably 
8   health-related phrases “good nutrition” and “goodness and nutrition” in these two Representations 
9   does not convert clear advertising copy into a verifiable, specific statement of fact.   
10        Even if did, however, the Court’s ultimate view would not change.  To the extent that the 
11   Representations are read literally (which, again, context dictates they should not be), they are not 
12   false because even as alleged the Products do contain “nutrition” as indicated in the nutrition 
13   panels, including vitamin C.  Plaintiffs argue that the “good nutrition” and “goodness and 
14   nutrition” Representations are nevertheless misleading because they connote that the Products are 
15   healthy overall, which – due to the amount of FA sugar – is allegedly not the case.  To be clear, 
16   the Products do not expressly invoke healthiness.  Instead, Plaintiffs’ argument requires 
17   extrapolating an affirmative representation of “healthiness” from the “good nutrition” and 
18   “goodness and nutrition” Representations, and then challenging the truthfulness of the 
19   extrapolated but not actually present “healthiness” representation.   
20        While that extrapolation may be plausible in some circumstances, this is not one of them, 
21   as the inferential leap from the Representations to generalized, overall “healthiness” is 
22   unsupported.  See Davidson v. Sprout Foods Inc., No. 22-CV-01050-RS, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 13801090</span><pre class="inline"> at 
23   *3 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 21, 2022) (“The California Court of Appeal has cautioned against permitting 
24   food labeling claims that rely on interferential leaps and which could ultimately ‘place almost any 
25   advertisement truthfully touting a product’s attributes at issue for litigation.’”) (internal citations 
26   omitted); Andrade-Heymsfield v. Danone US, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2019 WL 3817948</span><pre class="inline">, at *2, *9 (S.D. Cal. Aug. 
27   14, 2019) (“[Allegations of deception must be assessed according to what the advertisement or 
1   context in which Defendant’s [Representations] are presented here make it implausible for a 
2   reasonable consumer to find that [the “good nutrition” and “goodness and nutrition” statements] 
3   [are] synonymous with ‘healthy.’”  Stewart v. Kodiak Cakes, LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">568 F. Supp. 3d 1056</span><pre class="inline">, 1067 
4   (S.D. Cal. 2021).  It is simply implausible that “a reasonable consumer who knows they are 
5   buying a sweet product, such as Diced Peaches in Strawberry Flavored Gel (¶ 27), and who reads 
6   a challenged statement like ‘We believe in Sunshine for All™. It’s our promise to provide 
7   everyone, everywhere with good nutrition!’ immediately adjacent to the Nutrition Facts panel 
8   showing the amount of both naturally occurring and added sugar, would assume that the Product is 
9   generally healthy or would not increase the risk of any disease.”  Reply at 16.   
10        Other courts have reached similar conclusions analyzing similar – and even stronger (i.e. 
11   more directly health-related) – representations.  See, e.g., Lee v. Nature’s Path Food, Inc., No. 23-
12   CV-00751-H-MSB, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 7434963</span><pre class="inline"> (S.D. Cal. Nov. 9, 2023) (rejecting as implausible the 
13   allegation that, even assuming labels explicitly communicated healthiness, they misleadingly 
14   touted the granola’s healthiness); Sanchez v. Nurture, Inc., No.,5:21-CV-08566-EJD, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 
15</span><pre class="inline">   6391487 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 29, 2023) (rejecting as implausible the allegation that representation 
16   “Here’s to a happy and healthy start!” on baby food product misled consumers about presence of 
17   FA sugar); Clark v. Perfect Bar, LLC, No. C 18-06006 WHA, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WL 7048788</span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. Dec. 
18   21, 2018) (dismissing allegations that nut bar label referencing “health food” misled consumers 
19   about the bar’s health benefits) , aff’d, </pre><span class="citation no-link">816 F. App’x 141</span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2020); see also Truxel v. Gen. 
20   Mills Sales, Inc., No. C 16-04957 JSW, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2019 WL 3940956</span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. Aug. 13, 2019).

7   IV.  MOTION TO STAY                                                   
8        Defendant seeks to stay discovery until thirty days after the Court’s ruling on the motion to 
9   dismiss.  See Dkt. No. 36 at 6.  The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure do not provide for an 
10   automatic stay of discovery simply because a potentially dispositive motion is pending.  
11   Nevertheless, a “district court has wide discretion in controlling discovery.”  Little v. City of 
12   Seattle, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="515804"><a href="/opinion/515804/ernest-s-little-v-city-of-seattle-john-doe-jane-doe-and-david-paul/#685" aria-description="Citation for case: Ernest S. Little v. City of Seattle John Doe Jane Doe,...">863 F.2d 681, 685</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1988).  And under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(c), 
13   “[t]he court may, for good cause, issue an order to protect a party or person from annoyance, 
14   embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden or expense[.]”  Courts in this district often apply a 
15   two-prong test to determine whether a stay of discovery is appropriate.  See In re Nexus 6p Prod. 
16   Liab. Litig., No. 17-CV-02185-BLF, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 3581188</span><pre class="inline">, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 18, 2017) 
17   (collecting cases).  First, the moving party must demonstrate that the pending motion is 
18   “potentially dispositive of the entire case, or at least dispositive on the issue at which discovery is 
19   directed.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  Second, “the court must determine whether the pending motion can be decided 
20   absent discovery.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">                                               
21        Having found that the underlying motion to dismiss warrants dismissal of the complaint in 
22   its entirety, as Defendant urged, the Court believes “[i]t is sounder practice to determine whether 
23   there is any reasonable likelihood that plaintiffs can construct a claim before forcing the parties to 
24   undergo the expense of discovery.”  Rutman Wine Co. v. E. &amp; J. Gallo Winery, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="494674"><a href="/opinion/494674/rutman-wine-company-v-e-j-gallo-winery/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rutman Wine Company v. E. &amp; J. Gallo Winery">829 F.2d 729</a></span><pre class="inline">, 738 
25   (9th Cir. 1987).  Accordingly, the Court finds that there is good cause under Rule 26(c) to 
26   temporarily stay discovery until otherwise ordered.
