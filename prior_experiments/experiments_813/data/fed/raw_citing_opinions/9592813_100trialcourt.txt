8       II.  LEGAL STANDARD FOR DEFAULT JUDGMENT                         
9       Pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 55(b)(2), a plaintiff can apply to the court for 
10  a default judgment against a defendant that has failed to plead or otherwise defend against the 
11  action.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 55(b)(2).  “Upon default, the well-pleaded allegations of a complaint 
12  relating to liability are taken as true.” Dundee Cement Co. v. Howard Pipe &amp; Concrete Prods., 
13  Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="428368"><a href="/opinion/428368/dundee-cement-company-v-howard-pipe-concrete-products-inc/#1323" aria-description="Citation for case: Dundee Cement Company v. Howard Pipe &amp; Concrete Products,...">722 F.2d 1319, 1323</a></span><pre class="inline"> (7th Cir. 1983); TeleVideo Sys., Inc. v. Heidenthal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="492745"><a href="/opinion/492745/televideo-systems-inc-k-philip-hwang-c-gemma-hwang-v-fred-p/" aria-description="Citation for case: Televideo Systems, Inc. K. Philip Hwang C. Gemma Hwang v....">826 F.2d 915</a></span><pre class="inline">, 917-
14  18 (9th Cir. 1987).                                                   
15       Factors which may be considered by courts in exercising discretion as to the entry of a 
16  default judgment include: (1) the possibility of prejudice to the plaintiff; (2) the merits of 
17  plaintiff’s substantive claim; (3) the sufficiency of the complaint; (4) the sum of money at stake in 
18  the action; (5) the possibility of a dispute concerning material facts; (6) whether the default was 
19  due to excusable neglect; and (7) the strong policy underlying the Federal Rules of Civil 
20  Procedure favoring decisions on the merits.  Eitel v. McCool, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="464670"><a href="/opinion/464670/gary-r-eitel-v-william-d-mccool/#1471" aria-description="Citation for case: Gary R. Eitel v. William D. McCool">782 F.2d 1470, 1471-72</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
21  1986); PepsiCo, Inc. v. Cal. Sec. Cans, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2417992"><a href="/opinion/2417992/pepsico-inc-v-california-security-cans/#1174" aria-description="Citation for case: Pepsico, Inc. v. California Security Cans">238 F. Supp. 2d 1172, 1174</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 2002). 
22       III.  DISCUSSION                                                 
23            A.   Service of Process                                     
24       In deciding whether to grant or deny a default judgment, a court should assess the 
25  adequacy of the service of process on the party against whom default is requested.  See, e.g., 
26  Trujillo v. Harsarb, Inc., No. 1:21-cv-00342-NONE-SAB, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 3783388</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (E.D. Cal. 
27  Aug. 26, 2021) (“As a general rule, the Court considers the adequacy of service of process before 
28  evaluating the merits of a motion for default judgment.”); Coach, Inc. v. Diva Shoes &amp; 
1  Accessories, No. 10-5151 SC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2011 WL 1483436</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 19, 2011); Katzakian v. 
2  Check Resolution Service, Inc., No. 1:10-cv-00716 AWI GSA, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2010 WL 5200912</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (E.D. 
3  Cal. Dec. 15, 2010).                                                  
4       Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4 sets forth the requirements for serving an individual 
5  within a judicial district of the United States. Under Rule 4, an individual may be served by: (1) 
6  delivering a copy of the summons and the complaint to that person personally; (2) leaving a copy 
7  of each at the individual&#x27;s dwelling or usual place of abode with someone of suitable age and 
8  discretion who resides there; or (3) delivering a copy of each to an agent authorized by 
9  appointment or by law to receive service of process.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 4(e)(2).  
10       Rule 4 also permits service on an individual in accordance with state law.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 
11  4(e)(1).  California law permits service by mailing a “copy of the summons and of the 
12  complaint… (by first-class mail or airmail, postage prepaid) to the person to be served, together 
13  with two copies of the notice and acknowledgment provided for in subdivision (b) and a return 
14  envelope, postage prepaid, addressed to the sender.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 415.30</span><pre class="inline">(a). 
15  Subsection (b) of the statute provides the form for a Notice and Acknowledgement of Receipt of 
16  Summons which cautions the recipient that “[f]ailure to complete this form and return it to the 
17  sender within 20 days may subject you (or the party on whose behalf you are being served) to 
18  liability for the payment of any expenses incurred in serving a summons upon you in any other 
19  manner permitted by law.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> § 415.30(b), 415.30(d).  Service by mail is “deemed complete on 
20  the date a written acknowledgment of receipt of summons is executed.”  Id. § 415.30(c); see also 
21  Barlow v. Ground, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="681363"><a href="/opinion/681363/brian-barlow-v-george-ground-officer-9129-lo-oberlies-officer-1706-a/#234" aria-description="Citation for case: Brian Barlow v. George Ground, Officer 9129 L.O....">39 F.3d 231, 234</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1994).

7            B.  The Eitel Factors Weigh in Favor of Default Judgment    
8            1.  Possibility of Prejudice to Plaintiff                   
9       The first factor considers whether a plaintiff would suffer prejudice if default judgment is 
10  not entered. See PepsiCo, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2417992"><a href="/opinion/2417992/pepsico-inc-v-california-security-cans/#1177" aria-description="Citation for case: Pepsico, Inc. v. California Security Cans">238 F. Supp. 2d at 1177</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Generally, where default has been 
11  entered against a defendant, a plaintiff has no other means by which to recover against that 
12  defendant.  Id.; Moroccanoil, Inc. v. Allstate Beauty Prods., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8696879"><a href="/opinion/8713687/moroccanoil-inc-v-allstate-beauty-products-inc/#1200" aria-description="Citation for case: Moroccanoil, Inc. v. Allstate Beauty Products, Inc.">847 F. Supp. 2d 1197, 1200-01</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">13  Cal. 2012</span><pre class="inline">).   Here, the Court finds Plaintiff would be prejudiced if default judgment were not 
14  granted.   Default has been entered against Defendants and Plaintiff has no other means to recover 
15  against him.  (Docs. 17-18.)  This factor weighs in favor of default judgment. 
16            2.  Merits of Plaintiff’s Claims and Sufficiency of the Complaint 
17       The second and third Eitel factors, taken together, “require that [the] plaintiff[s] state a 
18  claim on which [they] may recover.” PepsiCo, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2417992"><a href="/opinion/2417992/pepsico-inc-v-california-security-cans/#1175" aria-description="Citation for case: Pepsico, Inc. v. California Security Cans">238 F. Supp. 2d at 1175</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Notably a 
19  “defendant is not held to admit facts that are not well-pleaded or to admit conclusions of law.” 
20  DIRECTV, Inc. v. Hoa Huynh, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9565694"><a href="/opinion/1217988/directv-inc-v-hoa-huynh/#854" aria-description="Citation for case: DirecTV, Inc. v. Hoa Huynh">503 F.3d 847, 854</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2007).        
21       Copyright Infringement                                           
22       To establish copyright infringement, a plaintiff must show: (1) “that he owns a valid 
23  copyright…; and (2) that [the alleged infringer] copied protected aspects of the work.”  Skidmore 
24  as Tr. for Randy Craig Wolfe Tr. v. Led Zeppelin, </pre><span class="citation no-link">952 F.3d 1051</span><pre class="inline">, 1064 (9th Cir. 2020).  To qualify 
25  for valid ownership of a copyright, “a work must be original to the author.”  Feist Publ’ns, Inc. v. 
26  Rural Tel. Serv. Co., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="112568"><a href="/opinion/112568/feist-publications-inc-v-rural-telephone-service-co/#345" aria-description="Citation for case: Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.">499 U.S. 340, 345</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1981).  “Original, as the term is used in copyright, 
27  means only that the work was independently created by the author (as opposed to copied from other 
28  works), and that it possesses at least some minimal degree of creativity.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="112568"><a href="/opinion/112568/feist-publications-inc-v-rural-telephone-service-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  “The second prong 
1  of  the  infringement  analysis  contains  two  separate  components:  ‘copying’  and  ‘unlawful 
2  appropriation.’”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="112568"><a href="/opinion/112568/feist-publications-inc-v-rural-telephone-service-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Plaintiffs may offer either direct evidence of copying or show that the 
3  “similarities between the two works are due to ‘copying rather than ... coincidence, independent 
4  creation, or prior common source.’”  Bernal v. Paradigm Talent &amp; Literary Agency, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1599785"><a href="/opinion/1599785/national-treasury-employees-union-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: National Treasury Employees Union v. United States">788 F. Supp. 
5</a></span><pre class="inline">  2d 1043, 1052 (C.D. Cal. 2010).

8       Violation of California Unfair Competition Law – Unfair Practices Prong 
9       Plaintiff’s second claim is for violation of California Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”), 
10  and Plaintiff clarifies in his motion for default judgment that he relies upon “fraud” and “unfair 
11  practices” prongs of UCL violation.  (Doc. 1 ¶¶ 55-58; Doc. 20-1 at 16.) 
12       California  Business  and  Professions  Code  §  17200  concerns  unfair  competition  and 
13  prohibited activities and states that “unfair competition shall mean and include any unlawful, unfair 
14  or fraudulent business act or practice.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200</span><pre class="inline">.  Each prong of the UCL 
15  is a separate and distinct theory of liability.  Lozano v. AT &amp; T Wireless Servs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1429600"><a href="/opinion/1429600/lozano-v-at-t-wireless-services-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lozano v. AT &amp; T Wireless Services, Inc.">504 F.3d 718</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
16  731 (9th Cir. 2007).  To state a claim under the UCL, a plaintiff must identify an underlying statute 
17  that defendant violated.  Ingels v. Westwood One Broad. Servs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2255528"><a href="/opinion/2255528/ingels-v-westwood-one-broadcasting-services-inc/#1060" aria-description="Citation for case: Ingels v. Westwood One Broadcasting Services, Inc.">129 Cal. App. 4th 1050, 1060, 
18</a></span><pre class="inline">  (2005) (“A defendant cannot be liable under § 17200 for committing ‘unlawful business practices&#x27; 
19  without having violated another law.”).                               
20       California  courts  of  appeal  have  applied  three  different  tests  to  evaluate  claims  by 
21  consumers under the UCL&#x27;s unfair practices prong.  Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Assn., 182 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">22  Cal.App.4th 247</span><pre class="inline">, 256, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2277001"><a href="/opinion/2277001/drum-v-san-fernando-valley-bar-assn/" aria-description="Citation for case: Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Assn.">106 Cal.Rptr.3d 46</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2010).  Under the first test (the tethering test), a 
23  consumer must allege a “violation or incipient violation of any statutory or regulatory provision, or 
24  any significant harm to competition.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2277001"><a href="/opinion/2277001/drum-v-san-fernando-valley-bar-assn/" aria-description="Citation for case: Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Assn.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  The “public policy which is a predicate to a consumer 
25  unfair competition action under the ‘unfair prong’ of the UCL must be tethered to specific 
26  constitutional, statutory, or regulatory provisions.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2277001"><a href="/opinion/2277001/drum-v-san-fernando-valley-bar-assn/" aria-description="Citation for case: Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Assn.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">            
27       The test applied in a second line of cases is whether the alleged business practice “is 
28  immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous or substantially injurious to consumers and requires 
1  the court to weigh the utility of the defendant&#x27;s conduct against the gravity of the harm to the alleged 
2  victim.”  Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Assn., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2277001"><a href="/opinion/2277001/drum-v-san-fernando-valley-bar-assn/#257" aria-description="Citation for case: Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Assn.">182 Cal. App. 4th 247, 257</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2010); Bardin v. 
3  DaimlerChrysler Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2280950"><a href="/opinion/2280950/bardin-v-daimlerchrysler-corp/#1260" aria-description="Citation for case: Bardin v. DaimlerChrysler Corp.">136 Cal. App. 4th 1255, 1260</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2006); Davis v. Ford Motor Credit Co. 
4  LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2256435"><a href="/opinion/2256435/davis-v-ford-motor-credit-co-llc/#593" aria-description="Citation for case: Davis v. Ford Motor Credit Co. LLC">179 Cal. App. 4th 581, 593-95</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009).  Under this second test (the balancing test), the “unfair 
5  prong”  requires  a  consumer  to  plead  that  (1) a  defendant&#x27;s  conduct  “is  immoral,  unethical, 
6  oppressive, unscrupulous or substantially injurious to consumers” and (2) “the utility of the 
7  defendant&#x27;s conduct” is outweighed by “the gravity of the harm to the alleged victim.”  Drum, 182 
8  Cal. App. 4th at 257.                                                 
9       The third test, which is based on the Federal Trade Commission&#x27;s definition of unfair 
10  business practices, requires that, as a result of unfair conduct, “(1) the consumer injury must be 
11  substantial; (2) the injury must not be outweighed by any countervailing benefits to consumers or 
12  competition; and (3) it must be an injury that consumers themselves could not reasonably have 
13  avoided.”  Id.  (citation and internal quotation marks omitted).   The Ninth Circuit has endorsed the 
14  tethering test and balancing test but declined “to apply the FTC standard in the absence of a clear 
15  holding from the California Supreme Court.”  Lozano, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1429600"><a href="/opinion/1429600/lozano-v-at-t-wireless-services-inc/#736" aria-description="Citation for case: Lozano v. AT &amp; T Wireless Services, Inc.">504 F.3d at 736</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Therefore, for this Court&#x27;s 
16  purposes, conduct is “unfair” under the UCL when the alleged unfairness is (1) “tethered to specific 
17  constitutional, statutory, or regulatory provisions;” or (2) when the practice is “immoral, unethical, 
18  oppressive, unscrupulous or substantially injurious to consumers” and the utility of that conduct is 
19  outweighed by the harm to victim.  Drum, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2277001"><a href="/opinion/2277001/drum-v-san-fernando-valley-bar-assn/#257" aria-description="Citation for case: Drum v. San Fernando Valley Bar Assn.">182 Cal. App. 4th at 257</a></span><pre class="inline">.    
20       Here, Plaintiff does not identify a legislatively declared policy that Defendants’ alleged 
21  actions is tethered to.  (See Doc. 1; Doc. 20-1.)  Nor does Plaintiff attempt to explain how 
22  Defendants’ practices are sufficiently immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous, or injurious 
23  that any utility is outweighed by consumer harm.  (Id.)  Plaintiff solely argues in his motion that 
24  Defendants are using Plaintiff’s copyrighted visual artwork to confuse or deceive the public 
25  regarding the sponsorship, affiliation, and approval of “annual copycat events which Defendants 
26  hold in direct competition to the annual, long-standing and traditional events of UNKNOWN 
27  RIDERS MC – FRESNO.”  (Doc. 20-1 at 16.)  However, Plaintiff does not allege in his 
28  Complaint that Defendant holds copycat events that are in direct competition with Plaintiff’s 
1  events or that Defendant is trying to deceive or confuse the public.  (See Doc. 1.)  Plaintiff simply 
2  alleges that “Defendants have engaged in unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business acts of unfair 
3  competition… [that] includes Defendants’ unauthorized use of Plaintiff’s copyrighted work (1) in 
4  promotions for UNKNOWN RIDERS M.C. events, and (2) in postings on social media to 
5  increase their goodwill and name recognition.”  (Id. ¶ 57.)  The allegations in Plaintiff’s 
6  Complaint therefore do not support Plaintiff’s suggested “unfair practices” theory of UCL 
7  violation under the tethering test or the balancing test approved by the Ninth Circuit. 
8       Moreover, Plaintiff’s pleadings do not satisfy the heightened pleading requirements raised 
9  by fraud-related claims.  The Ninth Circuit has held that where plaintiffs allege a unified 
10  fraudulent course of conduct, the “entire complaint must therefore be pleaded with particularity” 
11  pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b).  Kearns v. Ford Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208595"><a href="/opinion/1208595/kearns-v-ford-motor-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.">567 F.3d 1120</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
12  1127 (9th Cir. 2009) (applying Rule 9(b) requirement to unlawful practices and unfair practices 
13  prongs of the UCL); Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1094" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1094</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017) 
14  (“Where Plaintiff has alleged a ‘unified course of fraudulent conduct,’ Rule 9(b)&#x27;s particularity 
15  requirement applies to the unlawful and unfair prong of the UCL in addition to the above-
16  discussed fraudulent prong.”).  In clarifying the requirements of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 
17  9(b) in a case involving a UCL claim, the Ninth Circuit held that “[a]verments of fraud must be 
18  accompanied by ‘the who, what, when, where, and how’ of the misconduct charged.”  Vess v. 
19  Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1106" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d 1097, 1106</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2003).            
20       Furthermore, where plaintiffs’ UCL claims “sound in fraud, they must allege specific facts 
21  showing that they relied on the alleged misrepresentations or omissions.”  Watkins v. MGA Ent., 
22  Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">550 F. Supp. 3d 815</span><pre class="inline">, 833–34 (N.D. Cal. 2021);  Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Ct., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452554"><a href="/opinion/5608104/people-v-howard/#310" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Howard">51 Cal. 4th 
23  310</a></span><pre class="inline">, 326–27, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/#888" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">246 P.3d 877, 888</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011) (“Recognizing that ‘reliance is the causal mechanism of 
24  fraud’… we held that a plaintiff ‘proceeding on a claim of misrepresentation as the basis of his or 
25  her UCL action must demonstrate actual reliance on the allegedly deceptive or misleading 
26  statements, in accordance with well-settled principles regarding the element of reliance in 
27  ordinary fraud actions.’”).  District courts have held that the reliance requirement “applies not 
28  only to UCL claims asserted under the fraud prong of section 17200 but also to claims asserted 
1  under the ‘unlawful’ and ‘unfair’ prongs where those claims are predicated on misrepresentation 
2  and deception.  Watkins, 550 F. Supp. 3d at 834; Michel v. United States, No. 16-cv-277-GPC 
3  (AGS), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 4922831</span><pre class="inline">, at *18 (S.D. Cal. Oct. 31, 2017) (“district courts have subsequently 
4  held that if the allegations under the unlawful and unfair prongs are premised on the same 
5  allegations as the fraud prong, reliance is also a requirement for the unlawful and unfair 
6  prongs.”); Kwikset, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/#326" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">51 Cal. 4th at 326</a></span><pre class="inline">.  To sufficiently plead reliance, a plaintiff “must ‘truthfully 
7  allege’ that ‘she would not have bought the product but for the’ allegedly actionable 
8  misrepresentation or omission.”  Shaeffer v. Califia Farms, LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">44 Cal. App. 5th 1125</span><pre class="inline">, 1143, 
9  </pre><span class="citation no-link">258 Cal. Rptr. 3d 270</span><pre class="inline">, 283 (2020).  In the case of omission-related fraud, a plaintiff must “prove 
10  that, had the omitted information been disclosed one would have been aware of it and behaved 
11  differently.”  Mirkin v. Wasserman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9539140"><a href="/opinion/1154737/mirkin-v-wasserman/#1093" aria-description="Citation for case: Mirkin v. Wasserman">5 Cal. 4th 1082, 1093</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9539140"><a href="/opinion/1154737/mirkin-v-wasserman/#574" aria-description="Citation for case: Mirkin v. Wasserman">858 P.2d 568, 574</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1993). 
12       Plaintiff does not allege sufficient specificity or reliance as to Defendants’ alleged 
13  fraudulent scheme.  (See Doc. 1.)  Plaintiff alleges that Defendants have used Plaintiff’s 
14  copyrighted work “(1) in promotions for UNKNOWN RIDERS M.C. events, and (2) in postings 
15  on social media to increase their goodwill and name recognition.”  (Doc. 1 ¶¶ 41, 57.)  Plaintiff 
16  also broadly states that, as “an actual and proximate result of Defendants’ unfair competition, 
17  Defendants have unjustly enriched themselves by, among other things, obtaining profits, 
18  depriving Plaintiff of the compensation to which he is rightly entitled, and taking credit for 
19  Plaintiff’s original work.”  (Id. ¶ 58.)  These allegations do not demonstrate that Plaintiff or others 
20  either relied on Defendants’ allegedly fraudulent actions or clarify “the who, what, when, where, 
21  and how” of Defendants’ allegedly fraudulent conduct.  Watkins, 550 F. Supp. 3d at 833-34; Vess, 
22  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1106" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d at 1106</a></span><pre class="inline">.  For these reasons, Plaintiff’s claim under the “unfair practices” prong of the 
23  UCL would likely not succeed on the merits.                           
24       Violation of California Unfair Competition Law – Fraud Prong     
25       Plaintiff further argues that Defendant violated the UCL based upon the UCL’s fraud prong.  
26  (Doc. 1 ¶¶ 55-58; Doc. 20-1 at 16.)                                   
27       “The fraudulent prong of the UCL does not require a plaintiff to establish the elements of 
28  the common law tort of fraud; rather, a ‘fraudulent business practice is one that is likely to deceive 
1  members of the public.’”  O&#x27;Connor v. Uber Techs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7227686"><a href="/opinion/7309782/oconnor-v-uber-technologies-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: O&#x27;Connor v. Uber Technologies, Inc.">58 F. Supp. 3d 989, 1002</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 
2  2014) (citing Morgan v. AT &amp; T Wireless Servs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2275753"><a href="/opinion/2275753/morgan-v-att-wireless-services-inc/#1255" aria-description="Citation for case: Morgan v. AT&amp;T Wireless Services, Inc.">177 Cal.App.4th 1235, 1255</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation no-link">99 Cal.Rptr.3d 
3</span><pre class="inline">  768 (2009)).  District courts have generally required that “UCL fraud plaintiffs must allege their 
4  own reliance—not the reliance of third parties—to have standing under the UCL.”  See O&#x27;Connor, 
5  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7227686"><a href="/opinion/7309782/oconnor-v-uber-technologies-inc/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: O&#x27;Connor v. Uber Technologies, Inc.">58 F. Supp. 3d at 1002</a></span><pre class="inline">; Copart, Inc. v. Sparta Consulting, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7250494"><a href="/opinion/7332576/copart-inc-v-sparta-consulting-inc/#987" aria-description="Citation for case: Copart, Inc. v. Sparta Consulting, Inc.">339 F. Supp. 3d 959, 987</a></span><pre class="inline"> (E.D. Cal. 
6  2018) (“the plaintiff ‘must allege he or she was motivated to act or refrain from action based on the 
7  truth or falsity of a defendant&#x27;s statement, not merely on the fact it was made.’”) (citations omitted); 
8  ZL Techs., Inc. v. Gartner, Inc., No. CV 09-02393 JF (RS), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2009 WL 3706821</span><pre class="inline">, at *11 (N.D. Cal. 
9  Nov. 4, 2009) (finding that plaintiff lacked standing for UCL fraud claim where Plaintiff “alleges 
10  not its own reliance upon the Alleged Defamatory Statements but that of third parties—potential 
11  [plaintiff] customers—resulting in a loss of profits and injury”); Jent v. N. Tr. Corp., No. CIV 13-
12  01684-WBS-CKD, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 WL 5806024</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (E.D. Cal. Oct. 28, 2013) (“As discussed above 
13  regarding plaintiffs&#x27; negligent misrepresentation claim, allegations of actual reliance are absent 
14  here. Plaintiffs&#x27; UCL claim therefore cannot proceed under the fraudulent prong.”).  Additionally, 
15  to satisfy Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b), the Ninth Circuit has held that “[a]verments of fraud 
16  must be accompanied by ‘the who, what, when, where, and how’ of the misconduct charged.”  Vess, 
17  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1106" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d at 1106</a></span><pre class="inline">.                                                     
18       As discussed above, Plaintiff’s Complaint does not satisfy the standing requirements for 
19  UCL  fraud-related  claims.    Plaintiff’s  Complaint  does  not  allege  that  Plaintiff  relied  upon 
20  Defendants’ fraudulent actions.  (See Doc. 1.)  Plaintiff also does not suggest a specific injury or 
21  allege that he was motivated to act or refrain from action based on Defendants’ use of Plaintiff’s 
22  copyrighted visual artwork.  Copart, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7250494"><a href="/opinion/7332576/copart-inc-v-sparta-consulting-inc/#987" aria-description="Citation for case: Copart, Inc. v. Sparta Consulting, Inc.">339 F. Supp. 3d at 987</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Plaintiff only alleges that Defendants 
23  have used Plaintiff’s copyrighted visual artwork “(1) in promotions for UNKNOWN RIDERS M.C. 
24  events, and (2) in postings on social media to increase their goodwill and name recognition” and 
25  broadly  states  that,  as  “an  actual  and  proximate  result  of  Defendants’  unfair  competition, 
26  Defendants have unjustly enriched themselves by, among other things, obtaining profits, depriving 
27  Plaintiff of the compensation to which he is rightly entitled, and taking credit for Plaintiff’s original 
28  work.”  (Doc. 1 ¶¶ 57-58.)  These allegations do not demonstrate that Plaintiff either relied on 
1  Defendants’ allegedly fraudulent actions or clarify “the who, what, when, where, and how” of 
2  Defendants’ allegedly fraudulent conduct.  Watkins, 550 F. Supp. 3d at 833-34; Vess, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d at 
3</a></span><pre class="inline">  1106.  Therefore, Plaintiff does not have sufficient standing and has not properly pleaded a UCL 
4  claim under the fraud prong.  Accordingly, Plaintiff’s UCL claim would likely not succeed on the 
5  merits.                                                               
6       The Court finds that Plaintiff has sufficiently pleaded and would likely succeed on the 
7  merits of his copyright infringement claim, but insufficiently pleaded his UCL claim.  Because 
8  federal copyright statutes are the source for Plaintiff’s requested relief of an injunction and the 
9  award of attorneys’ fees, the insufficiency of Plaintiff’s pleading of the UCL claim does not 
10  impact the merits of the copyright claim and resultant relief.  The Court therefore finds that the 
11  second and third Eitel factors weigh in favor of default judgment.    
12            3.  The Sum of Money at Stake in the Action                 
13       Under the fourth factor cited in Eitel, “the court must consider the amount of money at 
14  stake in relation to the seriousness of Defendant’s conduct.”  PepsiCo, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2417992"><a href="/opinion/2417992/pepsico-inc-v-california-security-cans/" aria-description="Citation for case: Pepsico, Inc. v. California Security Cans">238 F. Supp. 2d at 
15</a></span><pre class="inline">  1176; see also Philip Morris USA, Inc. v. Castworld Prods., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8737227"><a href="/opinion/8753952/philip-morris-usa-inc-v-castworld-products-inc/#500" aria-description="Citation for case: Philip Morris USA Inc. v. Castworld Products, Inc.">219 F.R.D. 494, 500</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 
16  2003).                                                                
17       Here, Plaintiff seeks an injunction and attorneys’ fees and costs. (Doc. 20-1 at 16-17, 21-
18  22.)  Plaintiff’s counsel states that Plaintiff incurred costs in the amount of $512.10 and attorneys’ 
19  fees in the amount of $5,400.00.  (Doc. 20-3 ¶¶ 17-20.)  While Plaintiff initially notes that he 
20  seeks “compensatory damages” in his motion, Plaintiff’s motion also states that “Plaintiff Seeks 
21  No Damages-Only Permanent Injunctive Relief” and Plaintiff’s proposed order only includes 
22  injunctive relief and attorneys’ fees and costs.  (Doc.20-1 at 6, 16; Doc. 20-4.)  The amount of 
23  money at stake is relatively small and it does not seem unreasonable in light of the allegations 
24  contained in the complaint.  Accordingly, this factor does not weigh against entry of default 
25  judgment.                                                             
26            4.  The Possibility of a Dispute Concerning Material Facts  
27       Following the Clerk’s entry of default, the Court may assume the truth of well-pleaded 
28  facts in the complaint and, thus, there is no likelihood that any genuine issue of material fact 
1  exists.  Further, Defendants’ failure to file an answer in this case and his failure to file a response 
2  to the request for entry of default or a response to the instant motion supports the conclusion that 
3  the possibility of a dispute as to material facts is minimal.  See, e.g., Elektra Entm’t Grp. Inc. v. 
4  Crawford, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8739481"><a href="/opinion/8756205/elektra-entertainment-group-inc-v-crawford/#393" aria-description="Citation for case: Elektra Entertainment Group Inc. v. Crawford">226 F.R.D. 388, 393</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 2005) (“Because all allegations in a well-pleaded 
5  complaint are taken as true after the court clerk enters default judgment, there is no likelihood that 
6  any genuine issue of material fact exists.”).  This factor therefore weighs in favor of default 
7  judgment.                                                             
8            5.  Whether the Default Was Due to Excusable Neglect        
9       The sixth Eitel factor considers the possibility that Defendants’ default resulted from 
10  excusable neglect.  PepsiCo, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2417992"><a href="/opinion/2417992/pepsico-inc-v-california-security-cans/#1177" aria-description="Citation for case: Pepsico, Inc. v. California Security Cans">238 F. Supp. 2d at 1177</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Courts have found that where a 
11  defendant was “properly served with the complaint, the notice of entry of default, as well as the 
12  paper in support of the [default judgment] motion,” there is no evidence of excusable neglect. 
13  Shanghai Automation Instrument Co. v. Kuei, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2577631"><a href="/opinion/2577631/shanghai-automation-instrument-co-ltd-v-kuei/#1005" aria-description="Citation for case: Shanghai Automation Instrument Co., Ltd. v. Kuei">194 F. Supp. 2d 995, 1005</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2001).  
14       Upon review of the record, the Court finds that the default was not the result of excusable 
15  neglect.  See PepsiCo, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2417992"><a href="/opinion/2417992/pepsico-inc-v-california-security-cans/#1177" aria-description="Citation for case: Pepsico, Inc. v. California Security Cans">238 F. Supp. 2d at 1177</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Defendants were properly served with the 
16  complaint (Docs. 13-14) and the motion for default judgment, which included notification of the 
17  Clerk’s entry of default (Doc. 20-5).  Despite service with these documents, Defendants have not 
18  appeared in this action.  Thus, the record suggests that Defendants have chosen not to participate 
19  in this action, and not that the default resulted from any excusable neglect.  Accordingly, this 
20  factor weighs in favor of the entry of a default judgment.            
21            6.  The Strong Policy Favoring Decisions on the Merits      
22       “Cases should be decided upon their merits whenever reasonably possible.”  Eitel, 782 
23  F.2d at 1472.  However, district courts have concluded with regularity that this policy, standing 
24  alone, is not dispositive, especially where a defendant fails to appear or defend itself in an action.  
25  PepsiCo, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2417992"><a href="/opinion/2417992/pepsico-inc-v-california-security-cans/#1177" aria-description="Citation for case: Pepsico, Inc. v. California Security Cans">238 F. Supp. 2d at 1177</a></span><pre class="inline">; see also Craigslist, Inc. v. Naturemarket, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">694 F. 
26</span><pre class="inline">  Supp. 2d 1039, 1061 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 5, 2010).  Although the Court is cognizant of the policy 
27  favoring decisions on the merits, that policy is unavailable here because Defendants have not 
28  appeared in this action.  Accordingly, the Court finds that this factor does not weigh against entry 
1  of default judgment.                                                  
2            C.  Damages                                                 
3       Plaintiff states in his motion for default judgment and proposed order that he seeks 
4  injunctive relief and attorneys’ fees and costs.  (Doc. 20-1; Doc. 20-4.)  Though Plaintiff initially 
5  notes that he seeks “compensatory damages” in his motion, Plaintiff’s motion also states that 
6  “Plaintiff Seeks No Damages-Only Permanent Injunctive Relief” and Plaintiff’s proposed order 
7  only includes injunctive relief and attorneys’ fees and costs.  (Doc.20-1 at 6, 16; Doc. 20-4.)  
8  Accordingly, the Court only addresses the requested injunctive relief and attorneys’ fees and 
9  costs.                                                                
10               1.  Injunctive Relief                                    
11       Plaintiffs seek an injunction prohibiting Defendant from engaging any further activities 
12  which would constitute copyright infringement.  (Doc. 1 ¶¶ 43, 54; Doc. 20-1 at 18-21; Doc. 20-4 
13  at 2-3.)  Such relief is expressly authorized by </pre><span class="citation no-link">17 U.S.C. § 502</span><pre class="inline">.  In copyright infringement actions, 
14  “[a]s a general rule, a permanent injunction will be granted when liability has been established and 
15  there is a threat of continuing violations.”  MAI Systems Corp. v. Peak Computer, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9009862"><a href="/opinion/9016768/hite-v-national-transportation-safety-board/#511" aria-description="Citation for case: Hite v. National Transportation Safety Board">991 F.2d 
16  511, 520</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir.1993).                                              
17       Plaintiff has alleged that, in 2021, Plaintiff and members of his organization Unknown 
18  Riders MC - Fresno demanded Defendants cease and desist use of Plaintiff’s copyrighted visual 
19  artwork.  (Doc. 1 at 36, 42.)  Plaintiff then attempted to remedy the alleged infringement through 
20  this action.  Plaintiff’s process server attempted service upon Defendant Zeno and notified 
21  Defendant Zeno of the action but was told by Defendant Zeno that Defendant Zeno would not 
22  accept service.  (Doc. 11-1 ¶ 7.)  Based upon this sequence of events, the Court finds that there is 
23  a threat of continuing violations and injunctive relief is appropriate.  Therefore, an injunction 
24  should issue requiring Defendants to cease use of Plaintiff’s copyrighted visual artwork. 
25               2.  Attorneys’ Fees and Costs                            
26       Plaintiff seeks an award of attorneys&#x27; fees in the amount of $5,400.00.  Recovery of costs, 
27  including attorneys&#x27; fees, are authorized in copyright infringement actions.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">17 U.S.C. § 505</span><pre class="inline">.  
28  Attorney fee awards are calculated using the lodestar method, which multiplies the numbers of 
1  hours reasonably spent on the matter with a reasonable hourly rate.  Vogel v. Harbor Plaza Ctr., 
2  LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4287852"><a href="/opinion/4510599/martin-vogel-v-harbor-plaza-center-llc/#1160" aria-description="Citation for case: Martin Vogel v. Harbor Plaza Center, LLC">893 F.3d 1152, 1160</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018).  The district court has the discretion to make adjustments 
3  to the number of hours claimed or to the lodestar but is required to provide a clear but concise 
4  reason for the fee award.  Gates v. Deukmejian, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9007214"><a href="/opinion/9014163/gates-v-deukmejian/#1398" aria-description="Citation for case: Gates v. Deukmejian">987 F.2d 1392, 1398</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1992).  
5       Hourly Rate                                                      
6       As indicated, Plaintiff requests an hourly rate of $300.00 for work by Amy R. Lovegren-
7  Tipton, who has approximately 15 years of civil litigation experience.  (Doc. 20-3 ¶¶ 18-19.)  
8  Courts in this district have found similar rates reasonable.  See Roach v. Tate Publ&#x27;g &amp; Enters., 
9  No. 1:15-cv-00917-SAB, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 5070264</span><pre class="inline">, at *10 (E.D. Cal. Nov. 3, 2017) (awarding attorney 
10  with sixteen years of experience $325.00 per hour in copyright action); Trujillo v. GH Food Mart, 
11  Inc., No. 1:20-cv-00368-AWI-SKO, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2020 WL 4697139</span><pre class="inline">, at *7 (E.D. Cal. Aug. 13, 2020) (finding 
12  $300 to be a reasonably hourly rate in evaluating a motion for default judgment related to the 
13  Americans with Disabilities Act).  The Court therefore finds the requested hourly rate to be 
14  reasonable for Ms. Lovegren-Tipton.                                   
15       Attorney Time Expended                                           
16       Plaintiff seeks recovery for 18 hours of work performed by Ms. Lovegren-Tipton at 
17  $300.00 per hour.  (Doc. 20-3 ¶ 20.)  When considering the record in this matter, including the 
18  complaint, multiple service attempts, application for alternate service, and the instant motion for 
19  default judgment, the Court finds that the 18 hours billed by Ms. Lovegren-Tipton is reasonable.  
20  The Court will therefore recommend awarding Plaintiff $5,400.00 for 18 hours of work by Ms. 
21  Lovegren-Tipton to litigate this case.                                
22       Litigation Expenses and Costs                                    
23       Plaintiff requests recovery of litigation expenses and costs of $512.10, consisting of initial 
24  case filing fee of $402.00 and process service fees of $110.10.  (Doc. 20-3 ¶ 17.)  In a copyright 
25  infringement action, the “court in its discretion may allow the recovery of full costs by or against 
26  any party other than the United States or an officer thereof.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">17 U.S.C. § 505</span><pre class="inline">.  Plaintiff’s 
27  requested costs include the court filing fee and costs of service, which are compensable.  
28  Accordingly, the Court will recommend that Plaintiff be awarded the sum of $512.10 for 
1  litigation expenses and costs.
