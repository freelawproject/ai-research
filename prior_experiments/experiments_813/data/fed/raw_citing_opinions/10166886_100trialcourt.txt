16     II.  LEGAL STANDARD                                                
17        To survive a motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), “a 
18   complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to state a claim to relief 
19   that is plausible on its face.” Zixiang Li v. Kerry, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9505869"><a href="/opinion/856064/zixiang-li-v-john-f-kerry/#999" aria-description="Citation for case: Zixiang Li v. John F. Kerry">710 F.3d 995, 999</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2013). “A 
20   claim has facial plausibility when the pleaded factual content allows the court to draw the 
21   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Ashcroft v. 
22   Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009). “In evaluating a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the [C]ourt accepts 
23   the complaint’s well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draws all reasonable inferences 
24   in the light most favorable to the plaintiff.” Adams v. U.S. Forest Srvc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="622545"><a href="/opinion/622545/adams-v-united-states-forest-service/" aria-description="Citation for case: Adams v. United States Forest Service">671 F.3d 1138</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
25   1142–43 (9th Cir. 2012).                                             
26        Securities fraud claims, like the ones in this case, are also subject to the heightened 
27   pleading requirements of Rule 9(b) and the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act of 
28   1995 (“PSLRA”). In re Rigel Pharm., Inc. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="807992"><a href="/opinion/807992/inter-local-pension-fund-gcci-v-rigel-pharmaceuticals-inc/#876" aria-description="Citation for case: Inter-Local Pension Fund Gcc/i v. Rigel Pharmaceuticals,...">697 F.3d 869, 876</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012). 
1   Rule 9(b) “requires particularized allegations of the circumstances constituting fraud, 
2   including identifying the statements at issue and setting forth what is false or misleading 
3   . . . about the statement and why the statements were false or misleading at the time they 
4   were made.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="807992"><a href="/opinion/807992/inter-local-pension-fund-gcci-v-rigel-pharmaceuticals-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Inter-Local Pension Fund Gcc/i v. 14     IV.  DISCUSSION                                                    
15        The Court now turns to the merits of Sea’s motion to dismiss. Plaintiff asserts two 
16   claims: (1) violation of § 10(b) of the Exchange Act and Rule 10b-5 and (2) violation of 
17   § 20(a) of the Exchange Act. Both parties agree that the second claim’s fate depends on 
18   the Court’s resolution of the first.7 (Doc. 41 at 30; Doc. 47 at 30.) Accordingly, the Court 
19   analyzes the sufficiency of Plaintiff’s § 10(b) claim before turning to Plaintiff’s claim for 
20   control person liability under § 20(a).

24   (4) reliance  upon  the  misrepresentation  or  omission;  (5) economic  loss;  and  (6) loss 
25   causation.” Glazer Cap. Mgmt., L.P. v. Forescout Techs., Inc. (Glazer II), </pre><span class="citation no-link">63 F.4th 747</span><pre class="inline">,

    (See Doc. 42-9 at 5.)

1   re Mellanox Techs Ltd. Sec. Litig., No. 13-CV-04909-JST, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 12650991</span><pre class="inline">, at *17 
2   (N.D. Cal. Mar. 31, 2014). Granted, a duty to disclose does arise when an omission 
3   “affirmatively create[s] an impression of a state of affairs that differs in a material way 
4   from  the  one  that  actually  exists.”  See  Brody,  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="776588"><a href="/opinion/776588/jules-brody-joyce-t-crawford-v-transitional-hospitals-corporation-wendy/#1006" aria-description="Citation for case: Jules Brody Joyce T. Crawford v. Transitional Hospitals...">280  F.3d  at  1006</a></span><pre class="inline">.  But  in  this  case,

8   dismiss as to these statements.

         The Court is not aware of any Ninth Circuit case law discussing the disclosure 
28                                                                        
    obligations under Item 5(D) of Form 20-F. Nor does either side point to any relevant, 
1   authoritative case law. Courts in other circuits, however, have likened the disclosure 
2   obligations of Item 5(D) to Item 303 of Regulation S-K. See Sun v. Tal Educ. Grp., 22-cv-
3   01015, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 6394413</span><pre class="inline">, at *33 (S.D.N.Y. Sept. 29, 2023); see also Garnett v. RLX Tech 
4   Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">632 F. Supp. 3d 574</span><pre class="inline">, 598 (S.D.N.Y. 2022) (“Item 303 compels disclosure of ‘any 
5   known trends or uncertainties that have had or that are reasonably likely to have a material 
6   favorable or unfavorable impact on net sales or revenues or income from continuing 
7   operations.’ Likewise, Item 5(D) of Form 20-F requires that registrants ‘discuss . . . any 
8   known trends, uncertainties, demands, commitments or events that are reasonably likely to 
9   have a material effect on the company’s net sales or revenues, income from continuing 
10   operations,  profitability,  liquidity  or  capital  resources,  or  that  would  cause  reported 
11   financial information not necessarily to be indicative of future operating results or financial 
12   condition.’”). Accordingly, the Court looks to disclosures obligations under Item 303 as 
13   guidance for stating an adequate claim of misrepresentation as it relates to Item 5(D) for 
14   Form 20-F.                                                           
15        To state a claim for failure to adhere to Item 303, a plaintiff must plausibly allege 
16   “(1) the  existence  of  a  trend  or  uncertainty;  (2) known  to  management;  (3) that  is 
17   reasonably likely to have a material effect on the registrant’s financial condition or results 
18   of operation.” Steckman v. Hart Brewing, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6968477"><a href="/opinion/7064365/steckman-v-hart-brewing-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Steckman v. Hart Brewing, Inc.">143 F.3d 1293</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1296–97 (9th Cir. 1998). A 
19   “trend” is defined as an “observed pattern that accurately reflects persistent conditions of 
20   the particular registrant’s business environment.” In re Restoration Robotics, Inc. Sec. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">21   Litig.,  417</span><pre class="inline">  F.  Supp.  3d  1242,  1263  (N.D.  Cal.  2019).  Here,  Plaintiff  maintains  that 
22   Defendants were aware of the “ongoing trend of declining GMV” and that Defendants 
23   failed  to  affirmatively  disclose  this  trend,  thereby  misleading  investors.  Plaintiff’s 
24   allegations adequately support this.                                 
25        To  start,  Plaintiff’s  allegations  support  a  plausible  inference  that  at  the  time 
26   Defendants  filed  Sea’s  Form  20-F,  Shopee  was  experiencing  an  “ongoing  trend  of 
27   declining GMV.” Given that Defendants halted quarterly disclosures of GMV for 1Q23 
28   and 2Q23, Plaintiff is unable to point to those metrics to support its allegation of downward 
1   trending growth. Instead, Plaintiff points to other allegations, which the Court accepts as 
2   true, that create a reasonable inference that such a trend existed. For instance, Plaintiff 
3   notes that:                                                          
4             Shopee’s  GMV  and  sales  and  marketing  expense  moved  
              practically in lockstep with each other prior to and during the 
5             Class Period. In 2020 and 2021, both metrics grew sequentially 
              each quarter, as the Company’s massive use of promotions and 
6             other  marketing  tactics  drove  consistent  GMV  growth.  In 
              2022, however, as the Company embarked on Defendants’      
7             aggressive  cost-cutting  initiative  and  slashed  hundreds  of 
              millions of dollars in quarterly sales and marketing expenses, 
8             Shopee’s GMV similarly began to decelerate and then decline 
              – before Defendants abruptly stopped disclosing the metric 
9             altogether for the first two quarters of 2023.             
10                                                                        
    (Doc. 31 ¶ 51.) Plaintiff further provides a graphic to illustrate the point: as Shopee’s sales 
11                                                                        
    and marketing expense increased from 1Q20 to 4Q21, so too did Shopee’s GMV. And as 
12                                                                        
    sales and marketing decreased starting in 4Q21, so too did GMV decelerate and then 
13                                                                        
    decline. (Id.) Thus, accepting as true that (1) GMV and Shopee’s sales and marketing 
14                                                                        
    expense moved in lockstep and (2) Shopee’s sales and marketing expense declined from 
15                                                                        
    3Q22 to 1Q23, it is plausible that Shopee’s GMV was also downward trending during this 
16                                                                        
    time. In turn, by failing to disclose this trend—one that was reasonably likely to affect 
17                                                                        
    Sea’s financial condition—Defendants misled investors.

21

22

23

1        Plaintiff argues these statements are misleading because Defendants knew at the 
2   time they made these remarks that (1) Shopee had already reported a deceleration in GMV 
3   with its smallest ever quarter-to-quarter GMV increase in 3Q22, (2) Shopee subsequently 
4   reported the largest GMV decline in its history with further significant declines in sales 
5   and market expense in 4Q22, and (3) Shopee has experienced further undisclosed declines 
6   in GMV in 1Q23 alongside further reductions in sales and marketing expense.  
7        Again, Defendants’ statements remarking on Sea’s effort to establish “profitable 
8   long-term growth” and its ability to “execute growth and manag[e] bottom line at the same 
9   time” are forward-looking statements that qualify for safe harbor protection. They fail to 
10   provide an actionable basis for a securities fraud claim, and so the Court grants Defendants’ 
11   motion to dismiss them.                                              
12        However,  Defendants’  statements  regarding  Shopee’s  GMV  trends  remaining 
13   “consistent in terms of seasonality” are actionable. These are neither vague, unspecific 
14   remarks of corporate optimism nor predictive, forwarding-looking statements entitled to 
15   safe harbor protection. See In re Quality Sys., 865 F.3d at 1144–45 (finding that defendant’s 
16   reassurances to investors that sales were “unchanged, or even growing, compared to 
17   previous quarters” was a “concrete description,” not a “feel good optimistic statement[]”). 
18   Plaintiff plausibly asserts that in refusing to disclose GMV and by stating that GMV trends 
19   were “in line with the overall seasonality trends,” Defendants painted a misleading picture 
20   of Shopee’s growth—one that improperly concealed Shopee’s ongoing GMV decline. 
21   Accordingly, the Court will not grant Defendants’ motion to dismiss as to these statements.  
22          B. Scienter                                                   
23        Before proceeding with its scienter analysis and to better frame the foregoing 
24   discussion, the Court summarizes which of Plaintiff’s allegations sufficiently plead falsity 
25   and which do not. First, Plaintiff’s allegations regarding Garena sufficiently plead falsity. 
26   Specifically, Plaintiff has adequately pled that Defendants made misleading statements 
27   about the impact of Garena losing its licensing right to League of Legends and Teamfight 
28   Tactics. Second, only some of Plaintiff’s allegations regarding Shopee sufficiently plead 
1   falsity. Plaintiff has adequately pled that (1) Sea’s Form F-20 for FY22 misled investors 
2   by failing to disclose Shopee’s downward trending GMV and (2) Defendants’ misled 
3   investors on the 1Q23 earnings call (May 16, 2023) by claiming that Shopee’s GMV was 
4   “in line with seasonality trends” while also omitting the fact that Shopee’s GMV was 
5   experiencing  an  ongoing  decline.  However,  the  following  allegations  either  are 
6   inactionable or fail to adequately plead falsity: (1) Defendants’ Shopee-related statements 
7   on the 3Q22 earnings call (November 15, 2022) (Doc. 31 ¶ 70(b–c)); (2) Defendants’ 
8   Shopee-related statements on the 4Q22 earnings call (March 7, 2023) (¶ 78(b–e)); and 
9   (3) Defendants’ statements on the 1Q23 earnings call (May 16, 2023) regarding Sea’s 
10   potential for “profitable long-term growth” and its ability to “execute growth and manag[e] 
11   bottom line at the same time” (¶ 90(a)). Accordingly, the Court analyzes scienter only as it 
12   pertains to the surviving allegedly false statements.                
13        The PSLRA requires the complaint “state with particularity facts giving rise to a 
14   strong inference that the defendant” made the “false or misleading statements either 
15   intentionally or with deliberate recklessness.” Siracusano v. Matrixx Initiatives, Inc., 585 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="663176"><a href="/opinion/663176/margaret-taylor-v-director-office-of-workers-compensation-programs/#1180" aria-description="Citation for case: Margaret Taylor v. Director, Office of Workers&#x27;...">16   F.3d 1167, 1180</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2009). “[A]n inference of scienter must be more than merely 
17   plausible or reasonable—it must be cogent and at least as compelling as any opposing 
18   inference of nonfraudulent intent.” Tellabs, Inc v. Makor Issues &amp; Rights, Ltd., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435058"><a href="/opinion/145726/uttecht-v-brown/#308" aria-description="Citation for case: Uttecht v. Brown">551 U.S. 
19   308, 324</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007). That said, “[t]he inference that the defendant acted with scienter need not 
20   be irrefutable, i.e., of the smoking-gun genre, or even the most plausible of competing 
21   inference.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435058"><a href="/opinion/145726/uttecht-v-brown/" aria-description="Citation for case: Uttecht v. Brown">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (internal quotation marks omitted). Rather, “[t]he inquiry . . . is whether all 
22   of the facts alleged, taken collectively, give rise to a strong inference of scienter, not 
23   whether any individual allegation, scrutinized in isolation, meets the standard.” </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435058"><a href="/opinion/145726/uttecht-v-brown/" aria-description="Citation for case: Uttecht v. Brown">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 322–
24   23.                                                                  
25        Plaintiff brings its § 10(b) claim only against Li, Wang, Chen, and Sea. Because Sea 
26   is a corporation, it “can only act through its employees and agents and can likewise only 
27   have scienter through them.” In re ChinaCast Educ. Corp. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3149038"><a href="/opinion/3149038/costa-brava-partnership-iii-lp-v-chinacast-education-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Costa Brava Partnership III LP v. Chinacast Education Corp.">809 F.3d 471</a></span><pre class="inline">, 475 
28   (9th Cir. 2015). The “scienter of senior controlling officers of a corporation may be 
1   attributed to the corporation itself to establish liability as a primary violator of § 10(b) and 
2   Rule 10b-5 when those senior officials were acting within the scope of their apparent 
3   authority.” Id. Sea does not dispute that Li, Wang, and Chen were acting within the scope 
4   of their authority. As such, the Court’s analysis focuses on whether Plaintiff had adequately 
5   alleged scienter as to Li, Wang, and Chen.                           
6        Starting first with Li and Wang: Plaintiff posits that it has sufficiently alleged with 
7   particularly  that  Li  and  Wang  had  actual  knowledge  of  the  subjects  of  the  alleged 
8   misstatements—that is, the importance of League of Legends to Garena’s business and 
9   Shopee’s downward trending GMV in light of Sea’s pivot to profitability. Plaintiff further 
10   contends that given the importance of both League of Legends and Shopee’s GMV to Sea’s 
11   core operations, it would be absurd to argue that Li and Wang were unaware of both 
12   matters. The Court agrees.                                           
13        Under  the  “core  operations”  theory  of  scienter,  “[a]llegations  regarding 
14   management’s  role  may  help  satisfy  the  PSLRA  scienter  requirements  in  three 
15   circumstances:                                                       
16             First, the allegations may be viewed holistically, along with 
              other allegations in the complaint, to raise a strong inference of 
17             scienter under the Tellabs standard. Second the allegations may 
              independently satisfy the PSLRA where they are particular and 
18             suggest  that  defendants  had  actual  access  to  the  disputed 
              information . . . . Third, in rare circumstances, such allegations 
19             may  be  sufficient,  without  accompanying  particularized 
              allegations, where the nature of the relevant fact is of such 
20             prominence  that  it  would  be  “absurd”  to  suggest  that 
              management was without knowledge of the matter.            
21                                                                        
22   Reese v. Malone, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3066145"><a href="/opinion/3066145/claude-reese-v-robert-malone/#572" aria-description="Citation for case: Claude Reese v. Robert Malone">747 F.3d 557, 572</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014) (internal citations and quotation marks 
23   omitted).                                                            
24        Here,  Plaintiff’s allegations, which the Court takes as true, demonstrates  how 
25   critical League of Legends was to Garena’s success—so much so that it would be “absurd” 
26   to argue that Defendants were unaware of—or, as Sea contends in its motion to dismiss, 
27   “did not expect”—the impact the expiration of the game’s license agreement would have 
28   on Garena’s business. For instance, Plaintiff alleges that in the years leading up to the 
1   expiration of license agreement at the end of 2022, Sea’s annual report on Form 20-F 
2   consistently listed League of Legends among Garena’s most popular licensed games. To 
3   be specific: Sea listed League of Legends as one of its most popular games in FY17, FY18, 
4   FY19, FY20, and FY21. (Doc. 31 ¶ 45.) These same forms also reported that Garena’s top 
5   five games accounted for more than 95% of Sea’s digital entertainment revenue from 2020 
6   to 2022. (Id.)                                                       
7        But League of Legends wasn’t just one of Sea’s most popular games—it was and 
8   continues to be one of the most popular and monetized video games in the world. In 2022, 
9   the last year Garena retained its right to publish the game, League of Legends had more 
10   than 180 million monthly players and was the second most-viewed live-streamed video 
11   game globally, with its League of Legends World Championship drawing 5.1 million 
12   concurrent viewers and becoming the second most-watched event in esports history at that 
13   time (¶ 41). See Di Donato v. Insys Therapeutics Inc., No. CV-16-00302-PHX-NWV, 2017 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">14   WL 3268797</span><pre class="inline">, at *15 (D. Ariz. Aug. 1, 2017) (finding it implausible that defendant-CEO 
15   would not have known that most of the company’s revenue was coming from a specific 
16   prescription drug).                                                  
17        Granted, the importance of League of Legends to Garena’s core operations is not 
18   alone sufficient to establish scienter. Rather, to establish scienter under the core operations 
19   theory, a plaintiff must produce either “admissions by one or more corporate executives of 
20   detailed involvement in the minutia of a company’s operations, such as data monitoring,” 
21   or “witness accounts demonstrating that executives had actual involvement in creating false 
22   reports.” Police Ret. Sys. of St. Louis v. Intuitive Surgical, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2683964"><a href="/opinion/2683964/police-retire-sys-of-st-louis-v-intuitive-surgical-inc/#1062" aria-description="Citation for case: Police Retire. Sys of St Louis v. Intuitive Surgical, Inc.">759 F.3d 1051, 1062</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th 
23   Cir. 2014). Plaintiff sufficiently pleads the former.                
24        Plaintiff  alleges  that  throughout  the  Class  Period,  Li  and  Wang  regularly 
25   acknowledged that they tracked Garena’s user engagement, monetization metrics, and 
26   trends for specific games on a granular basis (Doc. 31 ¶ 106). Indeed, Garena’s license 
27   agreement itself—to  which Li served  as Garena’s  signatory—required Defendants to 
28   maintain and provide Riot/Tencent with monthly and quarterly reports on the game’s 
1   revenue and usage. (Doc. 42-2 at 23.)                                
2        Moreover, Defendants’ allegedly misleading statements themselves further bolster 
3   the inference that Defendants were aware of the import of League of Legends on Garena’s 
4   business. (See e.g., Doc. 31 ¶¶ 65, 74, 76.)  Take, for instance, Wang’s statements on the 
5   3Q22 earnings call, in which he fielded questions regarding the impact of the license 
6   agreement’s  expiration  and  explicitly  represented  that  the  game’s  contribution  is 
7   “immaterial”  to  Garena’s  publishing  business.  Or,  for  another  example,  take  Li’s 
8   statements on the 4Q22 earnings call, in which he assured investors that Garena’s paying 
9   user ratio and average revenue per user remained relatively stable. Among the possible 
10   inferences that could be drawn from these statements, the more compelling one is that 
11   Wang and Li were aware of Garena’s key operating metrics and had access to information 
12   regarding the impact the license agreement’s expiration would have on Garena’s business. 
13   Reese, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3066145"><a href="/opinion/3066145/claude-reese-v-robert-malone/#572" aria-description="Citation for case: Claude Reese v. Robert Malone">747 F.3d at 572</a></span><pre class="inline"> (finding that an assertion that a defendant was unaware of an alleged 
14   issue can be “directly contradicted by the fact that [the defendant] specifically addressed it 
15   in [his] statements”).                                               
16        Li and Wang’s respective leadership roles also support scienter. See In re Alphabet, 
17   Inc. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation no-link">1 F.4th 687</span><pre class="inline">, 706 (9th Cir. 2021) (“[W]e may consider a senior executive’s 
18   role in a company to determine whether there is a cogent and compelling inference that the 
19   senior executive knew of the information at issue. This includes consideration of the 
20   executive’s  access  to  the  information,  and,  whether,  given  the  importance  of  the 
21   information, it would be absurd to suggest that management was without knowledge of the 
22   matter.”) Li and Wang are both among the highest-level executives at Sea. Li is one of 
23   Sea’s cofounders and serves as its CEO and Chairman of the Board of Directors. (Doc. 31 
24   ¶ 16.) And Wang is Sea’s Chief Corporate Officer, Chief General Counsel, and Company 
25   Secretary. Wang and Li regularly attended and actively participated in Sea meetings and 
26   earnings call. (¶¶ 101–02.) Sea’s Form F-20 for FY22 also states that Li “has control over 
27   key decision making as a result of his control of a majority of the voting power of our 
28   outstanding share capital and has substantial influence over our company.” (¶ 98.)  
1        Thus, piecing all these allegations together—that League of Legends was a key-
2   revenue earning game leading up to 2022, that Li and Wang had access to and were aware 
3   of League of Legends’ user engagement and monetization metrics, and that Li and Wang 
4   were among the highest-ranked executives—the Court finds Plaintiff’s CAC raises a strong 
5   inference of Li and Wang’s scienter as it pertains to the Garena-related misstatements. 
6        Plaintiff’s allegations also support Li and Wang’s scienter with respect to Shopee.  
7   Similar to the analysis above, Plaintiff’s allegations present as a strong argument that GMV 
8   was a key metric for Shopee and that it would be “absurd” for Defendants not to have been 
9   aware of its trending decline in light of Sea’s “pivot to profitability.”  
10        To start, Plaintiff alleges that Shopee’s revenue is a function of GMV (gross 
11   merchandise sold over a given period of time) and fees charged. See Shenwick, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8826451"><a href="/opinion/8841277/in-re-hub-carpet-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Hub Carpet Co.">282 F. 
12</a></span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d at 1146 (finding that it would be absurd for defendants not to have been aware of 
13   a specific metric’s adverse trends given the metric’s “direct impact on Twitter’s ability to 
14   generate revenue”). Since its inception as a publicly-traded company, Sea has regularly 
15   reported its GMV on a quarterly basis—the only exception being 1Q23 and 2Q23, the two 
16   quarters succeeding the largest GMV decline in Shopee’s history. (Doc. 31 ¶ 32.) The 
17   importance of GMV as a key operating metric is further illustrated by the fact the GMV is 
18   one of only two operative metrics that Sea reports in its SEC filings for Shopee. (Doc. 42-
19   1 at 49.)                                                            
20        Plaintiff also points to specific admissions by Li and Wang that they were aware of 
21   Shopee’s GMV and how it was trending throughout the Class Period. (See e.g., Doc. 31 ¶ 
22   107.) Take for instance, Wang’s remarks on the 1Q23 earnings call, in which he stated: “In 
23   terms of GMV . . . we don’t discuss quarter-on-quarter GMV numbers specifically. And 
24   what I mentioned though was that the trend we’re observing, the general trend Q-on-Q 
25   observed for this quarter versus last quarter is in line as what we observed in Q1 last year 
26   versus Q4 2021.” (¶ 87.) Wang’s ability to comment on how Shopee’s GMV was trending 
27   as compared to years prior while also refusing to disclose specific numbers to investors 
28   raises a strong inference of scienter. See Shenwick, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7245690"><a href="/opinion/7327774/shenwick-v-twitter-inc/#1147" aria-description="Citation for case: Shenwick v. Twitter, Inc.">282 F. Supp. 3d at 1147</a></span><pre class="inline"> (finding that 
1   although defendant “did not provide ‘specific’ data” about a key metric, defendant’s 
2   statement regarding the metric’s dependence on market forces strongly implied that he had 
3   access to the disputed information). Indeed, Wang “bridge[d] the [scienter] gap” himself 
4   by referencing the very metric that Sea argues Defendants lacked “actual access” to. See 
5   Reese, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3066145"><a href="/opinion/3066145/claude-reese-v-robert-malone/#572" aria-description="Citation for case: Claude Reese v. Robert Malone">747 F.3d at 572</a></span><pre class="inline">.                                              
6        And although Defendants’ decision to withhold quarterly GMV numbers for 1Q23 
7   and 2Q23 may not itself be an actionable misrepresentation as currently pled in Plaintiff’s 
8   CAC,  the  allegation  further  supports  scienter.  That  Defendants  decided  to  begin 
9   withholding this metric for the first time in Shopee’s history immediately after Shopee 
10   experienced its largest GMV decline in history bolsters the strong inference that Li and 
11   Wang,  at  a  minimum,  were  reckless  in  their  statements  and  omissions  to  investors 
12   regarding Shopee’s downward trending GMV. This is sufficient to survive a motion to 
13   dismiss.                                                             
14        Turning now to Chen: Plaintiff names Chen as having violated § 10(b) but, notably, 
15   does not attribute a single misleading statement to him during the Class Period. To the 
16   extent that Plaintiff asserts Chen’s liability by virtue of his position as an executive officer 
17   of the company, district courts within this circuit have largely rejected the “group pleading 
18   doctrine” as a means of establishing falsity under § 10(b). See In re Questcor Sec. Litig., 
19   </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 WL 5486762</span><pre class="inline">, No. SA CV 12-01623 DMG, at *9 (C.D. Cal. Oct. 1, 2023) (surveying 
20   cases). Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that “the maker of a statement is the person 
21   or entity with ultimate authority over the statement, including its content and whether and
