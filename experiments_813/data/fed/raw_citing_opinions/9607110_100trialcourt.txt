13        Now before the Court for consideration is the motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s First Amended 
14   Complaint (“FAC”) submitted by Defendants Amazon.com, Inc. and Audible, Inc. 
15   (“Defendants”).  The Court has considered the parties’ papers, relevant legal authority, and the 
16   record in this case, and it finds this matter suitable for disposition without oral argument.  See 
17   N.D. Civ. L.R. 7-1(b).  For the following reasons, the Court GRANTS Defendants’ motion with 
18   leave to amend.                                                       
19                              BACKGROUND                                 
20        Plaintiff Julia Heck (“Plaintiff”) alleges that Amazon subscribes its Prime members to a 
21   paid Audible membership by offering Prime members free digital credits in exchange for delayed 
22   Amazon Prime delivery (the “No-Rush Shipping Program”).  (Dkt. No. 21 (“FAC”) ¶ 15.)  
23   Plaintiff alleges Amazon uses the customer’s agreement to the “No-Rush Shipping Program” to 
24   enroll the customer in a “free trial” membership with Audible.  (Id.)  After the “free trial” ends, 
25   Amazon passes the Audible credit card information on file to Audible, allowing Audible to charge 
26   monthly subscription fees for its services.  (Id.)  Plaintiff alleges the inadvertent Audible 
27   membership is not adequately disclosed to Prime members and as a result, it can take months 
1        In March of 2021, Plaintiff Julia Heck (“Plaintiff”) made several purchases with Amazon 
2   through her Amazon Prime account.  (Id.  ¶ 9.)  At the time of purchase, Amazon gave Plaintiff 
3   the option to wait an additional two days for her delivery in exchange for free digital credits from 
4   Amazon (the “No-Rush Shipping Program.”)  (Id.)  Plaintiff agreed to the delayed delivery so she 
5   could receive the free digital credits.  (Id.)  Plaintiff alleges Amazon used the “No-Rush Shipping 
6   Program” to “surreptitiously enroll Plaintiff in an Audible account and gave Audible Plaintiff’s 
7   credit card information on file with Amazon.”  (Id.)  Plaintiff alleges Audible improperly charged 
8   her a monthly fee of $14.95 for an Audible subscription for a total of $59.80 until she discovered 
9   the error and cancelled her Audible subscription.  (Id.)              
10        Based on these allegations, Plaintiff brings two causes of action for violations of 
11   California’s Consumer Legal Remedies Act, Cal Civ. Code section 1750, et seq. (“CLRA”) and 
12   California’s Unfair Competition Law, Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code section 17200 et seq. (“UCL”).  
13                               ANALYSIS                                  
14   A.   Applicable Legal Standard.                                       
15        A motion to dismiss is proper under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) where the 
16   pleadings fail to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.  A court’s “inquiry is limited to 
17   the allegations in the complaint, which are accepted as true and construed in the light most 
18   favorable to the plaintiff.”  Lazy Y Ranch Ltd. v. Behrens, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1361176"><a href="/opinion/1361176/lazy-y-ranch-ltd-v-behrens/#588" aria-description="Citation for case: Lazy Y Ranch Ltd. v. Behrens">546 F.3d 580, 588</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).  Even 
19   under the liberal pleading standard of Rule 8(a)(2), “a plaintiff’s obligation to provide ‘grounds’ of 
20   his ‘entitle[ment] to relief’ requires more than labels and conclusions, and formulaic recitation of 
21   the elements of a cause of action will not do.”  Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544</a></span><pre class="inline">, 555 
22   (2007) (citing Papasan v. Allain, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9430664"><a href="/opinion/111742/papasan-v-allain/#286" aria-description="Citation for case: Papasan v. Allain">478 U.S. 265, 286</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1986)).  Pursuant to Twombly, a plaintiff 
23   cannot merely allege conduct that is conceivable but must instead allege “enough facts to state a 
24   claim to relief that is plausible on its face.”  Id. at 570.  “A claim has facial plausibility when the 
25   plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the 
26   defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) 
27   (citing Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#556" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 556</a></span><pre class="inline">).                                    
1   ruling on Rule 12(b)(6) motion.”  Branch v. Tunnell, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="660713"><a href="/opinion/660713/jerry-l-branch-valenna-branch-colby-branch-v-dale-l-tunnell/#453" aria-description="Citation for case: Jerry L. Branch, Valenna Branch, Colby Branch v. Dale L....">14 F.3d 449, 453</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1994), overruled 
2   on other grounds by Galbraith v. County of Santa Clara, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7014886"><a href="/opinion/7108812/galbraith-v-county-of-santa-clara/" aria-description="Citation for case: Galbraith v. County of Santa Clara">307 F.3d 1119</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2002) (citation 
3   omitted).  However, a court may consider documents that subject to judicial notice on a motion to 
4   dismiss without converting the motion to one for summary judgment.  See Mack S. Bay Beer 
5   Distrib., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="474911"><a href="/opinion/474911/henry-g-mack-v-south-bay-beer-distributors-inc-dba-bay-beer/#1282" aria-description="Citation for case: Henry G. Mack v. South Bay Beer Distributors, Inc., Dba...">798 F.2d 1279, 1282</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1986), overruled on other grounds by Astoria Fed. Sav. &amp; 
6   Loan Ass’n v. Solimino, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="112618"><a href="/opinion/112618/astoria-federal-savings-loan-assn-v-solimino/" aria-description="Citation for case: Astoria Federal Savings &amp; Loan Ass&#x27;n v. Solimino">501 U.S. 104</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1991).  If the allegations are insufficient to state a claim, a 
7   court should grant leave to amend unless amendment would be futile.  See, e.g., Reddy v. Litton 
8   Indus. Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8415252"><a href="/opinion/8443826/meridian-capital-cis-fund-v-burton-in-re-buccaneer-res-llc/#296" aria-description="Citation for case: Meridian Capital CIS Fund v. Burton (In re Buccaneer...">912 F.3d 291, 296</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1990); Cook, Perkiss &amp; Liehe, Inc. v. N. Cal. Collection 
9   Serv., Inc., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.2d/911/242/">911 F.2d 242</a></span><pre class="inline">, 246-47 (9th Cir. 1990).                    
10   B.   Plaintiff Failed to Comply with the CLRA’s Pre-Suit Notice Requirement.   
11        Defendants contend that Plaintiff’s CLRA claim is procedurally defective.  A CLRA 
12   plaintiff seeking monetary damages must give notice to the defendant of the alleged violations at 
13   least 30 days before filing suit, and “[d]emand that the person correct, repair, replace, or otherwise 
14   rectify the goods or services alleged to be in violation of Section 1770.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1782</span><pre class="inline">(a).  
15   “The CLRA’s notice requirement is not jurisdictional, but compliance with the requirement is 
16   necessary to state a claim” for damages.  Keilholtz v. Superior Fireplace Co., No. 08-cv-836 CW, 
17   </pre><span class="citation no-link">2009 WL 839076</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Mar.30, 2009) (citing Outboard Marine Corp. v. Sup. Ct., 52 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="2118485"><a href="/opinion/2118485/stern-v-superior-court/#40" aria-description="Citation for case: Stern v. Superior Court">18   Cal.App.3d 30, 40-41</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1975)).  “The purpose of the notice requirement ... is to give the 
19   manufacturer or vendor sufficient notice of alleged defects to permit appropriate corrections or 
20   replacements,” and this “purpose may only be accomplished by a literal application of the notice 
21   provisions.”  Outboard Marine, 52 Cal.App.3d at 40-41.                
22        Plaintiff’s pre-suit notice to Amazon failed to comply with the CLRA’s pre-suit notice 
23   requirement.  Plaintiff sent Amazon a letter on March 11, 2022 notifying Amazon that it violated 
24   the CLRA because it “marketed, labeled, and advertised—and continues to market, label, and 
25   advertise—[Audible] as free with a Prime subscription.”  Dkt. No. 36-1, Declaration of Jedediah 
26   Wakefield, Ex. 1.  However, Plaintiff abandoned that theory of liability, which was advanced in 
27   her original complaint, in her FAC.  She now claims Amazon violated the CLRA because it used 
1   Audible improperly charged Plaintiff a monthly subscription fee.  The March 11, 2022 pre-suit 
2   notice letter did not give Amazon sufficient notice of this alleged violation to permit corrections. 
3        Plaintiff also failed to provide pre-suit notice to Audible.  Plaintiff contends because she 
4   sent notice to Amazon, she satisfied the CLRA pre-suit notice requirement as to Audible because 
5   Audible is a wholly owned subsidiary of Amazon and is represented by the same attorneys.  
6   Plaintiff provides no authority supporting this proposition, and the Court finds she cannot rely on 
7   the notice she sent to Amazon to satisfy the requirement as to Audible.  See Herron v Best Buy 
8   Stores, L.P., No. 12-cv-02103-GEB-JEM, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 465906</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (E.D. Cal. Feb. 4, 2014) 
9   (rejecting argument that plaintiff satisfied CLRA notice requirement by sending notice to the 
10   defendant’s parent company).  Further, the letter Plaintiff sent to Audible on October 5, 2022 is 
11   insufficient to satisfy the pre-suit notice requirement because, like the letter to Amazon, it states 
12   the CLRA violation is based on the marketing, labeling, and advertising of Audible as free with an 
13   Amazon Prime Subscription; it does not notify Audible of the “No-Rush Shipping Program” 
14   enrollment theory.  See Dkt. No. 44-1, Declaration of Sue J. Nam, Ex. B.  Additionally, Plaintiff 
15   sent her letter to Audible on October 5, 2022, two months after she filed the FAC.   
16        The Court grants Defendants’ motion to dismiss the CLRA cause of action for damages for 
17   failing to comply with the CLRA’s pre-suit notice requirement.1  The Court will allow Plaintiff 
18   leave to amend to correct the errors in her pre-suit notice.          
19   C.   Plaintiff Fails to Allege an Actionable Misrepresentation or Omission.  
20        Plaintiff’s claims are based on the theory that Amazon deceived its customers by failing to 
21   disclose that choosing the “No-Rush Shipping Program” when making a purchase with Amazon 
22   Prime would cause the customer to be enrolled in and charged for an Audible subscription.  
23   Because Plaintiff’s claims under the UCL and CLRA sound in fraud, they must meet the 
24   particularity standards of Rule 9(b).2  Kearns v. Ford Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208595"><a href="/opinion/1208595/kearns-v-ford-motor-co/#1125" aria-description="Citation for case: Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.">567 F.3d 1120, 1125</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
25                                                                         
    1 Plaintiff seeks damages and injunctive relief under the CLRA.  The notice requirement applies 
26   only to claims for damages.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1782</span><pre class="inline">(d) (“An action for injunctive relief brough 
    under the specific provisions of Section 1770 may be commenced without compliance with 
27   subdivision (a).”).                                                   
1   2009) (noting that UCL claims under the fraudulent prong must meet the heightened pleading 
2   standards of Rule 9(b); see also Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1106" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d 1097, 1106</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
3   2003) (“Averments of fraud must be accompanied by ‘the who, what, when, where, and how of 
4   the misconduct charged.’”).  The allegations “must be specific enough to give defendants notice of 
5   the particular misconduct which is alleged to constitute the fraud charged so that they can defend 
6   against the charge and not just deny that they have done anything wrong.”  Swartz v. KPMG LLP, 
7   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="796813"><a href="/opinion/796813/theodore-c-swartz-v-kpmg-llp-and-presidio-advisory-services-inc/#764" aria-description="Citation for case: Theodore C. Swartz v. Kpmg Llp, and Presidio Advisory...">476 F.3d 756, 764</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2007).                                    
8        Plaintiff’s allegations regarding Amazon’s alleged misrepresentation fall short of 9(b).  
9   Plaintiff’s conclusory labels of “who, what, where, when, and how” in the FAC lack the factual 
10   support to meet the pleading requirements of Rule 8(a) and Rule 9(b).  Although Plaintiff alleges 
11   Amazon and Audible are the “who,” she offers no facts regarding Audible’s role in the alleged 
12   fraud.  The FAC also fails to allege the “what” or “how” of the alleged fraudulent scheme.  
13   Plaintiff fails to plausibly allege how the “No-Rush Shipping Program” relates to Audible 
14   enrollment.  She alleges no facts connecting the “No-Rush Shipping Program” to Audible 
15   enrollment, and thus, she has failed to show that her theory of fraud is plausible, let alone meet 
16   Rule 9(b)’s more stringent pleading standard.  Plaintiff also fails to specify what the alleged 
17   misstatement or misstatements said or provide any context for the alleged misrepresentations, 
18   including when or where Plaintiff saw them.   Even if she had, Plaintiff does not allege that she 
19   did not receive the digital free credits promised with the “No-Rush Shipping Program” or that the 
20   credits failed to work as promised.  Thus, the FAC fails to sufficiently allege that Amazon’s offer 
21   of free digital credits through its “No-Rush Shipping Program” was false or misleading.  Nor does 
22   Plaintiff allege facts showing how her agreement to receive “No Rush Shipping” in exchange for 
23   free digital credits from Amazon led to her enrollment in Audible.  Indeed, the FAC is devoid of 
24   facts about the Audible enrollment process and thus, Plaintiff fails to establish that the enrollment 
25   process is likely to deceive reasonable consumers.                    
26        Plaintiff also fails to sufficiently allege an actionable omission.  Omissions are actionable 
27                                                                         
1   only when the omission is contrary to a representation actually made by the defendant or where a 
2   duty to disclose exists.  Keegan v. Am. Honda Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8695658"><a href="/opinion/8712468/keegan-v-american-honda-motor-co/#939" aria-description="Citation for case: Keegan v. American Honda Motor Co.">838 F.Supp.2d 929, 939</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 
3   2012).  A duty to disclose can arise “when the defendant makes partial representations but also 
4   suppresses some material facts.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8695658"><a href="/opinion/8712468/keegan-v-american-honda-motor-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Keegan v. American Honda Motor Co.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Smith v. Ford Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2473126"><a href="/opinion/2473126/smith-v-ford-motor-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Ford Motor Co.">749 F.Supp.2d 980</a></span><pre class="inline">, 987 
5   (N.D. Cal. 2010)).  Omissions-based claims must be pled with particularity.  Watkins v. MGA Ent., 
6   Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">550 F. Supp. 3d 815</span><pre class="inline">, 833 (N.D. Cal. 2021) (“Rule 9(b) requires the plaintiff to allege the 
7   particular circumstances surrounding the representations at issue…regardless of whether the 
8   statements at issue are misleading because they are affirmative misrepresentations or because they 
9   contain material omissions.”).                                        
10        Plaintiff alleges that Defendants made partial representations about free digital credits but 
11   failed to disclose material information about the supposedly “free” credits, namely that accepting 
12   the credits would cause Plaintiff to be enrolled in a paid subscription to Audible.  Plaintiff’s 
13   omissions-based theory suffers from the same flaws as her theory based on affirmative 
14   misrepresentations.  Plaintiff has not pled with particularity what partial representations 
15   Defendants made or what facts Defendants supposedly omitted including how the “No-Rush 
16   Shipping Program” led Plaintiff to believe Audible would be free or how choosing the No-Rush 
17   Shipping Program led to her enrollment in Audible in the first place.     
18        Plaintiff claims she cannot plead the specifics of the misrepresentations or omissions 
19   because Defendants “failed to disclose anything about an Audible subscription in connection with 
20   the ‘No-Rush Shipping Program.’”  (Opp. at 5 (emphasis in original.))  This argument is 
21   unpersuasive.  There are insufficient facts in the FAC to permit the Court to conclude that 
22   choosing Amazon’s “No-Rush Shipping Program” leads to an unauthorized Audible subscription 
23   enrollment.  It is insufficient for Plaintiff to label the scheme as “surreptitious” without more.  
24   Rather, Plaintiff must plead specific facts that explain the mechanism connecting the “No-Rush 
25   Shipping Program” and enrollment in Audible before she can plausibly plead her theory of fraud.    
26        Plaintiff’s reliance on In re Apple In-App Purchase Litigation, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8697845"><a href="/opinion/8714652/in-re-apple-in-app-purchase-litigation/" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Apple In-App Purchase Litigation">855 F.Supp.2d 1030</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">27   Cal. 2012</span><pre class="inline">) is unpersuasive.  In that case, the plaintiffs alleged Apple violated the CLRA and UCL 
1   knowledge or permission while playing gaming apps advertised as free.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8697845"><a href="/opinion/8714652/in-re-apple-in-app-purchase-litigation/#1033" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Apple In-App Purchase Litigation">Id. at 1033</a></span><pre class="inline">.  At the 
2   pleading stage, the court permitted the CLRA and UCL claims to proceed finding that the 
3   plaintiffs pled specific facts detailing the misrepresentations they were exposed to, the information 
4   that was allegedly omitted, their reliance on those misrepresentations and omissions, and the 
5   resulting harm.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8697845"><a href="/opinion/8714652/in-re-apple-in-app-purchase-litigation/#1039" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Apple In-App Purchase Litigation">Id. at 1039</a></span><pre class="inline">.  That is not the case here, where Plaintiff has failed to plead specific 
6   facts to support her conclusory assertions of fraud.                  
7        For these reasons, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged the 
8   misrepresentations and omissions that are the basis for her CLRA and UCL claims and thus fails 
9   to state a claim under the UCL and CLRA.                              
10        Plaintiff also makes a passing reference to a claim under the “unfair” prong of the UCL, 
11   which she alleges is based on the same “actions, omissions, misrepresentations, practices, and 
12   non-disclosures” as her fraud claim.  (FAC ¶ 58.)  Courts in this district have held that where the 
13   alleged unfair business practices overlap entirely with the practices alleged under the fraudulent 
14   prong of the UCL, as is the case here, the unfair prong of the UCL cannot survive if the UCL fraud 
15   claims do not survive.  See Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1104" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1104-05</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">16   Cal. 2017</span><pre class="inline">) (collecting cases).  As discussed above, Plaintiff has failed to sufficiently allege a 
17   fraud-based claim under the UCL.  Thus, Plaintiff’s claim under the unfair prong of the UCL also 
18   fails.                                                                
19   D.   Plaintiff’s Claim Under the UCL’s Unlawful Prong Fails.          
20        The UCL’s unlawful prong allows plaintiffs to “borrow” other laws and make claims 
21   independently actionable under the UCL.  Cel-Tech Commc’ns, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Tel. 
22   Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9567993"><a href="/opinion/1301891/cel-tech-communications-inc-v-los-angeles-cellular-telephone-co/#180" aria-description="Citation for case: Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular...">20 Cal. 4th 163, 180</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1999).  Here, Plaintiff&#x27;s unlawful claim is premised on violations of the 
23   CLRA and California’s Auto-Renewal Law (“ARL”).  Because Plaintiff has not sufficiently 
24   alleged that Defendants violated the CLRA, the unlawful claim fails to the extent it is premised on 
25   a violation of that statute.                                          
26        The ARL requires all businesses offering an automatic renewal payment system to: (1) 
27   present automatic renewal terms to the consumer in a clear and conspicuous manner; (2) obtain the 
1   and (4) provide an easy-to-use mechanism for cancellation.  McKee v. Audible, Inc., No. CV 17-
2   1941-GW(EX), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 7388530</span><pre class="inline">, at *17 (C.D. Cal. Oct. 26, 2017) (citing Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. 
3   Code § 17602).  “Clear and conspicuous” means “in larger type than the surrounding text, or in 
4   contrasting type, font, or color to the surrounding text of the same size, or set off from the 
5   surrounding text of the same size by symbols or other marks, in a manner that clearly calls 
6   attention to the language.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17601</span><pre class="inline">(c).       
7        As discussed above, Plaintiff has failed to sufficiently allege what statements or 
8   disclosures she allegedly saw related to the “No-Rush Shipping Program” or Audible enrollment.  
9   Thus, the Court is unable to evaluate whether any such disclosures or lack thereof violate the 
10   ARL, and Plaintiff’s unlawful claim predicated on a violation of the ARL fails.  McKee, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 
11</span><pre class="inline">   7388530, at *21 (C.D. Cal. Oct. 26, 2017) (granting motion to dismiss UCL claim based on ARL 
12   violation where the plaintiff did not sufficiently detail the content of disclosures Audible included 
13   during the sign-up process).3                                         
14        For these reasons, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s CLRA and UCL claim are subject to 
15   dismissal, and it does not reach Defendants’ remaining challenges to these claims.  Because the 
16   Court cannot say that amendment would be futile, it permits Plaintiff leave to amend her 
17   complaint.
