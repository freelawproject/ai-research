9   II.  LEGAL STANDARD                                                   
10     A. Dismissal Pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6)   
11        Rule 8(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure requires a complaint to include “a short 
12   and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a). 
13   A complaint that fails to meet this standard may be dismissed pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil 
14   Procedure 12(b)(6). Rule 8(a) requires a plaintiff to plead “enough facts to state a claim to relief 
15   that is plausible on its face.” Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007). “A claim 
16   has facial plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the 
17   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">18   U.S. 662</span><pre class="inline">, 678 (2009). “The plausibility standard is not akin to a probability requirement, but it 
19   asks for more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> (internal 
20   quotation marks omitted). For purposes of ruling on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the Court “accept[s] 
21   factual allegations in the complaint as true and construe[s] the pleadings in the light most 
22   favorable to the nonmoving party.” Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1194077"><a href="/opinion/1194077/manzarek-v-st-paul-fire-marine-insurance/" aria-description="Citation for case: Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Insurance">519 F.3d 1025</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
23   1031 (9th Cir. 2008).                                                 
24        The Court, however, need not accept as true allegations contradicted by judicially 
25   noticeable facts, see Shwarz v. United States, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6990180"><a href="/opinion/7084861/shwarz-v-united-states/#435" aria-description="Citation for case: Shwarz v. United States">234 F.3d 428, 435</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2000), and it “may look 
26   beyond the plaintiff’s complaint to matters of public record” without converting the Rule 12(b)(6) 
27   motion into a motion for summary judgment, Shaw v. Hahn, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="697357"><a href="/opinion/697357/gina-shaw-v-james-k-hahn-john-emerson-james-pearson-thomas-hokinson/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gina Shaw v. James K. Hahn John Emerson James Pearson...">56 F.3d 1128</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1129 n.1 (9th Cir. 
1   1995). Nor must the Court “assume the truth of legal conclusions merely because they are cast in 
2   the form of factual allegations.” Fayer v. Vaughn, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="216101"><a href="/opinion/216101/fayer-v-vaughn/#1064" aria-description="Citation for case: Fayer v. Vaughn">649 F.3d 1061, 1064</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (per 
3   curiam) (quoting W. Mining Council v. Watt, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.2d/643/618/">643 F.2d 618</a></span><pre class="inline">, 624 (9th Cir. 1981)). Mere 
4   “conclusory allegations of law and unwarranted inferences are insufficient to defeat a motion to 
5   dismiss.” Adams v. Johnson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408553"><a href="/opinion/8438027/adams-v-johnson/#1183" aria-description="Citation for case: Adams v. Johnson">355 F.3d 1179, 1183</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2004).      
6     B.  Leave to Amend                                                  
7        If the Court determines that a complaint should be dismissed, it must then decide whether 
8   to grant leave to amend. Under Rule 15(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, leave to amend 
9   “shall be freely given when justice so requires,” bearing in mind “the underlying purpose of Rule 
10   15 to facilitate decisions on the merits, rather than on the pleadings or technicalities.” Lopez v. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">11   Smith, 203</span><pre class="inline"> F.3d 1122, 1127 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (alterations and internal quotation marks 
12   omitted). When dismissing a complaint for failure to state a claim, “&#x27;a district court should grant 
13   leave to amend even if no request to amend the pleading was made, unless it determines that the 
14   pleading could not possibly be cured by the allegation of other facts.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. at 1130</span><pre class="inline"> (internal 
15   quotation marks omitted). Accordingly, leave to amend generally shall be denied only if allowing 
16   amendment would unduly prejudice the opposing party, cause undue delay, or be futile, or if the 
17   moving party has acted in bad faith. Leadsinger, Inc. v. BMG Music Publ’g, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1446183"><a href="/opinion/1446183/leadsinger-inc-v-bmg-music-publishing/" aria-description="Citation for case: Leadsinger, Inc. v. BMG Music Publishing">512 F.3d 522</a></span><pre class="inline">, 532 
18   (9th Cir. 2008).

24     A. State Law Claims                                                 
25        The County argues that Plaintiffs’ state law claims should be dismissed. Mot. at 4–5, 6–7. 
26   The Court agrees for two independent reasons. First, Plaintiffs did not comply with the CGCA’s 
27   claim presentation requirement. Second, the County and its employees are immune from 
1   Plaintiffs’ claims pursuant to state law. The Court discusses each of these arguments in turn.  
2        1.  Claim Presentation Requirement                               
3        Before a plaintiff can sue a public entity or employee for monetary damages, he or she 
4   must first fulfill the claim presentation requirement by filing a claim with the public entity. Cal. 
5   Gov’t Code § 945.4; Briggs v. Lawrence, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2114919"><a href="/opinion/2114919/briggs-v-lawrence/#613" aria-description="Citation for case: Briggs v. Lawrence">230 Cal. App. 3d 605, 613</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1991). The plaintiff must 
6   file a claim no more than six months after the cause of action has accrued. Cal. Gov’t Code § 
7   911.2; Curtis T. v. County of Los Angeles, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2256277"><a href="/opinion/2256277/curtis-t-v-county-of-los-angeles/#1415" aria-description="Citation for case: CURTIS T. v. County of Los Angeles">123 Cal. App. 4th 1405, 1415</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2004). The plaintiff must 
8   then sue no more than six months after the public entity mails written notice that it has rejected the 
9   plaintiff’s claim. Cal. Gov’t Code § 945.6(a)(1); County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court, 127 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="2254039"><a href="/opinion/2254039/people-v-clark/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Clark">10   Cal. App. 4th 1263</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1267–68 (2005).                                   
11        “[A] plaintiff must allege in the complaint compliance or circumstances excusing 
12   compliance with the claims presentation requirement in order to state facts sufficient to constitute 
13   a cause of action against a public entity.” Dang v. Oakland Police Dep’t, Case No. C 13-4155 
14   PJH, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 793613</span><pre class="inline">, at *9 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 26, 2014) (citing State of California v. Superior 
15   Court (Bodde), </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2634703"><a href="/opinion/2634703/state-v-superior-court/" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Superior Court">32 Cal.4th 1234</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1239–45 (2004)). “[C]ompliance with the claims presentation 
16   requirement is a condition precedent to filing suit.” Id. at *10. “[F]ailure to allege compliance or 
17   circumstances excusing compliance with the claim presentation requirement subjects a complaint 
18   to a general demurrer for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” Bodde, 90 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">19   P.3d 116</span><pre class="inline">, 119 (Cal. 2004); see also D.V. v. City of Sunnyvale, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7228287"><a href="/opinion/7310383/dv-v-city-of-sunnyvale/#786" aria-description="Citation for case: D.V. v. City of Sunnyvale">65 F. Supp. 3d 782, 786</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 
20   2014) (“[U]nder Bodde, the complaint must include facts demonstrating compliance with the 
21   claim presentation requirement or the claim will be dismissed.”) (citing Bodde, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2634703"><a href="/opinion/2634703/state-v-superior-court/" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Superior Court">32 Cal.4th at 
22</a></span><pre class="inline">   1239).

20        2.  Immunity Under State Law                                     
21        Under Section 856 of the California Government Code, “[n]either a public entity nor a 
22   public employee acting within the scope of his employment is liable for any injury resulting from 
23   determining . . .  [w]hether to confine a person for mental illness or addiction . . . [or] [w]hether to 
24   parole, grant a leave of absence to, or release a person confined for mental illness or addiction.” 
25   Cal. Gov’t Code §§ 856(a)(1), (3). Section 856 immunity extends to “all those involved in the 
26   process leading to release,” not just “the custodial officer who makes the final determination to 
27   release.”  Hernandez v. State of California, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2156852"><a href="/opinion/2156852/hernandez-v-state-of-california/#899" aria-description="Citation for case: Hernandez v. State of California">11 Cal. App. 3d 895, 899</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1970). “[T]he scope of the 
1   immunity extends not only to the final determination to confine or not to confine the person for 
2   mental illness, but to all determinations involved in the process of commitment.” Tarasoff v. 
3   Regents of University of California, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9548405"><a href="/opinion/1175611/tarasoff-v-regents-of-university-of-california/#448" aria-description="Citation for case: Tarasoff v. Regents of University of California">551 P.2d 334, 448</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. 1976). By creating this immunity, 
4   “the Legislature intended to encourage a policy of free release from confinement, unfettered by the 
5   possibility of charges of negligence against those who participate in the process leading to 
6   release.” Hernandez, 11 Cal. App. at 900.                             
7        Section 856 immunity protects public entities from liability for injuries stemming from 
8   their determination that a person confined for mental illness should be released. For example, in 
9   Hernandez v. State of California, the plaintiffs were heirs of a decedent who had been killed by 
10   her son following his release from confinement at a state mental hospital. 11 Cal. App. at 897. The 
11   plaintiffs alleged that the son had been released because the hospital’s employees had failed to 
12   maintain proper records of the son’s treatment. Id. at 898. The Court of Appeals concluded that 
13   Section 856 provided immunity to the employees because Section 856 immunity extended to “all 
14   those involved in the process leading to release.” Id. at 899.        
15        Section 856 provides immunity to the County in the instant case as well. Plaintiffs’ state 
16   law claims stem from the County’s allegedly erroneous decision to prematurely release Harris, 
17   who was being confined for mental illness at the Santa Clara Valley Medical Center. Compl. ¶¶ 
18   10, 12. Plaintiffs allege that “[the County] knew or should have known that [Harris] was unfit to 
19   be released prematurely” but “failed to take necessary steps to prevent [Harris’s] release.” Id. ¶ 12. 
20   Plaintiffs allege that, as a result of the County’s failure, Harris “remained in an unfit and delirious 
21   state, entered onto [I]nterstate 280 . . . and was struck and killed.” Id. ¶ 10. Thus, Plaintiffs’ state 
22   law claims stem from the County’s allegedly erroneous decision to release Harris, who was being 
23   confined for mental illness. Accordingly, the County cannot be held liable because, under Section 
24   856(a)(3), “[n]either a public entity nor a public employee acting within the scope of his 
25   employment is liable for any injury resulting from determining . . .  [w]hether to . . . release a 
26   person confined for mental illness or addiction.” Cal. Gov’t Code § 856(a)(3); see also Hernandez, 
27   11 Cal. App. at 900 (concluding that employees of a state mental hospital who failed to maintain 
1   proper records and therefore erroneously released a person confined for mental illness were not 
2   liable for injuries that stemmed from the release).4                  
3        Plaintiffs argue that Section 856 does not immunize the County because California 
4   Government Code Section 845.6 created a statutory duty for the County to summon additional 
5   medical care. Opp’n at 7–8. However, Section 845.6 applies only to prisoners. See Cal. Gov’t 
6   Code § 845.6 (providing that “a public employee, and the public entity where the employee is 
7   acting within the scope of his employment, is liable if the employee knows or has reason to know 
8   that the prisoner is in need of immediate medical care”) (emphasis added). Because Harris was not 
9   a prisoner, Section 845.6 does not apply. Thus, under Section 856, the County is immune from 
10   Plaintiffs’ state law claims, which stem from the County’s decision to release Harris from 
11   confinement.5
