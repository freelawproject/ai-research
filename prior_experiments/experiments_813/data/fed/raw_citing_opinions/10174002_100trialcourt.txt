13                               DISCUSSION                                   
14       To survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain enough facts to “state a 
15  claim to relief that is plausible on its face.” Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009); 
16  see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6). Plausibility requires more than mere “conclusions” or a 
17  “formulaic recitation” of elements; it must be based on “factual allegations” that “raise a 
18  right to relief above the speculative level.” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544</a></span><pre class="inline">, 555 
19  (2007) (cleaned up).                                                      
20  A.   Vicarious Liability                                                  
21       Before addressing the specific claims, this Court must determine whose actions 
22  Ottawa University may be held accountable for. Vashisht-Rota seeks to impute to Ottawa 
23  the  actions  of  HMS,  DeWald,  and  Stevens.  (See,  e.g.,  ECF  70,  at  13–16  (alleging 
24  harassment while relying almost exclusively on HMS-related conduct).) Under the doctrine 
25  of respondeat superior, “principals or employers” are ordinarily “vicariously liable for acts 
26  of their agents or employees in the scope of their authority or employment.” Meyer v. 
27  Holley, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="122256"><a href="/opinion/122256/meyer-v-holley/#285" aria-description="Citation for case: Meyer v. Holley">537 U.S. 280, 285</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2003). “In determining whether vicarious liability may be 
28  imposed, the extent of control exercised by the principal is the essential ingredient.” Jones 
1  v. Royal Admin. Servs., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015724"><a href="/opinion/9022499/jones-v-royal-admin-servs-inc/#450" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Royal Admin. Servs., Inc.">887 F.3d 443, 450</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018) (cleaned up) (discussing 
2  California  agency  law);  see  also  Restatement  (Third)  Of  Agency  §  2.04  cmt.  b 
3  (“Respondeat superior is inapplicable when a principal does not have the right to control 
4  the actions of the agent . . . .”).                                       
5       1.  HMS                                                              
6       In dismissing the previous complaint, this Court deemed allegations of Ottawa’s 
7  control over HMS “entirely conclusory,” except for Ottawa’s limited authority to set 
8  “guidelines and procedures” unrelated to HMS’s employment decisions. (ECF 65, at 7.) 
9  In the new complaint, Vashisht-Rota makes several conclusory references to Ottawa’s 
10  “control” or “total control” of HMS. (See, e.g., ECF 70, at 4.) And she adds some factual 
11  detail,  including  discussions  of  the  parties’  operative  contract,  applicable  federal 
12  regulations, and shared activities. Each of these new allegations, however, still fall short of 
13  establishing the necessary employer-employee or principal-agent relationship. 
14       First, Vashisht-Rota relies on the contract between Ottawa and HMS—or at least her 
15  account of it, as she does not provide the document itself. (See ECF 70, at 4.) She says it 
16  sets out “[i]ndustry standards and norms,” including “the parties’ roles and responsibilities, 
17  performance measures, ethical behaviors, compensation, the need for confidentiality and 
18  other issues.” (Id.) She also asserts that “the terms of the agreements negotiated between 
19  [HMS] and its partner colleges and universities have a ‘trickle down’ effect and apply 
20  equally to any third parties that come within their purview,” presumably including herself. 
21  (Id.)                                                                     
22       Even if we assume her description of this crucial contract is accurate, however, it 
23  does  not  suggest  that  HMS  was  Ottawa’s  agent  or  that  Ottawa  controlled  HMS’s 
24  employment decisions. Contracts with performance standards are common in both agency 
25  relationships (like employer-employee) and non-agency relationships (like independent 
26  contractors).  See,  e.g.,  Jones,  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015724"><a href="/opinion/9022499/jones-v-royal-admin-servs-inc/#452" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Royal Admin. Servs., Inc.">887  F.3d  at 452</a></span><pre class="inline">  (finding  no  employment  relationship 
27  although defendant “exercised some amount of control” over the offending telemarketing 
28  company,  including  setting  “guidelines  and  procedures”).  And  “evidence  of  an 
1  independent  contractor  relationship  is  insufficient  in  itself  to  establish  an  agency 
2  relationship.”  United States  v.  Bonds,  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435710"><a href="/opinion/148428/united-states-v-bonds/#505" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Bonds">608  F.3d  495,  505</a></span><pre class="inline">  (9th Cir.  2010).  Thus,  the 
3  contract’s mere existence doesn’t plausibly imply enough control over HMS to make it an 
4  agent. Nor does it establish that Ottawa controlled HMS’s employment-related decisions 
5  beyond the identified contractual obligations.                            
6       Next, Vashisht-Rota suggests that Ottawa has “total control” over HMS because 
7  Ottawa must “follow federal regulation[s] for immigration,” which dictate “its admission 
8  criteria for each program as noted in its catalog to which it is legally bound.” (ECF 70, 
9  at 4.) But federal regulations can’t conjure the necessary principal-agent relationship here. 
10  By analogy, the tax code dictates many requirements for taxpayers in preparing their 
11  filings. But if Ottawa hired H&amp;R Block to prepare its tax returns, Ottawa would not become 
12  vicariously liable for H&amp;R Block’s employment decisions.                  
13       Finally,  the  new  complaint  alleges  that  Ottawa  “dictates  HMS’  conduct  in 
14  recruitment,” as the two parties “attend recruitment events together that are replete with 
15  technical  information  about  the  program,  tuition,  admissions  policies,”  and  the  like. 
16  (ECF 70, at 4–5.) Yet the parties’ joint attendance at such events does not imply that one 
17  controls the other. In particular, nothing about these affairs suggests that Ottawa dictates 
18  HMS’s employment-related decisions.                                       
19       Looking beyond these questionable arguments for Ottawa’s control of HMS, this 
20  Court has scoured the complaint for facts that might suggest any employment or principal-
21  agent relationship. The Ninth Circuit has adopted ten non-exhaustive factors for judging 
22  when “a principal has enough authority to control the actions of its agent such that the 
23  principal may  be held vicariously  liable  to the  same  extent  as  an  employer.”  Jones, 
24  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015724"><a href="/opinion/9022499/jones-v-royal-admin-servs-inc/#450" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Royal Admin. Servs., Inc.">887 F.3d at 450</a></span><pre class="inline"> (listing factors). Unfortunately for plaintiff, the third amended complaint 
25  is devoid of information about most of these factors and contains only hints of others—
26  which mostly cut against vicarious liability. Take the “distinct occupation” factor. HMS is 
27  a for-profit “recruiter,” which is a distinct occupation from Ottawa University’s “not-for-
28  profit” educational mission. (See ECF 70, at 3.) Also, HMS “offered the same services to 
1  others during the same period.” See Jones, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015724"><a href="/opinion/9022499/jones-v-royal-admin-servs-inc/#452" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Royal Admin. Servs., Inc.">887 F.3d at 452</a></span><pre class="inline">; (ECF 70, at 4 (pointing out 
2  that HMS served other “partner colleges and universities” in the same capacity)); see also 
3  Vashisht-Rota v. Harrisburg Univ., No. 20-CV-0967-AGS-KSC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2024 WL 85884</span><pre class="inline">, at *1 
4  (S.D. Cal. Jan. 8, 2024) (noting that, during the same period, another university hired HMS 
5  “to be its ‘sole vendor’ for ‘international student recruitment’”). All these facts “strongly 
6  suggest[]” HMS was “not subject to sufficient control [by Ottawa] to establish vicarious 
7  liability.” See Jones, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015724"><a href="/opinion/9022499/jones-v-royal-admin-servs-inc/#452" aria-description="Citation for case: Jones v. Royal Admin. Servs., Inc.">887 F.3d at 452</a></span><pre class="inline">.                                   
8       At best, one might arguably infer that HMS was Ottawa’s agent for the narrow 
9  purpose of recruiting international college students. Even then, the facts don’t plausibly 
10  suggest that Ottawa’s control over HMS was akin to an employer-employee relationship. 
11  Nor do they establish that the scope of any more limited agency relationship included 
12  Ottawa accepting responsibility for HMS’s employment decisions. Without more, HMS’s 
13  employment  decisions—including  any  alleged  harassment,  retaliation,  hostile  work 
14  environment, or the like—cannot plausibly be imputed to Ottawa.           
15       2.  DeWald and Stevens                                               
16       In the prior complaint, Vashisht-Rota plausibly pleaded that DeWald and Stevens 
17  were Ottawa University employees, acting within the scope of their employment. That is, 
18  she previously alleged that they are “Ottawa’s direct employees” who “received details of 
19  complaints  about  a  university  contractor—which  presumably  are  only  available  to 
20  university employees,” and Vashisht-Rota “even provide[d] their titles: supervisor for 
21  international recruitment and supervisor for compliance.” (ECF 65, at 7–8 (citing ECF 48, 
22  at 7).) Her current complaint is similar, though it omits Stevens’s title this time. (See 
23  generally ECF 70.) Regardless, she still claims that Stevens is Ottawa’s “employee,” with 
24  access to its EthicsPoint complaint system. (See ECF 70, at 39.) This is sufficient to 
25  plausibly suggest Ottawa may be vicariously liable for Stevens’s and DeWald’s actions.  
26  B.   Sufficiency of the Claims                                            
27       Having resolved whose behavior can be attributed to Ottawa, the Court turns to each

1       1.  Negligence (Count 1)                                             
2       For Vashisht-Rota’s garden-variety California negligence claim, she must show that 
3  Ottawa “had a duty to use due care, that [it] breached that duty, and that the breach was the 
4  proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.” See Brown v. USA Taekwondo, </pre><span class="citation no-link">483 P.3d 
5</span><pre class="inline">  159, 164 (Cal. 2021) (cleaned up). Ottawa disputes the duty element, contending that it 
6  was  under  no  duty  to  keep  confidential  the  information  that  Vashisht-Rota  sent  it 
7  “voluntarily.” (See ECF 71-1, at 21.)                                     
8       Although Vashisht-Rota never specifies the source of Ottawa’s alleged duty, it might 
9  arise solely from the foreseeable nature of a plaintiff’s harm. California law begins from 
10  the premise that all persons are under a duty “to use ordinary care to prevent others being 
11  injured as the result of their conduct.” Rowland v. Christian, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9632125"><a href="/opinion/1447366/rowland-v-christian/#564" aria-description="Citation for case: Rowland v. Christian">443 P.2d 561, 564</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. 1968) 
12  (cleaned up). But some situations justify a “departure from this fundamental principle,” 
13  such that there is no general duty of care. </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9632125"><a href="/opinion/1447366/rowland-v-christian/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rowland v. Christian">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> Plaintiff’s alleged “injury” is a central focus 
14  in evaluating whether to apply—or depart from—this general duty. See Hayes v. County of 
15  San Diego, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1038647"><a href="/opinion/1038647/hayes-v-county-of-san-diego/#257" aria-description="Citation for case: Hayes v. County of San Diego">305 P.3d 252, 257</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. 2013). In assessing its applicability, courts consider, 
16  among other things, “whether the category of negligent conduct at issue is sufficiently 
17  likely to result in the kind of harm experienced.” Kuciemba v. Victory Woodworks, Inc., 
18  </pre><span class="citation no-link">531 P.3d 924</span><pre class="inline">, 943–44 (Cal. 2023) (emphasis added).

12       3.  Unruh Civil Rights Act (Count 6)                                 
13       In Count 6, Vashisht-Rota renews her allegations of violations of the Unruh Civil 
14  Rights Act. See </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civil Code § 51</span><pre class="inline">. This law still “has no application to employment 
15  discrimination” or any other issue in this case. See Brennon B. v. Superior Ct., </pre><span class="citation no-link">513 P.3d 
16</span><pre class="inline">  971, 988 (Cal. 2022). This statute is exclusively concerned with non-discriminatory access 
17  to California businesses by that business’s “clients, patrons or customers.” Alcorn v. Anbro 
18  Eng’g,  Inc.,  </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/P.2d/468/216/">468  P.2d  216</a></span><pre class="inline">,  220  (Cal.  1970).  It  cannot  serve  as  the  basis  for  an 
19  employment-discrimination  claim,  as  that  “would  create  an  end-run  around  the 
20  administrative procedures” of the Fair Employment and Housing Act. Bass v. County of 
21  Butte, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="3038417"><a href="/opinion/3038417/bass-v-county-of-butte/#982" aria-description="Citation for case: Bass v. County of Butte">458 F.3d 978, 982</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2006).                                 
22       Vashisht-Rota’s citation to a FEHA section which makes it “unlawful” to “deny or 
23  to aid, incite, or conspire in the denial of the rights created by [California Civil Code] 
24  Section 51” does not alter this result. See Cal. Gov’t Code § 12948. A FEHA cause of 
25  action  under  the  cited  section—California  Government  Code  section 12948—“cannot 
26  proceed to the extent that it is an employment-related discrimination claim premised on 
27  rights incorporated through the Unruh Act.” Allford v. Barton, No. 1:14-CV-00024-AWI-
28  JLT, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2015 WL 2455138</span><pre class="inline">, at *23 (E.D. Cal. May 22, 2015). And even if section 12948 did 
1  apply to allegations of sexual harassment in employment, the claim would fail for the same 
2  reason as the other harassment claims previously discussed. Count 6 is dismissed. 
3       4.  Business Discrimination (Count 7)                                
4       In count 7, Vashisht-Rota alleges a violation of California Civil Code section 51.5 
5  that  again  is  missing  any  allegation  regarding  the  essential  element  of  “intentional 
6  discrimination.” See Long v. Playboy Enters. Int’l, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2657873"><a href="/opinion/2657873/long-v-playboy-enterprises-international-inc/#647" aria-description="Citation for case: Long v. Playboy Enterprises International, Inc.">565 F. App’x 646, 647</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
7  2014). Section 51.5 requires, in pertinent part, that “no business establishment of any kind 
8  whatsoever shall discriminate against, boycott or blacklist, or refuse to buy from, contract 
9  with, sell to, or trade with any person in this state on account of [gender].” Cal. Civ. Code 
10  § 51.5(a). When the Court last dismissed this claim, it noted: “Although Vashisht-Rota 
11  contends  that  Ottawa  has  a  gender  animus,  her  allegations  are  entirely  conclusory.” 
12  (ECF 65, at 12.) The new complaint adds little to its deficient predecessor. In this count, 
13  Vashisht-Rota now remarks: “Should Plaintiff finally get to compete, perhaps Dr. DeWald 
14  does not get paid from her male white friends.” (ECF 70, at 27.) And she adds some 
15  conclusory statements, such as: “The differential treatment in the messages sent, responses 
16  between male and female founders is contrary to California’s strong public policy that 
17  discrimination based on the basis of sex is substantial and fundamental.” (Id.) Yet the same 
18  deficiencies remain. She has not alleged any plausible, non-conclusory facts to show that 
19  Ottawa is refusing to work with her on account of her sex. Count 7 must be dismissed. 
20       5.  Unfair Competition (Count 8)                                     
21       Vashisht-Rota’s Unfair Competition Law claim also falls, as it is derivative of other 
22  deficient claims. California prohibits unfair competition by means of “any unlawful, unfair 
23  or fraudulent business act or practice.” </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200</span><pre class="inline"> (emphasis added). 
24  “Because the statute is written in the disjunctive, it is violated where a defendant’s act or 
25  practice violates any of the foregoing prongs.” Davis v. HSBC Bank Nevada, N.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9501096"><a href="/opinion/806436/united-states-v-santiago-mendez/#1152" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Santiago-Mendez">691 F.3d 
26  1152, 1168</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012). Vashisht-Rota contends that she meets the “unlawful” prong 
27  with allegations of “retaliating,” “aiding and abetting harassment,” and “failing to prevent 
28  harassment.” (ECF 70, at 28.) In other words, she predicates her proof of the “unlawful 
1  prong” on her now-dismissed claims in counts 3 to 5. “If a plaintiff cannot state a claim 
2  under the predicate law, . . . the [Unfair Competition Law] claim also fails.” Hadley v. 
3  Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1094" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1094</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017).           
4       6.  Sherman Act (Count 9)                                            
5       In her last cause of action—ostensibly a claim under the Sherman Act, </pre><span class="citation no-link">15 U.S.C. § 1</span><pre class="inline"> 
6  (see ECF 70, at 29–37)—Vashisht-Rota managed to violate not just one, but two court 
7  orders. First, because the Sherman Act claim did not appear in her prior complaint, she was 
8  not permitted to add it to this new complaint. Specifically, in its order dismissing the prior 
9  complaint, this Court provided an opportunity to amend, but with the important restriction 
10  that Vashisht-Rota “not add . . . new causes of action to any such amended complaint.” 
11  (ECF 65, at 13.) The Court imposed this somewhat unusual constraint due to hard-won 
12  wisdom in dealing with Vashisht-Rota’s prior abuses of court processes. (See, e.g., ECF 78 
13  (noting the Court received “over 40 emails from plaintiff” in one week, on topics ranging 
14  “from criticizing other judges, to legal arguments and theories, to damages calculations, to 
15  the British royals, and even to pop star Taylor Swift’s age”); ECF 52 (347-page response 
16  to a motion to dismiss); ECF 62 &amp; 63 (unauthorized “supplemental briefs”)); see also 
17  Vashisht-Rota  v.  Howell  Management  Servs.,  No. 3:20-cv-0321-AGS-KSC  (S.D. Cal. 
18  Dec. 11, 2023), ECF 316 (striking over 700 pages of unauthorized filings despite “repeated 
19  warnings” to stop filing in that closed case).                            
20       In addition, the Court later issued a second order reiterating limits on the new 
21  complaint. Due to the existing restriction, Vashisht-Rota sought leave to add claims under 
22  the “Speak Out Act,” </pre><span class="citation no-link">42 U.S.C. § 19401</span><pre class="inline"> et seq., and under provisions relating to “slavery, 
23  involuntary servitude, or forced labor.” (ECF 66; ECF 68, at 1.) The Court denied these 
24  requests, given the lack of any coherent explanation for them. (See ECF 69.) In defiance of 
25  this explicit ruling, Vashisht-Rota smuggled both legal theories into the third amended 
26  complaint under the banner of the new Sherman Act claim. That is, within count 9 she 
27  specifically discusses the “Speak Out Act” and includes a lengthy subsection pertaining to 
28  “Involuntary Servitude/Slavery.” (See ECF 70, at 29–36 (emphasis removed).) 
1       A judge “may strike claims from any pleading for failure to comply with the court’s 
2  orders.” Naranjo v. Bank of Am. Nat’l Ass’n, No. 14-CV-02748-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2015 WL 4463851</span><pre class="inline">, 
3  at *13 (N.D. Cal. July 21, 2015). Based on the foregoing violations, this Court strikes from 
4  the third amended complaint any portions relating to the new legal theories concerning the 
5  Speak Out Act and slavery/involuntary servitude. Even if they weren’t stricken, both these 
6  legal theories fail for insufficient allegations of conspiracy or vicarious liability. For 
7  instance, Vashisht-Rota alleges that Ottawa “knows or should have known that Plaintiff is 
8  being held as a slave without payment,” but that doesn’t suggest that Ottawa agreed to—
9  or is responsible for—keeping her in “indentured servitude.” (See ECF 70, at 30.) And the 
10  Speak  Out  Act  allegations  are  even  more  tenuous.  Vashisht-Rota  believes  that  the 
11  settlement offer she rejected from HMS in a different case violates that Act, because it 
12  allegedly included a confidentiality clause “that Plaintiff stay silent about the harassment.” 
13  (Id. at 35.) Ottawa appears to have nothing whatsoever to do with these allegations. 
14       Although the rest of count 9—the Sherman Act claim—also violated the amendment 
15  restriction, this Court will nonetheless review what remains. After all, the prior complaint 
16  included  a  California  Cartwright  Act  claim,  and  the  “analysis  of  a  claim  under  the 
17  Cartwright Act mirrors the analysis” under the federal Sherman Act. See Olean Wholesale 
18  Grocery Coop., Inc. v. Bumble Bee Foods LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">31 F.4th 651</span><pre class="inline">, 666 n.8 (9th Cir. 2022). 
19       In its last order, the Court dismissed Vashisht-Rota’s Cartwright Act claim because 
20  she had “not detailed any conspiracy—or any agreement whatsoever—involving Ottawa.” 
21  (ECF 65, at 11.) The same problem applies here. The “crucial question” in Sherman Act 
22  litigation “is whether the challenged anticompetitive conduct stems” “from an agreement, 
23  tacit or express.” Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#553" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 553</a></span><pre class="inline"> (cleaned up). Once again, the alleged facts 
24  don’t plausibly point to any conspiracy. The allegation that comes closest to hitting the 
25  mark is Vashisht-Rota’s claim that Ottawa and HMS “mimicked the same behavior,” which 
26  “stemmed from a tacit agreement to not allow women or people of color in management.” 
27  (ECF  70,  at  31.)  But  a  “statement  of  parallel  conduct,  even  conduct  consciously 
28  undertaken,” is insufficient to plead a conspiracy in a Sherman Act claim. Twombly, 
1   ||</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#557" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 557</a></span><pre class="inline">. Aside from the fact that neither Ottawa nor HMS worked with Vashisht- 
2 || Rota after 2017, there is simply nothing in the complaint to suggest they colluded to box 
3   ||her out of the industry. As before, the complaint “offers no details as to a specific time, 
4   ||place,  or person  involved  in  how  Defendant  allegedly  ‘agreed  to  boycott  Plaintiff.’  It 
5 || therefore insufficiently shows the formation and operation of a conspiracy.” (ECF 65, at 11 
6    (cleaned up).) The Sherman Act claim is thus dismissed.
