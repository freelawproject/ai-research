<opinion type="majority"> <author id="b136-19" pgmap="136">CHIN, Circuit Judge:</author> <p id="b136-20" pgmap="136">In this case, defendant-appellant Latch-man Singh pleaded guilty to one count of illegally reentering'the United States after having been removed following a conviction for an aggravated felony. His Guidelines range was 15 to 21 months’ imprisonment, and both the government and the Probation Office recommended ‘ a within-Guidelines sentence. The district court, however, sentenced Singh to a term of imprisonment, of 60 months—nearly three times the top of the Guidelines range.</p> <p id="b136-21" pgmap="136">Singh appeals, contending that the sentence was both procedurally and substantively unreasonable. For the reasons set forth below, we vacate the sentence and remand- for further proceedings. Singh’s request that we order reassignment of the case to a different judge is denied.</p> <p id="b136-22" pgmap="136">BACKGROUND</p> <p id="Aj6" pgmap="136">A. Applicable Law</p> <p id="b141-11" pgmap="141"> We review a sentence for procedural and substantive reasonableness under a “deferential abuse-of-discretion, standard.” Gall v. United States, <span class="citation no-link">562 U.S. 38</span>, 41, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">128 S.Ct. 586</a></span>, <span class="citation no-link">169 L.Ed.2d 446</span> (2007); see United States v. Broxmeyer, <span class="citation" data-id="9501245"><a href="/opinion/807518/united-states-v-broxmeyer/#278" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Broxmeyer">699 F.3d 265, 278</a></span> (2d Cir. 2012) (“our standard is ‘reasonableness,’ ‘a particularly deferential form of abuse-of-discretion review’ ”) (quoting United States v. Cavera, <span class="citation" data-id="9561968"><a href="/opinion/1206802/united-states-v-cavera/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cavera">550 F.3d 180</a></span>, 188 &amp; n.5 (2d Cir. 2008) (en banc)); accord United States v. Thavaraja, <span class="citation" data-id="2650623"><a href="/opinion/2650623/united-states-v-pratheepan-thavaraja/#258" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Pratheepan Thavaraja">740 F.3d 253, 258</a></span> (2d Cir. 2014)..</p> <p id="b141-13" pgmap="141"> A sentence is procedurally unreasonable if the district court “fails to calculate (or improperly calculates) the Sentencing Guidelines - range, treats the Sentencing Guidelines- as mandatory, fails to consider the § 3553(a) factors, selects a sentence based on clearly erroneous facts, or fails adequately to explain the chosen sentence.” United States v. Chu, <span class="citation" data-id="866796"><a href="/opinion/866796/united-states-v-chu/#746" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Chu">714 F.3d 742, 746</a></span> (2d Cir. 2013) (internal quotation marks omitted).-We will set aside a sentence as substantively unreasonable only in “exceptional cases where the trial court’s decision ‘cannot be located within the range of permissible decisions.’ ” Cavera, <span class="citation" data-id="9561968"><a href="/opinion/1206802/united-states-v-cavera/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cavera">550 F.3d at 189</a></span> (quoting United States v. Rigas, <span class="citation" data-id="2725"><a href="/opinion/2725/united-states-v-rigas/#238" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Rigas">490 F.3d 208, 238</a></span> (2d Cir. 2007)). We wjll identify “as substantively unreasonable only those sentences that are so ‘shockingly high, shockingly low, or otherwise unsupportable as a matter of law’ that allowing them to stand would ‘damage the administration of justice.’” Broxmeyer, <span class="citation" data-id="9501245"><a href="/opinion/807518/united-states-v-broxmeyer/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Broxmeyer">699 F.3d at 289</a></span> (quoting United States v. Rigas, <span class="citation" data-id="2467"><a href="/opinion/2467/united-states-v-rigas/#123" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Rigas">583 F.3d 108, 123</a></span> (2d Cir. 2009)).</p> <p id="b141-14" pgmap="141(130) 142(55)"> Our review for substantive unreasonableness is “particularly deferential.” Broxmeyer, <span class="citation" data-id="9501245"><a href="/opinion/807518/united-states-v-broxmeyer/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Broxmeyer">699 F.3d at 289</a></span> (citing Gall, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/#51" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">552 U.S. at 51</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">128 S.Ct. 586</a></span>). The Supreme Court has made clear that “responsibility for sentencing is placed largely in the precincts of the district courts.” Cavera, <span class="citation" data-id="9561968"><a href="/opinion/1206802/united-states-v-cavera/#191" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cavera">550 F.3d at 191</a></span>. Hence, “our role in sentencing appeals is to ‘patrol the boundaries of reasonableness,’ ” Rigas, <span class="citation" data-id="2467"><a href="/opinion/2467/united-states-v-rigas/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Rigas">583 F.3d at 122</a></span> (quoting Cavera, <span class="citation" data-id="9561968"><a href="/opinion/1206802/united-states-v-cavera/#191" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cavera">550 F.3d at 191</a></span>), ^vith due respect for the sentencing court’s “very wide latitude to decide the proper degree of punishment for an individual offender and a particular crime,” Cavera, 560 F.3d at 188. Though the standard for finding substantive unreasonableness is high, this Court has not shied away from doing so when appropriate. See United States v. Dorvee, <span class="citation" data-id="152212"><a href="/opinion/152212/united-states-v-dorvee/#188" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Dorvee">616 F.3d 174, 188</a></span> (2d Cir. 2010) (“[I]t would be manifestly unjust to let Dorvee’s sentence stand.”); United States v. Jenkins, <span class="citation" data-id="9874285"><a href="/opinion/4383392/united-states-v-joseph-vincent-jenkins/#188" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Joseph Vincent Jenkins">854 F.3d 181, 188</a></span> (2d Cir. 2017) (“We conclude that the factors upon which the district court relied ... cannot bear the weight of the sentence the district court imposed.”).</p> <p id="b142-4" pgmap="142"> District courts are to use the Guidelines as a “starting point,” and then make an independent sentencing determination, taking into account the “nature and circumstances of the offense and the history and characteristics of the defendant,” and all the statutory factors. Cavera, <span class="citation" data-id="9561968"><a href="/opinion/1206802/united-states-v-cavera/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cavera">550 F.3d at 188</a></span> (quoting <span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 3553</span>(a)). Sentencing courts are not to “presume that the Guidelines range is reasonable,” and instead they “must make an individualized assessment based on the facts presented.” Gall, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/#50" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">552 U.S. at 50</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">128 S.Ct. 586</a></span>.</p> <p id="b142-5" pgmap="142"> Where there is a variance from the Guidelines range, on appellate review, “ Ve may take the degree of variance into account and consider the extent of a deviation from the Guidelines’.... ‘[A] major departure should be supported by a more significant justification than a minor one.’ ” United States v. Stewart, <span class="citation" data-id="9628995"><a href="/opinion/1434123/united-states-v-stewart/#135" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Stewart">590 F.3d 93, 135</a></span> (2d Cir. 2009) (quoting Gall, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/#47" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">552 U.S. at 47, 50</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">128 S.Ct. 586</a></span>).</p> <p id="b142-6" pgmap="142">B. Application</p> <p id="b142-7" pgmap="142">Singh challenges his sentence on both procedural and substantive grounds. While we usually address the procedural arguments first in sentencing appeals, here we begin with the substantive challenge because it informs our analysis of the procedural arguments.</p> <p id="b142-9" pgmap="142">1. Substantive Unreasonableness</p> <p id="b142-10" pgmap="142"> As the Supreme Court has observed in a different context, “the measure of what is conscience shocking is no calibrated yard stick.” Cty. of Sacramento v. Lewis, <span class="citation" data-id="9433650"><a href="/opinion/118214/county-of-sacramento-v-lewis/#847" aria-description="Citation for case: County of Sacramento v. Lewis">523 U.S. 833, 847</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9433650"><a href="/opinion/118214/county-of-sacramento-v-lewis/" aria-description="Citation for case: County of Sacramento v. Lewis">118 S.Ct. 1708</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9433650"><a href="/opinion/118214/county-of-sacramento-v-lewis/" aria-description="Citation for case: County of Sacramento v. Lewis">140 L.Ed.2d 1043</a></span> (1998); accord O’Connor v. Pierson, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/426/187/">426 F.3d 187</a></span>, 203 (2d Cir. 2005) (“The shocks-the-conscience test is necessarily imprecise.”). In this case, on this record, even if Singh’s sentence does not shock the conscience, “it at the very least stirs the conscience.” United States v. Aldeen, <span class="citation" data-id="8413509"><a href="/opinion/8442268/united-states-v-aldeen/#255" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Aldeen">792 F.3d 247, 255</a></span> (2d Cir. 2015).</p> <p id="b142-11" pgmap="142">The district court imposed a sentence of 60 months’ imprisonment for an illegal reentry case, where the Guidelines range was only 15 to 21 months and the government and the Probation Office had both recommended a within-Guidelines sentence. The district court imposed a sentence almost three times the high end of the Guidelines range. Moreover, as shown by Sentencing Commission statistics, the sentence of 60 months drastically exceeded nationwide norms. In fiscal year 2013, the average sentence for illegal reentry offenders was 18 months and the median sentence was 12-months. Sentencing Comm’n, Illegal Reentry Offenses, at 9 (April 2015) (“Comm’n Report”), available at https:// www.ussc.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/ researeh-and-publications/research-projects-and-surveys/immigration/2015_ Illegal-Reentry-Report.pdf.<footnotemark>3</footnotemark> An above-Guidelines sentence was imposed in only 1.3% of all illegal reentry cases, id. at 10, and in cases (like the instant one) with an 8-level enhancement for an aggravated felony, an above-Guidelines sentence was imposed in only 1.2% of the cases, id. at 11 Table 2.</p> <p id="b143-4" pgmap="143"> The district court concluded that a substantial variance was warranted here because of Singh’s “consistent history” of criminal conduct and the “very high likelihood of recidivism.” App. at 115.<footnotemark>4</footnotemark> While Singh indeed had eight prior convictions, as the district court acknowledged, however, none involved violence or narcotics trafficking. See id, at 109. Six of the eight convictions were more than ten years old. Four were more than twenty years old, counting back from the date of sentencing, and Singh was only 21 and 22 years old when he committed those offenses.<footnotemark>5</footnotemark> The four more recent convictions occurred over the course of fifteen years, and three were so minor they resulted in conditional discharges, that is, the sentencing court did not believe the crime warranted imprisonment or even probation. Moreover, the Sentencing Commission statistics show that 57.2% of illegal reentry offenders were in Criminal History Category (“CHC”) III or higher, Comm’n Report at 9. Singh was only in CHC II, and yet he was sentenced to more than three times the national average for all illegal reentry offenders, 57.2% of whom were in a higher CHC.</p> <p id="b143-5" pgmap="143">As we note above, a major variance must be supported by “a more significant justification.” United States v. Stewart, <span class="citation" data-id="9628995"><a href="/opinion/1434123/united-states-v-stewart/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Stewart">590 F.3d at 168</a></span> (quoting Gall, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/#47" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">552 U.S. at 47, 50</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9435287"><a href="/opinion/145843/gall-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gall v. United States">128 S.Ct. 586</a></span>). In the context of the Sentencing Commission’s statistics for illegal reentry cases and all the circumstances here, we are not persuaded, on this record, that the justification offered by the district court was sufficient to support the magnitude of the variance. See Cavera, <span class="citation" data-id="9561968"><a href="/opinion/1206802/united-states-v-cavera/#189" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cavera">550 F.3d at 189</a></span>. Our concern is heightened by the procedural issues we turn to now.</p> <p id="b143-9" pgmap="143">2. Procedural Reasonableness</p> <p id="b143-10" pgmap="143">We have two areas of concern with respect to procedural reasonableness. First, there may have been factual errors in the district court’s discussion of the record. Second, the district court’s reluctance to credit Singh’s acceptance of responsibility (although it did so in the end) suggests that the district court may have conflated Singh’s statements in mitigation with a failure to accept responsibility.</p> <p id="b143-11" pgmap="143">a. Factual Issues</p> <p id="b143-12" pgmap="143(41) 144(148)"> As to the apparent factual errors, the district court concluded that “reentry is almost certain, an attempt at reentry is almost certain for this defendant. He has done it two times before.” App. at 113 (emphasis added). The district court also referred to “the multiple[] numbers of times that he has been arrested and spent time in jail and been deported.” Id. at 114. These comments suggest that the district court believed that Singh had been guilty of two earlier illegal reentries and that it was sentencing him for a third illegal reentry. Indeed, the PSR had erroneously stated that “the instant offense is the defendant’s third arrest for illegally reentering the United States,” PSR at 23, a statement the parties acknowledge was wrong. In fact, Singh had been arrested for illegal reentry only twice—including for the instant offense. While the government argues on appeal that the district court might have been including the current offense in its comments and that the district court understood there were two and not three illegal reentries all together, the district court’s words suggest otherwise. '</p> <p id="b144-4" pgmap="144">The district court also concluded that Singh “has spent the majority of Ms adult life back and forth,” that is, between Guyana and the United States. App, at 115. In fact, however, Singh, who was 44 years old at the time of sentencing, was not deported for the first time-until 2010, when he was nearly 39. It was simply not correct that he had spent “the majority 'of his adult life back and forth.” To the contrary, he had spent the majority of his adult life living in the United States. The district court’s conclusion that Singh was “almost certain” to illegally reenter again surely was influenced-by these apparently erroneous views of the facts.</p> <p id="b144-5" pgmap="144">Finally, the district court determined as a factual-matter that this was “a defendant who has repeatedly harmed the public. ... [T]he kinds ,of crimes he is engaged in relate to a variety of conduct which is harmful,' and it is harmful to members of the public. The public shouldn’t be exposed to it. It’s repeated and it’s' repeated so often and so brazenly that I do not have any hope.” App. at 109. The district court further commented: “I am-indeed almost certain that'this defendant-will try to reenter. I also believe that he 'will then be extremely likely to commit additional crimes, which exposes the American public to crimes from this individual.” Id. at 114.</p> <p id="b144-7" pgmap="144">We are not persuaded that’ the district court’s abject view of Singh’s record and prospects for reform is supported by the record. As discussed above, although Singh had eight prior convictions, the district court may have overstated their seriousness. Four of the convictions were more than twenty years old, involving crimes committed by Singh when he was only 21 or 22 year’s old, and two others were more than ten years old. None involved guns or violence or narcotics. Three of the more recént convictions resulted only in conditional discharges, without any term of prison or probation. A sentencing judge has the formidable task of trying, to some extent, to predict the future, as she must determine whether the defendant has really learned his lesson, whether he is likely to break the law again, and whether he will be able to turn his life around for the better. While the trial judge’s decisions on these difficult matters are entitled to great deference, we do not believe the record here supports the trial, court’s apparent conclusion that Singh was essentially beyond redemption. See Yuen Jung v. Barber, <span class="citation" data-id="225751"><a href="/opinion/225751/yuen-jung-v-barber/#495" aria-description="Citation for case: Yuen Jung v. Barber">184 F.2d 491, 495</a></span> (9th Cir. 1950) (“All modern legislation dealing with crime and punishment proceeds upon the theory that aside from capital cases, no man is beyond redemption.”),</p> <p id="b144-8" pgmap="144(45) 145(66)"> A sentencing determination based on clearly erroneous factual findings is procedurally unreasonable. See United States v. Chu, <span class="citation" data-id="866796"><a href="/opinion/866796/united-states-v-chu/#746" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Chu">714 F.3d at 746</a></span>; United States v. Cossey, <span class="citation" data-id="183791"><a href="/opinion/183791/united-states-v-cossey/#86" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cossey">632 F.3d 82, 86</a></span> (2d Cir, 2011) (per curiam)1. A variance; such as that imposed here—some three times the high end of the range—must be based on an accurate reading of the record. A remand for clarification of the facts is therefore appropriate. See United States v. Juwa, <span class="citation" data-id="1229535"><a href="/opinion/1229535/united-states-v-juwa/#699" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Juwa">508 F.3d 694, 699-700</a></span> (2d Cir. 2007) (remanding for resentencing where there was uncertainty as to whether district court had assumed defendant had engaged in “multiple instances of sexual abuse, as opposed to [a] single instance”).</p> <p id="b145-5" pgmap="145">b. The Question of Acceptance of Responsibility</p> <p id="b145-6" pgmap="145">At the outset of the sentencing, the district court explained that it was not “inclined to allow the acceptance of responsibility deductions.” App. at 90. After hearing from counsel, the district court advised that it did not “want to create an appeal point” and granted Singh the acceptance points, but noted that it would consider the acceptance of responsibility issues “under 3553(a).” App. at 100. Hence, despite giving Singh credit for acceptance of responsibility, the district court remained concerned that Singh had not fully accepted responsibility. The concern emanated from statements in Singh’s letter to the court that he had been acting foolishly and selfishly, under the influence of friends, when he committed his earlier crimes and that he had returned to the United States in part for fear of his life in Guyana.</p> <p id="b145-7" pgmap="145">It appears that the district concluded that Singh’s comments in his letter were a basis for imposing a higher sentence and that the district court may have imposed a substantive variance and punished Singh for trying to offer explanations for his conduct. If indeed the district court conflated Singh’s statements in mitigation with a failure to accept responsibility, then it committed procedural error.</p> <p id="b145-8" pgmap="145">Considered as a whole, Singh’s letter was an apology and a statement of remorse. It was a plea for mercy-and an attempt to explain his motivations. Singh stated that he was “so sorry” for .his actions and admitted that he had engaged in “wrongdoing.” App. at 76. He acknowledged that he had caused his family and others a lot of “pain and trouble.” Id, He explained that his “long jail month’s” (he had been in prison for. more .than eight months at the time) had taught him a “hard lesson, not to do anything wrong again.” Id, at 78. He promised not “to break the law again for the sake of (his] daughter and family.” <span class="citation" data-id="1229535"><a href="/opinion/1229535/united-states-v-juwa/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Juwa">Id.</a></span> It was in. this context that Singh made the statements of concern to the district court,</p> <p id="b145-10" pgmap="145"> A defendant’s acceptance of responsibility and his assertion of mitigating circumstances are not necessarily inconsistent or incompatible. The concepts are related but independent. Cf. United States v. Douglas, <span class="citation" data-id="66399"><a href="/opinion/66399/united-states-v-douglas/#527" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Douglas">569 F.3d 523, 527</a></span> (5th Cir. 2009) (“We hold that ‘lack of remorse’ and ‘acceptance- of responsibility’ can be separate factors and that a district court may consider each independently of the other.”); United States v. Andrews, <span class="citation" data-id="36874"><a href="/opinion/36874/united-states-v-andrews/#847" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Andrews">390 F.3d 840, 847-48</a></span> (5th Cir. 2004) (holding, in pre-Booker case, that district court-erred in upwardly departing from Guidelines range on grounds defendant “had not completely accepted 'responsibility,” where it had granted defendant acceptance-of-responsibility reduction in calculating range),</p> <p id="b145-11" pgmap="145(74) 146(189)"> The Guidelines authorize a two-step decrease in offense -level if á defendant “clearly demonstrates acceptance of responsibility.” U.S. Sentencing Guidelines Manual § 3El;l(a). An additional one-step decrease is available, “upon motion of the government,” where the defendant’s offense ■ level without any acceptance-of-responsibility credit is at least 16 and his “timely” notification of his intent to plead guilty saves the government from preparing for trial and permits “the gov-eminent and the court to allocate their resources efficiently.” Id. § 3El.l(b); see generally United States v. Delacruz, <span class="citation" data-id="4183450"><a href="/opinion/4406197/united-states-v-delacruz/#177" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Delacruz">862 F.3d 163, 177</a></span> (2d Cir. 2017). “‘[T]he paramount factor in determining eligibility for § 3E1.1 credit is whether the defendant truthfully admits the conduct comprising the offense or offenses of conviction.’” United States v. Kumar, <span class="citation" data-id="9436861"><a href="/opinion/152880/united-states-v-kumar/#637" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Kumar">617 F.3d 612, 637</a></span> (2d Cir. 2010) (quoting United States v. Teyer, <span class="citation" data-id="8785961"><a href="/opinion/8801795/alford-v-whitsel/#376" aria-description="Citation for case: Alford v. Whitsel">322 F.Supp. 359, 376</a></span> (S.D.N.Y. 2004)). In deciding whether to grant acceptance-of-responsibility credit, the district court is to consider, inter alia, whether the defendant “truthfully admitted] the conduct comprising the offense(s) of conviction, and truthfully admitted] or not falsely denfied] any additional relevant conduct.” U.S. . Sentencing Guidelines Manual § 3E1.1 Application Note 1(A). A defendant is not required, however, “to volunteer, or affirmatively admit, relevant conduct beyond the offense of conviction” to qualify for the reduction. Id.; see United States v. Zapata, <span class="citation" data-id="194834"><a href="/opinion/194834/united-states-v-zapata-medina/#49" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Zapata-Medina">1 F.3d 46, 49-50</a></span> (1st Cir. 1993) (conduct underlying an “aggravated felony” for purposes of <span class="citation no-link">8 U.S.C. § 1326</span>(b)(2) is not conduct “part of the instapt offense” under § 4A1.2(a)(l) nor relevant conduct).</p> <p id="b146-4" pgmap="146"> A defendant has an “absolute right” to'be heard at sentencing to offer mitigating circumstances. United States v. Feng Li, <span class="citation" data-id="741740"><a href="/opinion/741740/united-states-v-feng-li-aka-li-feng-zhao-hui-aka-hui-zhao-ru-jie-lu/#132" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Feng Li, AKA Li Feng Zhao Hui, AKA Hui...">115 F.3d 125, 132-33</a></span> (2d Cir. 1997) (citation and internal quotation marks omitted). Rule 32 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires the coprt, before imposing sentence, “to permit the defendant to speak or present any information to mitigate the sentence.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 32 (i) (4) (A) (ii). A defendant’s right to “attempt to mitigate punishment” has “historical roots in the common law,” and “the opportunity to plead for mercy is another provision in a procedural body of law designed to enable our system of justice to mete out punishment in the most equitable fashion possible, to help ensure that sentencing is particularized and reflects individual circumstances.” Feng Li, <span class="citation" data-id="741740"><a href="/opinion/741740/united-states-v-feng-li-aka-li-feng-zhao-hui-aka-hui-zhao-ru-jie-lu/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Feng Li, AKA Li Feng Zhao Hui, AKA Hui...">115 F.3d at 133</a></span> (quoting United States v. Barnes, <span class="citation" data-id="571297"><a href="/opinion/571297/united-states-v-walter-barnes/#328" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Walter Barnes">948 F.2d 325, 328</a></span> (7th Cir. 1991)) (internal quotation marks omitted).<footnotemark>6</footnotemark> </p> <p id="b146-7" pgmap="146(219) 147(41)"> Singh’s assertions that he was “foolis[h], selfish and dumb” when he followed his friends in committing crimes some twenty years earlier, when he was only 21 or 22 years old, and that he returned to the United States in part for fear of his life (and to be with his family) were not, in our view, inconsistent with his acknowledgment of responsibility and wrongdoing. App. at 77. These comments did not indicate a lack of remorse, but were merely an effort by Singh to explain why he broke the law. He truthfully admitted the conduct comprising the offense of conviction. A defendant’s motivation for engaging in criminal conduct is unquestionably a proper consideration at sentencing. See, e.g., Stewart, <span class="citation" data-id="9628995"><a href="/opinion/1434123/united-states-v-stewart/#140" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Stewart">590 F.3d at 140-41</a></span> (“In evaluating culpability, we cannot discount the relevance of the defendant’s motivations—i.e., whether mercenary, see, e.g., <span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 1958</span> (murder for hire), or born from a commitment to the use of violence.”); United States v. Hansen, <span class="citation" data-id="415372"><a href="/opinion/415372/united-states-v-arthur-hansen/#1083" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Arthur Hansen">701 F.2d 1078, 1083</a></span> (2d Cir. 1983) (noting “ ‘the long unbr'oken tradition of the criminal law that harsh sanctions should not be imposed where moral culpability is lacking’” (quoting Lennon v. INS, <span class="citation" data-id="9462299"><a href="/opinion/331607/john-winston-ono-lennon-v-immigration-and-naturalization-service/#193" aria-description="Citation for case: John Winston Ono Lennon v. Immigration and Naturalization...">527 F.2d 187, 193</a></span> (2d Cir. 1975))); accord Porter v. McCollum, <span class="citation" data-id="1756"><a href="/opinion/1756/porter-v-mccollum/#41" aria-description="Citation for case: Porter v. McCollum">558 U.S. 30, 41</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="1756"><a href="/opinion/1756/porter-v-mccollum/" aria-description="Citation for case: Porter v. McCollum">130 S.Ct. 447</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="1756"><a href="/opinion/1756/porter-v-mccollum/" aria-description="Citation for case: Porter v. McCollum">175 L.Ed.2d 398</a></span> (2009) (holding that defense counsel failed to provide effective assistance where “[t]he judge and jury at [defendant’s] original sentencing heard almost nothing that would humanize [defendant] or allow them accurately to gauge his moral culpability”).<footnotemark>7</footnotemark> </p> <p id="b147-5" pgmap="147">There may be cases, of course, where a defendant’s claim of mitigating circumstances does undermine his acceptance of responsibility, and the district court may take that lack of genuineness into account. See U.S. Sentencing Guidelines Manual § 3E.1 Application Note 3. An example is provided by United States v. Douglas, in which the Fifth Circuit affirmed a sentence where the district court granted acceptance-of-responsibility credit but then upwardly departed because of the defendant’s lack of remorse. <span class="citation" data-id="66399"><a href="/opinion/66399/united-states-v-douglas/#527" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Douglas">569 F.3d at 527-28</a></span>.</p> <p id="b147-6" pgmap="147">There the defendant was charged with felony possession of a weapon; he had handed a loaded handgun to a child who then accidentally shot himself. The district court awarded the defendant aceeptance-of-responsibility credit, but departed above the Guidelines range because of the defendant’s lack of remorse. <span class="citation" data-id="66399"><a href="/opinion/66399/united-states-v-douglas/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Douglas">Id.</a></span> The sentencing allocution included the following exchange:</p> <blockquote id="b147-7" pgmap="147">The Defendant: I have nothing to say. I have nothing to say your honor.</blockquote> <blockquote id="b147-8" pgmap="147">The Court: So you have not learned anything from the offense?</blockquote> <blockquote id="b147-9" pgmap="147">The Defendant: Have I learned anything?</blockquote> <blockquote id="b147-10" pgmap="147">The Court: That’s exactly what I asked.</blockquote> <blockquote id="b147-11" pgmap="147">The Defendant: I should have stayed in Mexico. I shouldn’t have come back.</blockquote> <blockquote id="b147-13" pgmap="147">The Court: You have no remorse for what happened?</blockquote> <blockquote id="b147-14" pgmap="147">The Defendant: I didn’t do it. He [the child] done it to himself.</blockquote> <p id="b147-15" pgmap="147"><span class="citation" data-id="66399"><a href="/opinion/66399/united-states-v-douglas/#525" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Douglas">Id. at 525</a></span>. In contrast, in this case, Singh did show remorse. His efforts to seek mercy did not undermine his acceptance of responsibility.</p> <p id="b147-16" pgmap="147">“Sentencing, that is to say punishment, is perhaps the most difficult task of a trial court judge.” Jack B. Weinstein, Does Religion Have a Role in Criminal Sentencing?, <span class="citation no-link">23 Touro L. Rev. 539</span>, 539 (2007). While there are many competing considerations in every sentencing decision, a sentencing judge must have some understanding of “the diverse frailties of humankind.” See Woodson v. North Carolina, <span class="citation" data-id="9426567"><a href="/opinion/109535/woodson-v-north-carolina/#304" aria-description="Citation for case: Woodson v. North Carolina">428 U.S. 280, 304</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9426567"><a href="/opinion/109535/woodson-v-north-carolina/" aria-description="Citation for case: Woodson v. North Carolina">96 S.Ct. 2978</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9426567"><a href="/opinion/109535/woodson-v-north-carolina/" aria-description="Citation for case: Woodson v. North Carolina">49 L.Ed.2d 944</a></span> (1976) (plurality opinion). In deciding what sentence will be “sufficient, but not greater than necessary” to further the goals of punishment, <span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 3553</span>(a), a sentencing judge must have a “generosity of spirit, that compassion which causes one to know what it is like to be in trouble and in pain.” Guido Calabresi, What Makes a Judge Great: To A. Leon Higginbotham, Jr., <span class="citation no-link">142 U. Pa. L. Rev. 513</span>, 513 (1993); see also Edward J. Devitt, Ten Commandments for the New Judge, <span class="citation no-link">65 A.B.A. J. 574</span> (1979), reprinted in <span class="citation no-link">82 F.R.D. 209</span>, 209 (1979) (“Be kind. If we judges could possess but one attribute, it should be a kind and understanding heart. The bench is no place for cruel or callous people regardless of their other qualities and abilities. There is no burden more onerous than imposing sentence in criminal cases.”).</p> <p id="b148-3" pgmap="148">To the extent the district court increased Singh’s punishment because of a perception that in attempting to explain his actions and plead for mercy he did not fully accept responsibility, it committed procedural error.</p> <p id="b148-4" pgmap="148">C. The Request for Reassignment</p> <p id="b148-5" pgmap="148">Singh requests that we order that the case be reassigned bn remand to a different judge for resentencing.</p> <p id="b148-6" pgmap="148"> “Remanding a case to a different judge is a serious request rarely made and rarely granted.” United States v. Awadallah, <span class="citation" data-id="793137"><a href="/opinion/793137/united-states-v-osama-awadallah/#135" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Osama Awadallah">436 F.3d 125, 135</a></span> (2d Cir. 2006). We will grant- a request for reassignment on remand only in ' “ ‘unusual circumstances.’ ” United States v. Brennan, <span class="citation" data-id="788926"><a href="/opinion/788926/united-states-v-robert-e-brennan/#75" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Robert E. Brennan">395 F.3d 59, 75</a></span> (2d Cir. 2005) (quoting United States v. Robin, <span class="citation" data-id="344530"><a href="/opinion/344530/united-states-v-raymond-robin/#10" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Raymond Robin">553 F.2d 8, 10</a></span> (2d Cir. 1977)). We are not persuaded that reassignment is necessary or appropriate here. We are confident that on remand the experienced and capable district judge will conduct a full resentencing, in compliance with all . procedural requirements, and impose a sentence that is fair, reasonable, and sufficient but not longer than necessary to meet the goals of justice.</p> <p id="b148-16" pgmap="148">CONCLUSION</p> <p id="b148-17" pgmap="148">The judgment of the district court is VACATED' and the case is REMANDED for resentencing.
