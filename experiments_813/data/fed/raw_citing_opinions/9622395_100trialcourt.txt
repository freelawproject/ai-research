2       The Court provided a thorough recitation of this action’s factual and procedural 
3  background in the September 11, 2023 Order Denying Plaintiff’s Motion for Preliminary 
4  Injunction (the “Order,” ECF No. 15).  The Court repeats the relevant facts below, for ease

6       Plaintiff claims Defendant, an organization that “develops and enforces standards 
7  for DNA paternity and other relationship testing,” Verified Complaint (“Compl.”) ¶ 15, 
8  ECF No. 1, has amended its standards to permit “non-traditional” or “lookalike” paternity 
9  tests that “(1) violate[] the DOJ Directive to Judges deciding DNA paternity cases[,] (2) 
10  violate[] state statutes[,] (3) [are] not peer-reviewed, [and] (4) [were] never validated,” id. 
11  ¶¶ 12–13, 20.  Plaintiff alleges that in a “non-traditional” or “lookalike” paternity test, 
12  “maternal DNA is removed.”  Id. ¶ 27.  Such tests, Plaintiff contends, allow laboratories to 
13  issue “faux DNA results concerning biology but call it forensic science,” while Defendant 
14  “can rubber stamp its seal thereon . . . so the lookalike ‘results’ can be turned into money 
15  in both public and private markets.”  Id. ¶ 23.  Plaintiff explains:      
16            [W]hen these result[s] are used to create fictitious relationships 
             out  of  activities  that  never  occurred,  and  then  paraded  as 
17                                                                            
             “positive” evidence of paternity at 99.999% and which the target 
18            either (1) knows the result is impossible or (2) discovers the  
             result is fraudulent through a subsequent “forensic” tests, the 
19                                                                            
             targeted (adult) may suffer a shaken faith like syndrome with   
20            respect to his future lab/medical testing.

26  Defendant’s fraudulent attempt to switch the standards for commercial DNA testing in the

1                           MOTION TO DISMISS                                
2  I.   Legal Standards                                                      
3       A.   Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1)                        
4       Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction and thus have an obligation to 
5  dismiss claims for which they lack subject-matter jurisdiction.  Demarest v. United States, 
6  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="425254"><a href="/opinion/425254/stephen-h-demarest-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephen H. Demarest v. United States">718  F.2d  964</a></span><pre class="inline">,  965–66  (9th  Cir.  1983).    The  burden  of  establishing  subject-matter 
7  jurisdiction is on the party asserting jurisdiction.  Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of 
8  Am., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="117845"><a href="/opinion/117845/kokkonen-v-guardian-life-insurance-co-of-america/#377" aria-description="Citation for case: Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Insurance Co. of America">511 U.S. 375, 377</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1994).                                            
9       Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1), a party may raise by motion the 
10  defense that the complaint lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, and may do so via a facial or 
11  factual attack.  See White v. Lee, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6987266"><a href="/opinion/7082005/white-v-lee/#1242" aria-description="Citation for case: White v. Lee">227 F.3d 1214, 1242</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2000).  “In a facial attack, 
12  the challenger asserts that the allegations contained in a complaint are insufficient on their 
13  face to invoke federal jurisdiction.”  Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408936"><a href="/opinion/8438360/safe-air-for-everyone-v-meyer/" aria-description="Citation for case: Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer">373 F.3d 1035</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1039 
14  (9th Cir. 2004).  A court resolves a facial attack as it would under Rule 12(b)(6) motion: 
15  “Accepting the plaintiff’s allegations as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the 
16  plaintiff’s favor, the court determines whether the allegations are sufficient . . . to invoke 
17  the court’s jurisdiction.”  Leite v. Crane Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2671500"><a href="/opinion/2671500/douglas-leite-v-crane-company/#1121" aria-description="Citation for case: Douglas Leite v. Crane Company">749 F.3d 1117, 1121</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014).   
18       In a factual attack, on the other hand, “the challenger disputes the truth of the 
19  allegations that, by themselves, would otherwise invoke federal jurisdiction.”  Safe Air, 
20  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408936"><a href="/opinion/8438360/safe-air-for-everyone-v-meyer/#1039" aria-description="Citation for case: Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer">373 F.3d  at 1039</a></span><pre class="inline">.    In  such  challenges,  courts  (1)  “may  review  evidence beyond the 
21  complaint without converting the motion to dismiss into a motion for summary judgment,” 
22  and (2) “need not presume the truthfulness of the plaintiff&#x27;s allegations.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408936"><a href="/opinion/8438360/safe-air-for-everyone-v-meyer/" aria-description="Citation for case: Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  After the 
23  moving party evidences the lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, the party opposing the 
24  motion must “present affidavits or any other evidence necessary to satisfy its burden of 
25  establishing that the court, in fact, possesses subject matter jurisdiction.”  St. Clair v. City 
26  of Chico, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="526528"><a href="/opinion/526528/william-st-clair-john-shelton-v-city-of-chico-county-of-butte/#201" aria-description="Citation for case: William St. Clair John Shelton v. City of Chico County of...">880 F.2d 199, 201</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1989).                              
27       Dismissal under Rule 12(b)(1) is warranted “where the alleged claim under the 
28  Constitution or federal statutes clearly appears to be immaterial and made solely for the 
1  purpose of obtaining federal jurisdiction or where such claim is wholly insubstantial and 
2  frivolous.”  Safe Air, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408936"><a href="/opinion/8438360/safe-air-for-everyone-v-meyer/" aria-description="Citation for case: Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer">373 F.3d at 1039</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Bell v. Hood, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9419809"><a href="/opinion/104272/bell-v-hood/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell v. Hood">327 U.S. 678</a></span><pre class="inline">, 682–83 
3  (1946)).  But dismissal under 12(b)(1) is not appropriate when “the jurisdictional issue and 
4  substantive issues are so intertwined that the question of jurisdiction is dependent on the 
5  resolution of factual issues going to the merits of the action.”  Sun Valley Gasoline, Inc. v. 
6  Ernst Enter., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="421062"><a href="/opinion/421062/sun-valley-gasoline-inc-v-ernst-enterprises-inc-richard-h-ernst-and/#139" aria-description="Citation for case: Sun Valley Gasoline, Inc. v. Ernst Enterprises, Inc.,...">711 F.2d 138, 139</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1983) (internal quotation marks omitted) 
7  (quoting Augustine v. United States, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="416723"><a href="/opinion/416723/richard-augustine-v-united-states/#1077" aria-description="Citation for case: Richard Augustine v. United States">704 F.2d 1074, 1077</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1983)). 
8       B.   Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6)                        
9       Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) permits a party to raise by motion the 
10  defense that the complaint “fail[s] to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.”  To 
11  survive a 12(b)(6) motion, “a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as 
12  true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
13  678 (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007)).  A claim is 
14  facially plausible when the facts pled “allow[] the court to draw the reasonable inference 
15  that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  That is not to say that the claim 
16  must be probable, but there must be “more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has 
17  acted unlawfully.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Facts “‘merely consistent with’ a defendant’s liability” fall short of 
18  a plausible entitlement to relief.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#557" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 557</a></span><pre class="inline">).   
19       Though  this  plausibility  standard  “does  not  require  ‘detailed  factual 
20  allegations,’ . . . it [does] demand[] more than an unadorned, the-defendant-unlawfully-
21  harmed-me accusation.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 555</a></span><pre class="inline">).  A complaint will not 
22  suffice “if it tenders ‘naked assertion[s]’ devoid of ‘further factual enhancement.’”  Id. 
23  (alteration in original) (quoting Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#557" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 557</a></span><pre class="inline">).  Put differently, “a formulaic 
24  recitation of the elements of a cause of action will not do.”  Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 555</a></span><pre class="inline">. 
25       Review  under  Rule  12(b)(6)  requires  a  context-specific  analysis  involving  the 
26  Court’s “judicial experience and common sense.”  Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#679" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. at 679</a></span><pre class="inline">.  In performing 
27  that analysis, “a district court must accept as true all facts alleged in the complaint” and 
28  “draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the plaintiff.”  Wi-LAN Inc. v. LG Elecs., Inc., 
1  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7254030"><a href="/opinion/7336110/wi-lan-inc-v-lg-elecs-inc/#1020" aria-description="Citation for case: Wi-Lan Inc. v. LG Elecs., Inc.">382 F. Supp. 3d 1012, 1020</a></span><pre class="inline"> (S.D. Cal. 2019).  If a complaint does not survive Rule 
2  12(b)(6), a court grants leave to amend unless it determines that no modified contention 
3  “consistent  with  the  challenged  pleading  could . . . possibly  cure  the  deficiency.”  
4  Schreiber Distrib. Co. v. Serv-Well Furniture Co., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.2d/806/1393/">806 F.2d 1393</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1401 (9th Cir. 1986). 
5       C.   Applicability of a Deferential Pleading Standard                
6       Ordinarily, courts have a duty to construe a pro se2 litigant’s pleadings liberally.  See 
7  Lopez v. Smith, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6985107"><a href="/opinion/7079981/lopez-v-smith/#1130" aria-description="Citation for case: Lopez v. Smith">203 F.3d 1122, 1130</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2000).  That liberal pleading standard, 
8  however,  does  not  apply  to  pro  se  “practicing  attorneys.”    Huffman  v.  Lindgren, 
9  </pre><span class="citation no-link">81 F.4th 1016</span><pre class="inline">, 1021 (9th Cir. 2023).  To the extent solicitude extends to plaintiffs who 
10  have attended law school or have past litigation experience, said solicitude is generally 
11  reduced.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">id.</span><pre class="inline"> at 1020–21; see also Tracy v. Freshwater, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="177179"><a href="/opinion/177179/tracy-v-freshwater/#102" aria-description="Citation for case: Tracy v. Freshwater">623 F.3d 90, 102</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2d Cir. 2010) 
12  (“[T]he degree of solicitude may be lessened where the [litigant proceeding pro se] is 
13  experienced in litigation and familiar with the procedural setting presented.”). 
14       As Defendant points out, and Plaintiff does not dispute, Plaintiff was until recently 
15  a practicing attorney in the State of Minnesota.  See Lawyer Details, Minn. Judicial Branch, 
16  https://mars.courts.state.mn.us/AttorneyDetail.aspx?attyID=0270933  (last  visited 
17  Jan. 23, 2024).  In fact, Plaintiff was admitted to the Minnesota State Bar in 1996 and did 
18  not leave until he failed to pay his fees in 2021.  See </pre><span class="citation" data-id="177179"><a href="/opinion/177179/tracy-v-freshwater/" aria-description="Citation for case: Tracy v. Freshwater">id.</a></span><pre class="inline">               
19       To the Court’s knowledge, the Ninth Circuit has never determined whether a former 
20  attorney deserves the grace granted to other pro se individuals.  District courts, meanwhile, 
21  have expressed different views.3  Without binding precedent, the Court turns to Huffman

12  II.  Analysis                                                             
13       Defendant argues the Complaint must be dismissed because it fails to satisfy several 
14  initial requirements.  The Court must thus decide the order in which to tackle Defendant’s 
15  arguments.  Two threshold issues raised by Defendant—the Rooker-Feldman doctrine and 
16  Article III standing—go to the Court’s subject matter jurisdiction.  See Brown v. Duringer 
17  L. Grp. PLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">86 F.4th 1251</span><pre class="inline">, 1255 n.1 (9th Cir. 2023).  A third, issue preclusion, does not.  
18  See Snoqualmie Indian Tribe v. Washington, </pre><span class="citation no-link">8 F.4th 853</span><pre class="inline">, 861 (9th Cir. 2021).   
19       Courts may address non-merits threshold issues before jurisdictional questions when 
20  doing so is “less burdensome.”  Id. at 863.  Here, Rooker-Feldman does not pose a 
21  particularly arduous question, so the Court will start there.  That leaves standing and issue 
22  preclusion.  Defendant’s issue-preclusion argument targets all of Plaintiff’s claims, while 
23  Defendant’s  standing  theory  applies  differently  to  the  various  injuries  alleged  in  the 
24  Complaint.  Further, Defendant’s standing claim relies on its issue preclusion argument, 
25  see Mem. at 4–5, complicating the Court’s ability to assess standing before preclusion.  
26  Finally, Plaintiff did not respond to the Motion’s specific standing arguments; instead, he 
27  incorporated a section of an earlier brief relating to a different motion.  See Opp’n at 13.  
28  Plaintiff’s Opposition does, however, take on issue preclusion.  See id. at 14.   
1       With the above in mind, the Court will begin by addressing Defendant’s Rooker-

14       B.   Issue Preclusion                                                
15            1.   Legal Framework                                            
16       Issue preclusion, also known as collateral estoppel, “bars the relitigation of issues 
17  actually adjudicated in previous litigation.”  Clark v. Bear Stearns &amp; Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9001640"><a href="/opinion/9008822/clark-v-bear-stearns-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Clark v. Bear Stearns &amp; Co.">966 F.2d 1318</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
18  1320 (9th Cir. 1992).  Federal courts “considering whether to apply issue preclusion based 
19  on a prior state court judgment must look to state preclusion law.”  Hardwick v. Cnty. of 
20  Orange, </pre><span class="citation no-link">980 F.3d 733</span><pre class="inline">, 740 (9th Cir. 2020) (quoting McInnes v. California, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="567733"><a href="/opinion/567733/theresa-mcinnes-plaintiff-appellant-v-state-of-california-california/" aria-description="Citation for case: Theresa McINNES, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. STATE OF...">943 F.2d 1088</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
21  1092–93 (9th Cir. 1991)).                                                 
22       Relevant here is California law, under which issue preclusion applies “(1) after final 
23  adjudication (2) of an identical issue (3) actually litigated and necessarily decided in the 
24  first suit and (4) asserted against one who was a party in the first suit or one in privity with 
25  that party.”  Samara v. Matar, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4287856"><a href="/opinion/4510603/samara-v-matar/#926" aria-description="Citation for case: Samara v. Matar">419 P.3d 924, 926</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. 2018) (quoting DKN Holdings LLC 
26  v. Faerber, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2816609"><a href="/opinion/2816609/dkn-holdings-llc-v-faerber/#387" aria-description="Citation for case: DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber">352 P.3d 378, 387</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. 2015)).  Even when those elements are met, however, 
27  preclusion will not apply under certain circumstances.  “[P]reclusion exceptions include 
28  public policy, unforeseeability, and the inability or lack of incentive to litigate the prior 
1  adjudication.”  In Re CWS Enterprises, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4203463"><a href="/opinion/4426210/mcproud-v-siller-in-re-cws-enterprises-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: McProud v. Siller (In Re CWS Enterprises, Inc.)">870 F.3d 1106</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1119 n.37 (9th Cir. 2017) 
2  (citing Olson v. Cory, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2117735"><a href="/opinion/2117735/olson-v-cory/" aria-description="Citation for case: Olson v. Cory">184 Cal. Rptr. 325</a></span><pre class="inline">, 336 n.9 (Ct. App. 1982)).      
3       Courts often “look[] to the record of the prior proceeding to determine whether an 
4  issue was in fact raised, contested, and submitted for determination.”  Janjua v. Neufeld, 
5  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4424397"><a href="/opinion/4647144/khalil-janjua-v-donald-neufeld/#1065" aria-description="Citation for case: Khalil Janjua v. Donald Neufeld">933 F.3d 1061, 1065</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2019).  The party seeking to rely upon issue preclusion 
6  carries the burden of proving that each element has been met.  Kendall v. Visa U.S.A., Inc., 
7  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1404453"><a href="/opinion/1404453/kendall-v-visa-usa-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Kendall v. Visa U.S.A., Inc.">518 F.3d 1042</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1050–51 (9th Cir. 2008).  However, “[t]he party seeking the benefit of the 
8  doctrine . . . need not have been a party to the earlier lawsuit.”  Arias v. Superior Ct., 
9  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9795869"><a href="/opinion/2625696/arias-v-superior-court/#932" aria-description="Citation for case: Arias v. Superior Court">209 P.3d 923, 932</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. 2009).
