18   I.   BACKGROUND                                                       
19        Western Digital is a leading global and data storage brand incorporated in Delaware and 
20   headquartered in California.  ECF No. 37 ¶¶ 6, 17 (“FAC”).  The company offers technologies, 
21   devices, systems, and solutions to businesses and consumers, including external hard drives that 
22   are compatible with Apple MacBook computers.  Id. ¶¶ 2–4, 6.  Kevin Riordan, Ashley Laurent, 
23   Jeremy Bobo, and Nagui Sorial (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) are four individuals who reside in 
24   Tennessee, Minnesota, and California who had purchased and used Western Digital’s data storage 
25   devices.  Id. ¶¶ 21, 24, 32, 35, 45, 48, 53, 56.  Plaintiffs each lost data stored on these devices 
26   because of a cyber-attack.  Id. ¶¶ 22, 33, 46, 54.  Plaintiffs bring this class action against Western 
27   Digital for failure to properly secure and safeguard Plaintiffs’ and proposed class members’ 
1   personal, commercial, and proprietary information (“Stored Data”) within Western Digital’s data 
2   storage devices.  Id. ¶ 2.                                            
3        Two of Western Digital’s internet-connected devices are at issue in this action: My Book 
4   Live and My Book Duo (“Data Storage Devices” or “Covered Products”).  Id. ¶ 4.  Western 
5   Digital manufactured the Data Storage Devices in the early 2010s and has not supported them 
6   since 2015.  Id. ¶ 90.  The Data Storage Devices purportedly exhibit flaws stemming from product 
7   design and inadequate continued testing and monitoring of the devices.  Id. ¶ 93.  Plaintiffs allege 
8   that Western Digital knew of the flaws in the hardware since at least 2018.  Id. ¶ 91.   
9        On June 23, 2021, Western Digital announced that it was experiencing a cyber-attack on 
10   the Data Storage Devices.  Id. ¶ 80.  The hackers remotely executed a malicious code in the 
11   operating system of the Data Storage Devices and reset the devices’ factory settings without 
12   requiring a password.  Id. ¶ 81.  As a result, Plaintiffs’ Stored Data was wiped from Western 
13   Digital’s Data Storage Devices and users were unable to log into their devices using their own 
14   credentials.  Id. ¶¶ 81–82.  The lost data includes but is not limited to personal photos, information 
15   relating to users’ work and businesses, and financial information.  Id. ¶ 82.  Users also stored 
16   digital key data that could be used to access their digital wallets and accounts, such as 
17   cryptocurrency.  Id.  Plaintiffs are unaware of whether their Stored Data was merely wiped or 
18   whether the information is also in the hands of cyber criminals.  Id. ¶ 100.   
19        Following the cyber-attack, Western Digital announced a data recovery service program 
20   and offered to trade in affected devices for an upgraded product.  Id. ¶ 84.  To be eligible, affected 
21   users were required to contact Western Digital’s customer support center to receive a “case 
22   number” by July 31, 2021.  Id.  Data recovery operations “have mixed rates of success” and “it is 
23   highly probable that a significant portion of the lost data would be gone forever, corrupted, etc.”  
24   Id. ¶ 85.  According to Plaintiffs, it is unlikely that Western Digital will be able to reverse the 
25   effects of the factory reset and recover the Stored Data.  Id. ¶ 86.  
26        For example, Riordan stored priceless family photos and videos on his Data Storage 
27   Device which date back to the mid-1990s that memorialize important family events and 
1   milestones.  Id. ¶ 27.  Riordan contacted Western Digital’s customer support to assist with 
2   restoration of his lost data, but the attempts were unsuccessful.  Id. ¶ 30.  Riordan was able to 
3   partially recreate his photo and video library using data he had backed up on previous hard drives, 
4   but the bulk of his Stored Data (i.e., hundreds of thousands of photos and videos) has been 
    irretrievably lost.  Id. ¶¶ 27, 31.

14   II.  LEGAL STANDARDS                                                  
15        A.   Rule 12(b)(1)                                               
         Federal Rule of Procedure 12(b)(1) permits a party to assert a defense of lack of subject 
16                                                                         
    matter jurisdiction.  Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(1).  A jurisdictional attack may be factual or facial.  
17                                                                         
    White v. Lee, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6987266"><a href="/opinion/7082005/white-v-lee/#1242" aria-description="Citation for case: White v. Lee">227 F.3d 1214, 1242</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2000).  A facial attack “asserts that the allegations 
18                                                                         
    contained in a complaint are insufficient on their face to invoke federal jurisdiction.”  Safe Air for 
19                                                                         
    Everyone v. Meyer, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408936"><a href="/opinion/8438360/safe-air-for-everyone-v-meyer/#1039" aria-description="Citation for case: Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer">373 F.3d 1035, 1039</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2004). “The district court resolves a facial 
20                                                                         
    attack as it would a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6): Accepting the plaintiff’s allegations as 
21                                                                         
    true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff&#x27;s favor, the court determines whether the 
22                                                                         
    allegations are sufficient as a legal matter to invoke the court’s jurisdiction.”  Leite v. Crane Co., 
23                                                                         
    </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2671500"><a href="/opinion/2671500/douglas-leite-v-crane-company/#1121" aria-description="Citation for case: Douglas Leite v. Crane Company">749 F.3d 1117, 1121</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014).                                  
24                                                                         
         For a factual attack, defendant presents extrinsic evidence for the court’s consideration. In 
25                                                                         
    this case “the court need not presume the truthfulness of the plaintiff&#x27;s allegations.”  Safe Air for 
26                                                                         
    Everyone, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408936"><a href="/opinion/8438360/safe-air-for-everyone-v-meyer/#1039" aria-description="Citation for case: Safe Air for Everyone v. Meyer">373 F.3d at 1039</a></span><pre class="inline">.  “When the defendant raises a factual attack, the plaintiff must 
27                                                                         
1   support her jurisdictional allegations with ‘competent proof,’ under the same evidentiary standard 
2   that governs in the summary judgment context.”  Leite, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2671500"><a href="/opinion/2671500/douglas-leite-v-crane-company/#1121" aria-description="Citation for case: Douglas Leite v. Crane Company">749 F.3d at 1121</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citations omitted). 
3   “[T]he district court is not restricted to the face of the pleadings, but may review any evidence, 
4   such as affidavits and testimony, to resolve factual disputes concerning the existence of 
5   jurisdiction.”  McCarthy v. United States, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="508254"><a href="/opinion/508254/richard-mccarthy-v-united-states/#560" aria-description="Citation for case: Richard McCarthy v. United States">850 F.2d 558, 560</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1988). 
6        B.   Rule 12(b)(6)                                               
7        Rule 8(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure requires a complaint to include “a 
8   short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.”  A complaint 
9   that fails to meet this standard may be dismissed pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6).  Rule 8(a) requires a 
10   plaintiff to plead “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.”  Bell Atl. 
11   Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007).  “A claim has facial plausibility when the plaintiff 
12   pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is 
13   liable for the misconduct alleged.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009).  While plaintiffs 
14   must allege “more than a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully,” the plausibility 
15   standard “is not akin to a probability requirement.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">             
16        For purposes of ruling on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the Court generally “accept[s] factual 
17   allegations in the complaint as true and construe[s] the pleadings in the light most favorable to the 
18   nonmoving party.”  Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1194077"><a href="/opinion/1194077/manzarek-v-st-paul-fire-marine-insurance/#1031" aria-description="Citation for case: Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Insurance">519 F.3d 1025, 1031</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
19   2008).  The Court need not, however, “assume the truth of legal conclusions merely because they 
20   are cast in the form of factual allegations.”  Fayer v. Vaughn, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="216101"><a href="/opinion/216101/fayer-v-vaughn/#1064" aria-description="Citation for case: Fayer v. Vaughn">649 F.3d 1061, 1064</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) 
21   (per curiam).  Mere “conclusory allegations of law and unwarranted inferences are insufficient to 
22   defeat a motion to dismiss.”  Adams v. Johnson, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8408553"><a href="/opinion/8438027/adams-v-johnson/#1183" aria-description="Citation for case: Adams v. Johnson">355 F.3d 1179, 1183</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2004).  The Court 
23   may also “look beyond the plaintiff’s complaint to matters of public record” without converting 
24   the Rule 12(b)(6) motion into a motion for summary judgment.  Shaw v. Hahn, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="697357"><a href="/opinion/697357/gina-shaw-v-james-k-hahn-john-emerson-james-pearson-thomas-hokinson/" aria-description="Citation for case: Gina Shaw v. James K. Hahn John Emerson James Pearson...">56 F.3d 1128</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
25   1129 (9th Cir. 1995).                                                 
26   III.  DISCUSSION                                                      
27        Defendant argues that Plaintiffs lack Article III standing over claims based on future data 
1   misappropriation, prayers for injunctive relief, and putative nationwide class claims, and that 
2   Plaintiffs’ claims must be dismissed under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(1).  In the 
3   alternative, Defendant argues that this action must be dismissed under Federal Rule of Civil 
4   Procedure 12(b)(6) because the Complaint fails to state a claim for which relief can be granted.  
5   The Court addresses each argument in turn.                            
6        A.   Standing                                                    
7        To confer standing, a plaintiff must establish three elements: (i) plaintiff suffered an injury 
8   in fact; (ii) there is a causal connection between the defendant’s conduct and the alleged injury; 
9   and (iii) the injury is redressable by a favorable decision.  Lujan v. Defs. of Wildlife, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">504 U.S. 555</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
10   560 (1992).                                                           
11             1.   Claims Based on Future Data Misuse1                    
12        Defendant facially challenges the Court’s subject matter jurisdiction over Plaintiffs’ claims 
13   to the extent they are based on a theory of injury or seek relief for future data misuse.  Strictly 
14   facial challenges to subject matter jurisdiction are treated as “any other motion to dismiss on the 
15   pleadings for lack of jurisdiction,” and therefore the court must “determine whether the complaint 
16   alleges ‘sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its 
17   face.’”  Terenkian v. Republic of Iraq, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9501417"><a href="/opinion/808723/manuel-terenkian-v-the-republic-of-iraq/#1131" aria-description="Citation for case: Manuel Terenkian v. The Republic of Iraq">694 F.3d 1122, 1131</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012) (quoting Ashcroft v. 
18   Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009)) (quotations and citation omitted).   
19        According to Western Digital, Plaintiffs’ theory that they face a risk of future data misuse 
20   is based on a hypothetical injury.  Mot. at 5.  “To establish injury in fact, a plaintiff must show that 
21   he or she suffered ‘an invasion of a legally protected interest’ that is ‘concrete and particularized’ 
22   and ‘actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.’”  Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, </pre><span class="citation no-link">578 U.S. 330</span><pre class="inline">, 
23                                                                         

24                                                                         
    1 Plaintiffs allege two theories of injury: (1) they lost data stored on the My Book Live device as a 
25   result of the factory reset, and (2) they face a risk of future data misuse “if [their personal data] has 
    made its way into the hands of cyber-criminals.”.  FAC ¶ 100.  In the First Order, the Court found 
26   that Plaintiffs lacked standing under either theory.  See First Order at 7.  Plaintiffs have amended 
    the FAC to include detailed allegations showing concrete and particularized harm with respect to 
27   the four representative plaintiffs by alleging that the permanent loss of their stored data caused 
    them to suffer an injury in fact.  Western Digital does not challenge Plaintiffs’ loss of data injury. 
1   339 (2016) (quoting Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/#560" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">504 U.S. 555, 560</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1992)).  The “risk of real 
2   harm” can satisfy the requirement of concreteness.  Spokeo, 578 U.S. at 341.  A plaintiff alleging a 
3   risk of future harm satisfies the injury in fact requirement where he or she faces a “credible threat 
4   of harm” that is “both real and immediate” and “not conjectural or hypothetical.”  Krottner v. 
5   Starbucks Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="181056"><a href="/opinion/181056/krottner-v-starbucks-corp/#1143" aria-description="Citation for case: Krottner v. Starbucks Corp.">628 F.3d 1139, 1143</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2010).                 
6        The First Order found that Plaintiffs failed to plead any facts to support a risk of future 
7   data misuse, and unsupported speculative allegations of future harm do not establish an injury in 
8   fact.  First Order at 5–6.  Plaintiffs amended their complaint to include allegations that Laurent and 
9   Sorial stored sensitive personal information on their Data Storage Devices, including social 
10   security numbers, naturalization documents, and financial account information, which was wiped 
11   from their Data Storage Devices by cyber criminals during the hack.  Id. ¶¶ 41, 51, 60.  Plaintiffs 
12   allege that if cyber criminals gained access to Plaintiffs and Class Members’ Stored Data during 
13   the hack, it could be used “to perpetrate a variety of crimes that harm victims” such as identity 
14   theft and immigration fraud.  Id. ¶¶ 101, 107.                        
15        Plaintiffs rely on Zappos to argue the fact that the hackers accessed personal information 
16   that could be used to commit a crime establishes a “substantial risk” that the hackers will commit 
17   identity theft or fraud.  In re Zappos.com, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/#1029" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">888 F.3d 1020, 1029</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018).  In Zappos, 
18   plaintiffs’ personal data was accessed during a breach of Zappos.com’s servers where hackers 
19   allegedly stole the names, account numbers, passwords, contact information, and credit and debit 
20   card information of approximately 24 million Zappos customers.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/#1023" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">Id. at 1023</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Some plaintiffs 
21   alleged that the hackers used stolen information about them to conduct subsequent financial 
22   transactions resulting in financial losses while other plaintiffs alleged that the Zappos data breach 
23   put them at “imminent risk” of identity theft.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  The district court dismissed the claims of the 
24   plaintiffs who alleged that the Zappos data breach put them at risk of identity theft for lack of 
25   Article III standing.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  These plaintiffs appealed.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding that 
26   the appellees sufficiently alleged standing based on the risk of identity theft.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">   
27        Zappos is distinguishable from the present action.  In Zappos, plaintiffs alleged that 
1   hackers stole their data.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> at 1027 n.7 (noting that the complaint included emails sent to Zappos 
2   from other customers saying that their credit cards were fraudulently used following the breach).  
3   The Ninth Circuit reasoned that the “sensitivity of the personal information, combined with its 
4   theft, led us to conclude that the plaintiffs had adequately alleged an injury in fact supporting 
5   standing.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9015728"><a href="/opinion/9022502/stevens-v-zapposcom-inc-in-re-zapposcom-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stevens v. Zappos.com., Inc. (In re Zappos.com., Inc.)">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (emphasis added).  Here, Plaintiffs speculate that their lost data may have “made its 
6   way into the hands of cyber criminals.  FAC ¶ 100 (“Plaintiffs and Class Members are unaware as 
7   to whether their Stored Data was merely deleted, or if such information has made its way into the 
8   hands of cyber-criminals.”).  The Zappos court noted that several allegations lent support to 
9   appellees’ assertion that the information stolen by the hackers could be used to commit identity 
10   fraud or theft in the future, including: (1) allegations of the plaintiffs “who alleged that the hackers 
11   had already commandeered their accounts or identities using information taken from Zappos” had 
12   suffered financial losses as a result, and (2) allegations of two appellees that “the hackers took 
13   over their AOL accounts and sent advertisements to people in their address books.”  In re 
14   Zappos.com, Inc., 888 F.3d at 1027–28.  Here, the FAC is devoid of any allegations indicating 
15   data theft.  See e.g., Foster v. Essex Prop. Trust, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2015 WL 7566811</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 
16   25, 2015) (“Since Plaintiffs have not shown, . . . that any of their information was actually stolen, 
17   their theory of future harm is implausible.”); Stasi v. Inmediata Health Grp. Corp., No. 19-CV-
18   2353 JM (LL), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2020 WL 2126317</span><pre class="inline">, at *6 (S.D. Cal. May 5, 2020) (distinguishing Zappos based on 
19   plaintiffs failure to allege “their information was stolen or hacked” and noting that plaintiffs 
20   instead allege “that their information was temporarily accessible via the internet, but not 
21   necessarily copied or even viewed by a potential identity thief”); see also Burns v. Mammoth 
22   Media, Inc., No. 2-CV-004855-DDP-SKX, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 5608389</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (C.D. Cal. Aug. 29, 2023) 
23   (noting that Zappos does not suggest that a hacked account on its own “evidence[s] an ongoing 
24   risk of identity theft or constitute[s] an injury in fact”).  Instead, Plaintiffs rely on mere speculation 
25   and conjecture that their data may have been stolen.                  
26        For these reasons, Plaintiffs have not sufficiently alleged an injury in fact as to their claims 
27   based on future data misuse.  Because Plaintiffs have failed to establish a risk of future injury, they 
1   have not pled any allegations that could support their claims for injunctive relief and therefore lack 
2   standing to pursue such claims.  See Summers v. Earth Island Inst., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435380"><a href="/opinion/145904/summers-v-earth-island-institute/#493" aria-description="Citation for case: Summers v. Earth Island Institute">555 U.S. 488, 493</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009); see 
3   also DaimlerChrysler Corp. v. Cuno, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9434951"><a href="/opinion/145658/daimlerchrysler-corp-v-cuno/#352" aria-description="Citation for case: DaimlerChrysler Corp. v. Cuno">547 U.S. 332, 352</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2006) (“[A] plaintiff must demonstrate 
4   standing for each claim he seeks to press.”).  Accordingly, the Court dismisses Plaintiffs’ claims 
5   for injunctive relief.                                                
6             2.   Standing to Allege a Nationwide Class                  
7        Plaintiffs are California, Tennessee, and Minnesota citizens alleging violations of 
8   California’s Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (the “Song-Beverly Act”) and UCL on behalf 
9   of a California subclass.  Plaintiffs’ also assert negligence/failure to warn, breach of the implied 
10   covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and unjust enrichment claims on behalf of the nationwide 
11   class.2  Plaintiffs do not specify which state law or laws they intend to assert in their common law 
12   causes of action.  Western Digital therefore asks the Court to dismiss all putative claims on behalf 
13   of a nationwide class because Plaintiffs do not have standing to assert claims under the laws of 
14   states in which they do not live, i.e., they can only assert claims under California, Tennessee, and 
15   Minnesota law.  Mot. at 7.                                            
16        A district court may address Article III standing to bring nationwide-class allegations at the 
17   pleading stage.  See Drake v. Toyota Motor Corp., No. 2:20-CV-01421-SB-PLA, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2020 WL 
18</span><pre class="inline">   7040125, at *3 (C.D. Cal. Nov. 23, 2020) (collecting cases).  The issue may be properly addressed 
19   prior to class certification “when a plaintiff’s lack of standing is ‘plain enough from the 
20   pleadings,’ it can form appropriate grounds for dismissal even if it overlaps with issues regarding 
21   whether the named plaintiffs are adequate representatives under Rule 23.”  Carpenter v. PetSmart, 
22   Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">441 F. Supp. 3d 1028</span><pre class="inline">, 1039 (S.D. Cal. 2020) (quoting Broomfield v. Craft Brew All., Inc., 
23   No. 17-cv-01027-BLF, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 3838453</span><pre class="inline">, at *14 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 1, 2017)).  However, 
24   California district courts typically decline to rule on nationwide class claims arising under 
25                                                                         

26                                                                         
    2 Plaintiffs initially asserted a cause of action for violation of the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, 
27   </pre><span class="citation no-link">15 U.S.C. § 2301</span><pre class="inline">, et seq., but have since removed this claim from the FAC. 
1   California consumer protection statutes at the pleading stage “based on the need to conduct a case-
2   specific choice of law analysis.”  Czuchaj v. Conair Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 U.S. Dist. LEXIS 54410</span><pre class="inline">, *24 
3   (S.D. Cal. April 16, 2014); see also Won Kyung Hwang v. Ohso Clean, Inc., 2013 U.S. Dist. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">4   LEXIS 54002</span><pre class="inline">, *59 (N.D. Cal. April 16, 2013) (choice of law questions are premature at the 
5   pleading stage).                                                      
6        The Court declines to engage in a choice of law analysis at the pleading stage, particularly 
7   where it lacks sufficient briefing to engage in detailed analysis.  See Czuchaj, 2014 U.S. Dist. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">8   LEXIS 54410</span><pre class="inline">, *25; see also Cooper v. Tokyo Elec. Power Co. Holdings, </pre><span class="citation no-link">960 F.3d 549</span><pre class="inline">, 559 (9th 
9   Cir. 2020).  This inquiry is most appropriately addressed at class certification. 
10        B.   Sufficiency of the Allegations                              
11             1.   Song-Beverly Act Claim                                 
12        Western Digital alleges that Plaintiffs fail to state a claim under the Song-Beverly Act.  
13   Mot. at 13.  “Under the Song-Beverly Act, every retail sale of ‘consumer goods’ in California 
14   includes an implied warranty by the manufacturer and the retail seller that the goods are 
15   ‘merchantable.’”  Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2267213"><a href="/opinion/2267213/mexia-v-rinker-boat-co-inc/#1303" aria-description="Citation for case: Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc.">174 Cal. App. 4th 1297, 1303</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting 
16   Cal. Civ. Code, §§ 1791.3, 1792).  Merchantability refers to whether the goods:  
17             (1) Pass without objection in the trade under the contract description.  
              (2) Are fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used.  
18             (3) Are adequately contained, packaged, and labeled.        
              (4) Conform to the promises or affirmations of fact made on the 
19             container or label.                                         
    </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1791.1</span><pre class="inline">.                                              
20                                                                         
         First, Western Digital contends that this claim is deficient because Plaintiffs fail to allege 
21                                                                         
    whether they purchased the Data Storage Devices in California.  Mot. at 9–10.  The Court agrees.  
22                                                                         
    None of the representative plaintiffs are alleged to have purchased their devices in California.  
23                                                                         
    Indeed, the implied warranty of merchantability attaches to “every sale of consumer goods that are 
24                                                                         
    sold at retail in this state.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1792</span><pre class="inline">; see In re Nexus 6P Prod. Liab. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8832806"><a href="/opinion/8847506/backstay-machine-leather-co-v-zenite-metal-co/" aria-description="Citation for case: Backstay Machine &amp; Leather Co. v. Zenite Metal Co.">293 F. 
25</a></span><pre class="inline">                                                                         
    Supp. 3d 888, 948 (N.D. Cal. 2018) (dismissing Song-Beverly Act claim for failure to allege 
26                                                                         
    whether the phones were purchased in California).  Plaintiffs contend that, in drawing all 
27                                                                         
1   reasonable inferences in their favor, the Court should infer that Plaintiffs Bobo and Sorial 
2   purchased their devices in California simply because the FAC alleges they are California residents.  
3   Opp’n at 10.  However, it is well established that courts “may not supply essential elements of the 
4   claim that were not initially pled.”  Chapman v. Pier 1 Imps. (U.S.), Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9438879"><a href="/opinion/182444/chapman-v-pier-1-imports-us-inc/#954" aria-description="Citation for case: Chapman v. Pier 1 Imports (U.S.) Inc.">631 F.3d 939, 954</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th 
5   Cir. 2011) (quoting Pena v. Gardner, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9483477"><a href="/opinion/591450/joseph-l-pena-v-booth-gardner-lawrence-kincheloe-warden-john-lambert-amos/#471" aria-description="Citation for case: Joseph L. Pena v. Booth Gardner Lawrence Kincheloe Warden...">976 F.2d 469, 471</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1992)). 
6        Second, Western Digital alleges the Song-Beverly Act claim is barred by the statute of 
7   limitations.  Mot. at 10.  The Song-Beverly Act does not have its own statute of limitations.  
8   Mexia, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2267213"><a href="/opinion/2267213/mexia-v-rinker-boat-co-inc/#1305" aria-description="Citation for case: Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc.">174 Cal. App. 4th at 1305</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Instead, it is governed by Section 2725 of the Uniform 
9   Commercial Code.  MacDonald v. Ford Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7225924"><a href="/opinion/7308021/macdonald-v-ford-motor-co/#1100" aria-description="Citation for case: MacDonald v. Ford Motor Co.">37 F. Supp. 3d 1087, 1100</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2014).  
10   Section 2725 provides that “[a]n action for breach of any contract for sale must be commenced 
11   within four years after the cause of action has accrued.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Com. Code § 2725</span><pre class="inline">(1).  “A cause of 
12   action accrues when the breach occurs, regardless of the aggrieved party’s lack of knowledge of 
13   the breach.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Com. Code § 2725</span><pre class="inline">(2).  Here, Plaintiffs do not allege when they purchased their 
14   Data Storage Devices.  Western Digital challenges whether Plaintiffs’ claims fall outside the four-
15   year limitations period since the allegations indicate that Western Digital ended support for the 
16   devices at issue in 2015.  Mot. at 10–11.                             
17        Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Western Digital’s motion to dismiss the Song-Beverly 
18   Act claim with leave to amend to assert where and when the devices were purchased. 
19             2.   Failure to Warn Claim                                  
20        Plaintiffs’ second cause of action asserts a claim for negligent failure to warn.  
21   “Negligence law in a failure-to-warn case requires a plaintiff to prove that a manufacturer or 
22   distributer did not warn of a particular risk for reasons which fell below the acceptable standard of 
23   care, i.e., what a reasonably prudent manufacturer would have known and warned about.”  Rosa v. 
24   City of Seaside, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2508334"><a href="/opinion/2508334/rosa-v-city-of-seaside/#1014" aria-description="Citation for case: Rosa v. CITY OF SEASIDE">675 F. Supp. 2d 1006, 1014</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2009), aff’d sub nom. Rosa v. Taser Int’l, 
25   Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="803990"><a href="/opinion/803990/evelyn-rosa-v-taser-international-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Evelyn Rosa v. Taser International, Inc.">684 F.3d 941</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012) (emphasis in original) (quotations and citations omitted).  At the 
26   pleading stage, “a plaintiff must allege, at a minimum, that a defect in the product caused the 
27   plaintiff’s injury and that ‘the defect in the product was due to the negligence of the defendant.’”  
1   Mountain Club Owner’s Ass’n v. Graybar Elec. Co., No. CIV. 2:13-1835 WBS K, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 
2</span><pre class="inline">   130767, at *2 (E.D. Cal. Jan. 14, 2014) (quoting Merrill v. Navegar, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9782589"><a href="/opinion/2525263/merrill-v-navegar-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Merrill v. Navegar, Inc.">26 Cal.4th 465</a></span><pre class="inline">, 479 
3   (2001)).  “A bare allegation that defendants were negligent in their design is an insufficient legal 
4   conclusion.” Fontalvo v. Sikorsky Aircraft Corp., CIV. NO. 13–331 GPC KSC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 WL 
5</span><pre class="inline">   4401437, at *5 (S.D. Cal. Aug.15, 2013).                              
6        Here, Plaintiffs allege that Western Digital breached its general duty of care to Plaintiffs in 
7   failing to adequately warn them of the risks of the Data Storage Devices, “including the legitimate 
8   threat of cyber-attack, and in failing to comply with applicable industry standards, and state and 
9   federal laws, rules, regulations and standards.”  FAC ¶¶ 130–31.  Western Digital asserts that 
10   Plaintiffs failure to warn claim should be dismissed because Plaintiffs fail to plausibly allege that 
11   Western Digital knew or should have known of any danger of cyber-attack or that the alleged 
12   failure to warn caused Plaintiffs’ harm.  Mot. at 11–12.              
13        The FAC purportedly identifies one defect in the Data Storage Devices—a “flaw” in 
14   Western Digital’s My Cloud device system.  FAC ¶ 87.  In support, Plaintiffs cite one report 
15   finding “a pair of hacking researchers searching for exploits in various technology systems” 
16   identified a “vulnerability” in Western Digitals’ My Cloud OS 3 system published in February 
17   2021.  FAC ¶ 87.  However, Plaintiffs fail to explain in sufficient detail exactly what this “flaw” is 
18   or how it falls below the applicable standard of care.  Indeed, “[a] claim of negligent failure to 
19   warn necessarily implicates a defective product or a product rendered defective.”  Sapiro v. 
20   Encompass Ins., No. C 03-4587 MHP, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2004 WL 2496090</span><pre class="inline">, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Nov. 2, 2004).  
21        The remainder of the allegations conclusively state that Western digital knew or should 
22   have known that its Data Storage Devices were vulnerable to attacks by hackers.  For example, 
23   Plaintiffs allege that Western Digital “allowed inherent flaws in the WD My Book Live and My 
24   Book Duo to remain within those systems and left Representative Plaintiffs and Class Members 
25   unprotected against a cyber-attack,” FAC ¶ 88, however, Plaintiffs fail to identify any specific 
26   flaw in Western Digital’s Data Storage Device.  Plaintiffs also allege that the company “has a 
27   history of allowing inherent flaws in the Covered Products that were dangerous to customers’ 
1   information,” but fails to allege any specific instances showing that Western Digital “failed both to 
2   take action to protect customers’ data and to adequately address and warn consumers of 
3   Defendant’s resultant security concerns.”  Id. ¶ 89.  Without providing any support for this 
4   allegation, the Court cannot assume Digital Western has a “history” of flaws in its devices.   
5        In sum, the FAC is devoid of allegations specifying what the vulnerability is in the Data 
6   Storage Device and how it enabled a cyber-attack.  Without these allegations the Court cannot 
7   properly assess whether Western Digital failed to warn Plaintiffs.  Thus, Plaintiffs have failed to 
8   state a cognizable claim for negligent failure to warn.  The Court GRANTS Western Digital’s 
9   motion to dismiss with respect to this claim with leave to amend.     
10             3.   Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing Claim  
11        Plaintiffs’ third cause of action alleges that Digital Western breached the implied covenant 
12   of good faith and fair dealing.                                       
13        “Every contract imposes upon each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its 
14   performance and its enforcement.”  Carma Developers, Inc. v. Marathon Dev. Cal., Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2 Cal. 
15</span><pre class="inline">   4th 342, 371 (1992) (quotations omitted).  Under California law, to establish a breach of the 
16   covenant of good faith and fair dealing a plaintiff must plead that: “(1) the parties entered into a 
17   contract; (2) the plaintiff fulfilled his obligations under the contract; (3) any conditions precedent 
18   to the defendant’s performance occurred; (4) the defendant unfairly interfered with the plaintiff’s 
19   rights to receive the benefits of the contract; and (5) the plaintiff was harmed by the defendant’s 
20   conduct.”  Rosenfeld v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2470639"><a href="/opinion/2470639/rosenfeld-v-jpmorgan-chase-bank-na/#968" aria-description="Citation for case: Rosenfeld v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.">732 F. Supp. 2d 952, 968</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2010).  
21   &quot;In addition, the implied covenant “cannot impose substantive duties or limits on the contracting 
22   parties beyond those incorporated in the specific terms of their agreement.”  Agosta v. Astor, 120 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">23   Cal.App.4th 596</span><pre class="inline">, 607, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2257651"><a href="/opinion/2257651/agosta-v-astor/" aria-description="Citation for case: Agosta v. Astor">15 Cal.Rptr.3d 565</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2004) (internal citation omitted). 
24        Western Digital contends that Plaintiffs have failed to identify any express contractual term 
25   from which this duty arises.  Mot. at 13.  While a “breach of a specific provision of the contract is 
26   not a necessary prerequisite to establishing a breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair 
27   dealing,” a plaintiff must allege an express term “on which to hinge an implied duty.”  In re 
1   Facebook PPC Advert. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2540201"><a href="/opinion/2540201/in-re-facebook-ppc-advertising-litigation/#769" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Facebook PPC Advertising Litigation">709 F. Supp. 2d 762, 769</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2010) (quotations and citations 
2   omitted).  Plaintiffs maintain there is a contract because “Plaintiffs purchased and owned Western 
3   hard drives.”  Opp’n at 13.  Plaintiffs allege that they purchased the Data Storage Devices “in 
4   reliance on Defendant’s representation that they were secure and that Defendant was committed to 
5   safely preserving their data.”  FAC ¶ 8.  Digital Western allegedly breached the implied covenant 
6   of good faith and fair dealing “by failing to inform Representative Plaintiffs and Class Members of 
7   the defects and/or vulnerabilities inherent in the Covered Products and failing to properly repair 
8   and/or provide a patch for those defects and/or vulnerabilities.”  FAC ¶ 137.   
9        However, Plaintiffs do not specify whether the Data Storage Devices were purchased from 
10   Western Digital such that the Court can infer a valid contract exists between the parties.  Guz v. 
11   Bechtel Nat’l, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9785497"><a href="/opinion/2569285/guz-v-bechtel-national-inc/#375" aria-description="Citation for case: Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc.">24 Cal.4th 317, 375</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9785497"><a href="/opinion/2569285/guz-v-bechtel-national-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc.">100 Cal.Rptr.2d 352</a></span><pre class="inline">, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9785497"><a href="/opinion/2569285/guz-v-bechtel-national-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc.">8 P.3d 1089</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2000) (holding that 
12   breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing claim is predicated on the existence 
13   of a contract).  As pled, the FAC lacks sufficient allegations to establish the existence of a contract 
14   from which an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing may arise. 
15        Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair 
16   dealing is dismissed with leave to amend.                             
17             4.   UCL Claim                                              
18        Next, Western Digital argues that Plaintiffs’ fourth cause of action arising under 
19   California’s Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”) fails as a matter of law.  Mot. at 15.   
20        The UCL prohibits “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act.”  Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. 
21   Code § 17200.  Courts interpret the UCL as establishing three distinct “prongs” of liability for 
22   business practices that are (1) unlawful, (2) unfair, or (3) fraudulent.  Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., 
23   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1089" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1089</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017).  Plaintiffs assert causes of action under all three 
24   prongs.  Plaintiffs bring the UCL claim on behalf of the putative California subclass based on the 
25   following alleged unlawful business practices: Western Digital’s “sub-standard design” of the 
26   Data Storage Devices, failure to warn of the risks of continued use of the device, failure to provide 
27   a “patch” for defects, failure to recall the products, “and/or Defendant’s deceptive 
1   misrepresentations, concealments and suppressions of material fact.”  FAC ¶¶ 140, 144.   
2        As a threshold matter, Western Digital challenges whether Plaintiffs can show they have an 
3   adequate remedy at law.  Mot. at 15.  Plaintiffs request an injunction, restitution damages, and/or 
4   other equitable relief including disgorgement or ill-gotten gains, refunds, interest, and reasonable 
5   attorneys’ fees.  FAC ¶ 150.  Western Digital contends that Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp. 
6   forecloses Plaintiffs’ UCL claim.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">971 F.3d 834</span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2020).      
7        In Sonner, the district court dismissed a plaintiff’s UCL claim because she had “failed to 
8   establish that she lacked an adequate legal remedy” given that she had previously sought damages 
9   under another statute for the same conduct and the UCL is subject to California&#x27;s inadequate-
10   remedy-at-law doctrine.  Id. at 838.  The Ninth Circuit affirmed the district court, noting that a 
11   plaintiff “must establish that she lacks an adequate remedy at law before securing equitable 
12   restitution for past harm under the UCL and CLRA.”  Id. at 844.  Here, the fact that the FAC fails 
13   to allege that Plaintiffs lack an adequate remedy of law is fatal to Plaintiffs’ claims for equitable 
14   restitution under the UCL.  Id. (citing O’Shea v. Littleton, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9425502"><a href="/opinion/108906/oshea-v-littleton/#502" aria-description="Citation for case: O&#x27;Shea v. Littleton">414 U.S. 488, 502</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1974)).  Although 
15   Plaintiffs suggest in opposition that their requested remedies are inadequate or incomplete 
16   compensation for Plaintiffs’ harm, the FAC is devoid of any such allegations.  See In re California 
17   Gasoline Spot Mkt. Antitrust Litig., No. 20-CV-03131-JSC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 1176645</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (N.D. Cal. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">18   Mar. 29</span><pre class="inline">, 2021) (collecting cases).                                    
19        Plaintiffs UCL is therefore dismissed with leave to amend to the extent Plaintiffs can 
20   allege that they lack an adequate remedy of law.                      
21             5.   Unjust Enrichment Claim                                
22        Finally, Western Digital asserts that Plaintiffs’ fifth claim for unjust enrichment fails 
23   because it is not a standalone cause of action.  Mot. at 23.          
24        Although there is no standalone cause of action for unjust enrichment under California 
25   law, “[w]hen a plaintiff alleges unjust enrichment, a court may ‘construe the cause of action as a 
26   quasi-contract claim seeking restitution.’”  Astiana v. Hain Celestial Grp., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2792929"><a href="/opinion/2792929/skye-astiana-v-the-hain-celestial-group/" aria-description="Citation for case: Skye Astiana v. the Hain Celestial Group">783 F.3d 753</a></span><pre class="inline">, 762 
27   (9th Cir. 2015) (quoting Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2650741"><a href="/opinion/2650741/rutherford-holdings-llc-v-plaza-del-rey/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey">223 Cal. App. 4th 221</a></span><pre class="inline">, 231 
1   (2014)).  Here, Plaintiffs allege that Western Digital obtained a benefit by failing to disclose the 
2   fact that the Data Storage Device is defective before Plaintiffs purchased and used the product.  
3   FAC ¶¶ 152, 155.  Western Digital was “unjustly enriched at the expense of Representative 
4   plaintiffs and Class Members.”  Id. ¶ 156.  Plaintiffs seek an order from the Court “requiring 
5   Defendant to refund, disgorge, and pay as restitution any profits, benefits, and other compensation 
6   obtained by Defendant.”  Id. ¶ 158.  The Court construes these allegations as pleading a claim for 
7   quasi-contract seeking restitution and, therefore, Plaintiffs’ unjust enrichment claim is not subject 
8   to dismissal on the basis that it is improperly pled as a standalone claim.  See Rabin v. Google 
9   LLC, No. 22-CV-04547-BLF, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 4053804</span><pre class="inline">, at *12 (N.D. Cal. June 15, 2023). 
10        Western Digital also contends that, should the Court construe Plaintiffs’ claim as a quasi-
11   contract cause of action, Plaintiffs’ claim should still be dismissed because Plaintiffs allege the 
12   existence of a valid contract between the parties “covering the same subject matter” but have 
13   failed to allege facts showing that the contract is unenforceable.  Mot. at 23 (quoting Saroya v. 
14   Univ. of the Pac., No. 5:20-cv-03196-EJD, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 2400986</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. June 11, 2021)).  
15   Plaintiffs assert that they may plead quasi-contract damages in the alternative.  Opp’n at 17.  The 
16   Court agrees.  At the pleading stage plaintiffs may allege inconsistent remedies of recovery in the 
17   alternative.  Aberin v. Am. Honda Motor Co., Inc., No. 16-CV-04384-JST, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WL 1473085</span><pre class="inline">, at 
18   *9 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 26, 2018).  It is well established that “claims for restitution or unjust 
19   enrichment may survive the pleading stage when pled as an alternative avenue of relief, though the 
20   claims, as alternatives, may not afford relief if other claims do.”  Colucci v. ZonePerfect Nutrition 
21   Co., No. 12-2907-SC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 6737800</span><pre class="inline">, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 28, 2012) (collecting cases). 
22        Accordingly, the Court declines to dismiss Plaintiffs’ alternative claim for restitution based 
23   on quasi-contract.
