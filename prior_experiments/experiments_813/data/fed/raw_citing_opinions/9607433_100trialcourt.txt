13        Plaintiffs Gary Freund and Wayne McMath bring this class action lawsuit against 
14   Defendant HP, Inc.  Plaintiffs assert common law claims and claims under California and 
15   Minnesota consumer protection laws based on allegations that HP’s all-in-one printers will not 
16   perform certain functions that do not require ink when the devices have low or empty ink 
17   cartridges.  Plaintiffs seek to represent a nationwide class and California and Minnesota subclasses 
18   of consumers who purchased HP all-in-one printers.                    
19        Now before the Court is HP’s motion to dismiss Plaintiffs Complaint.  Mot., ECF No. 14.  
20   Plaintiffs oppose.  Opp’n, ECF No. 23.  HP has replied in support.  Reply, ECF No. 28.  The Court 
21   held a hearing on the motion on January 5, 2023.  ECF No. 36.  For the reasons discussed on the 
22   record and explained below, HP’s motion to dismiss is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND. 
23    I.  BACKGROUND                                                       
24        As alleged in the complaint, Defendant HP designs, manufactures, and sells all-in-one 
25   printers.  Compl. ¶¶ 23-25.  HP’s all-in-one printers are marketed and sold as having three core 
26   functions: printing, copying, and scanning.  Id. ¶ 25.  Some of these all-in-one printers also have a 
27   faxing feature.  Id.  HP advertises that its all-in-one printers are convenient because users do not 
1        HP all-in-one printers are manufactured, packaged, marketed, and sold to consumers in a 
2   manner that requires the device to contain ink to scan or fax documents.  Id. ¶ 42.  But HP does 
3   not disclose to consumers that the devices will not scan or fax documents if the ink cartridges are 
4   depleted.  Id. ¶ 39.                                                  
5        Plaintiffs Gary Freund and Wayne McMath purchased an HP Envy 6455e All-in-One 
6   Wireless Color Printer and HP Deskjet 2655 All-in-One Compact Printer, respectively.  Id. ¶¶ 65, 
7   74.  HP represented that the devices would be able to scan and did not include qualifying language 
8   that such features would not work if the ink cartridge levels were low or depleted.  Id. at ¶¶ 67-69, 
9   76-78.  The devices do not function as a scanner if the ink cartridges are low or empty.  Id. at ¶¶ 
10   70, 79.  Freund and McMath would not have purchased their devices or would not have paid as 
11   much for them had they known that they would have to maintain ink the devices to scan 
12   documents.  Id. ¶ 72, 81.  Freund and McMath may purchase the HP all-in-one printers again in 
13   the future should HP continue to advertise the devices as “all-in-one,” as they would think it meant 
14   HP manufactured the devices to make them scan even when the ink is low or depleted.  Id. ¶¶ 73, 
15   82.                                                                   
16        Plaintiffs filed this lawsuit on June 27, 2022.  See Compl.  The Complaint asserts claims 
17   for (1) negligent misrepresentation; (2) unjust enrichment; (3) breach of express warranty under 
18   </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Com. Code § 2313</span><pre class="inline">; (4) violation of California’s Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”), Cal. Bus. 
19   &amp; Prof. Code § 17200, et seq.; (5) violation of California’s False Advertising Law (“FAL”), Cal. 
20   Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17500, et seq.; (6) breach of express warranty under </pre><span class="citation no-link">Minn. Stat. § 336.2-313</span><pre class="inline">; 
21   (7) violation of Minnesota’s Deceptive Trade Practices Act (“MDTPA”); (8) violations of 
22   Minnesota’s False Advertising Law (“MSFAA”).                          
23    II.  FAILURE TO STATE A CLAIM – RULE 12(B)(6)                        
24        “A motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a 
25   claim upon which relief can be granted ‘tests the legal sufficiency of a claim.’”  Conservation 
26   Force v. Salazar, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="221641"><a href="/opinion/221641/conservation-force-v-salazar/#1241" aria-description="Citation for case: Conservation Force v. Salazar">646 F.3d 1240, 1241-42</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (quoting Navarro v. Block, </pre><span class="citation no-link">250 F.3d 
27</span><pre class="inline">   729, 732 (9th Cir. 2001)).  When determining whether a claim has been stated, the Court accepts 
1   plaintiff.  Reese v. BP Expl. (Alaska) Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="219976"><a href="/opinion/219976/reese-v-bp-exploration-alaska-inc/#690" aria-description="Citation for case: Reese v. BP Exploration (Alaska) Inc.">643 F.3d 681, 690</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011).  But the Court need 
2   not “accept as true allegations that contradict matters properly subject to judicial notice” or 
3   “allegations that are merely conclusory, unwarranted deductions of fact, or unreasonable 
4   inferences.”  In re Gilead Scis. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1400355"><a href="/opinion/1400355/in-re-gilead-sciences-securities-litigation/#1055" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Gilead Sciences Securities Litigation">536 F.3d 1049, 1055</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008) (internal quotation 
5   marks and citations omitted).  While a complaint need not contain detailed factual allegations, it 
6   “must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible 
7   on its face.’”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 
8   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007)).  A claim is facially plausible when it “allows the court to draw the 
9   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  On a motion to 
10   dismiss, the Court&#x27;s review is limited to the face of the complaint and matters judicially noticeable.  
11   MGIC Indem. Corp. v. Weisman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="478039"><a href="/opinion/478039/mgic-indemnity-corporation-v-lawrence-i-weisman-thomas-p-dunn-ln/#504" aria-description="Citation for case: Mgic Indemnity Corporation v. Lawrence I. Weisman, Thomas...">803 F.2d 500, 504</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1986); N. Star Int’l v. Ariz. Corp. 
12   Comm’n, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="426434"><a href="/opinion/426434/north-star-international-a-nevada-corporation-plaintiff-appellant-v-the/#581" aria-description="Citation for case: NORTH STAR INTERNATIONAL, a Nevada Corporation,...">720 F.2d 578, 581</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1983).                            
13        A.  Fraud-Based Claims (Counts 1, 4, 5, 7, and 8)                
14        Plaintiffs assert five claims that involve fraud or fraudulent representations: negligent 
15   misrepresentation and violations of the UCL; FAL; MDTPA; and MFSAA.  Compl. ¶¶95-199.  
16   HP asserts, and Plaintiffs do not dispute, that these claims are subject to Rule 9(b)’s heightened 
17   pleading requirements.  See Mot. 7-8; Opp’n 4.                        
18        Plaintiffs’ fraud-based claims may be premised on either omissions or affirmative 
19   misrepresentations.  See, e.g., Ocampo v. Apple Inc., No. 5:20-CV-05857-EJD, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 767614</span><pre class="inline">, 
20   at *3-4 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 14, 2022).  It is unclear from the complaint whether Plaintiffs seek to 
21   allege an omission-based or affirmative-misrepresentation-based theory of fraud.  Plaintiffs argue 
22   in their briefing that they seek to proceed on both theories.  Opp’n 5-8.  Plaintiffs’ allegations are 
23   deficient under either theory as set forth below.                     
24            1.  Affirmative Misrepresentation                            
25        Plaintiffs do not plausibly allege that HP made an affirmative misrepresentation.  Plaintiffs 
26   allege that HP represents that its all-in-one printers “can print, copy, scan, and in some cases, fax 
27   documents” but that these devices “are . . . sold to consumers in a manner which requires the 
1   There is no affirmative misrepresentation here.  Plaintiffs do not allege that HP represents that its 
2   devices can print, copy, scan, or fax documents without ink.  Rather, the allegations appear to be 
3   directed to the theory that HP omitted a fact that it was obliged to disclose.  See, e.g., Compl. ¶ 46 
4   (“HP fails to disclose to consumers that its All-in-One Printers suffer from the Design Flaw.”).  
5   Plaintiffs have therefore failed to plead facts of an affirmative misrepresentation to support its 
6   fraud-based claims.                                                   
7            2.  Omission                                                 
8        For an omission to be actionable “[it] must be contrary to a representation actually made 
9   by the defendant, or an omission of a fact the defendant was obliged to disclose.”  Hodsdon v. 
10   Mars, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4280855"><a href="/opinion/4503602/robert-hodsdon-v-mars-inc/#861" aria-description="Citation for case: Robert Hodsdon v. Mars, Inc.">891 F.3d 857, 861</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018) (quoting Daugherty v. Am. Honda Motor Co., 144 
</pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/Cal.%20App.%204th/11/824/">11   Cal. App. 4th 824</a></span><pre class="inline">, 835 (2006)).  Plaintiffs have not alleged either theory of omission. 
12        Plaintiffs have not alleged that HP omitted a fact contrary to its representations.  As noted 
13   above, accepting Plaintiff’s allegations as true, HP represents that its all-in-one printers are 
14   devices that can print, copy, scan, and fax documents.  Plaintiffs allege that HP has omitted the 
15   fact that its all-in-one printers do not scan or fax documents if the all-in-one printer has an ink 
16   cartridge that is low on or out of ink.  This omitted fact is not contrary to Plaintiffs’ representation.  
17   Plaintiffs have therefore not alleged that HP omitted a fact contrary to its representations. 
18        The Court next considers whether Plaintiffs have adequately pled that HP omitted facts 
19   that it had a duty to disclose.  “[T]here are four circumstances in which an obligation to disclose 
20   may arise: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the 
21   defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the 
22   defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes 
23   partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.”  Smith v. Ford Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">749 F. 
24</span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 2d 980, 987 (N.D. Cal. 2010) (citing LiMandri v. Judkins, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2267506"><a href="/opinion/2267506/limandri-v-judkins/#337" aria-description="Citation for case: LiMandri v. Judkins">52 Cal.App.4th 326, 337</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1997)), 
25   aff’d, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="619286"><a href="/opinion/619286/richard-smith-v-ford-motor-company/" aria-description="Citation for case: Richard Smith v. Ford Motor Company">462 F. App’x 660</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011).  “[A] fact is deemed ‘material,’ and obligates an 
26   exclusively knowledgeable defendant to disclose it, if a ‘reasonable [consumer]’ would deem it 
27   important in determining how to act in the transaction at issue.”  Collins v. eMachines, Inc., 202 
1   manufacturer’s duty to consumers is limited to . . . [an] affirmative misrepresentation or a safety 
2   issue.’”  Elias v. Hewlett-Packard Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8709146"><a href="/opinion/8725949/elias-v-hewlett-packard-co/#1135" aria-description="Citation for case: Elias v. Hewlett-Packard Co.">950 F. Supp. 2d 1123, 1135</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2013) (quoting 
3   Smith v. Ford Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2473126"><a href="/opinion/2473126/smith-v-ford-motor-co/#987" aria-description="Citation for case: Smith v. Ford Motor Co.">749 F.Supp.2d 980, 987</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D.Cal.2010)).      
4        The Court finds that Plaintiffs have not pled facts showing that HP omitted facts it had a 
5   duty to disclose.  Plaintiffs allege that HP “conceals a material fact and makes partial 
6   representations but also suppresses some material fact” and “intentionally concealed the Design 
7   Flaw,” Compl. ¶¶ 137, 181, 182.  Plaintiffs further allege that HP has known for years that its 
8   representations about its all-in-one printers were misleading and that HP sells its all-in-one devices 
9   as loss leaders to capture the ink cartridge replacement business.  Id. ¶ 47-48.  These allegations 
10   are insufficient because they do not plead any facts to suggest that HP knew or should have 
11   reasonably known about the alleged defect and concealed it at the time of sale.  See Davis v. HSBC 
12   Bank Nevada, N.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="807798"><a href="/opinion/807798/gary-davis-v-hsbc-bank-nevada-na/#1161" aria-description="Citation for case: Gary Davis v. Hsbc Bank Nevada, N.A.">691 F.3d 1152, 1161</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012) (California FAL applies to statements 
13   that defendant knew or should have known to be untrue or misleading); B.L.M. v. Sabo &amp; Deitsch, 
14   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9751474"><a href="/opinion/2301965/blm-v-sabo-deitsch/#834" aria-description="Citation for case: B.L.M. v. Sabo &amp; Deitsch">55 Cal. App. 4th 823, 834</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1997) (California negligent misrepresentation claim requires allegation 
15   that defendant lacked reasonable grounds to believe representation was true); Hardin Cnty. Sav. 
16   Bank v. Hous. &amp; Redevelopment Auth. of City of Brainerd, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8246515"><a href="/opinion/8280793/hardin-county-savings-bank-v-housing-redevelopment-authority-of-the-city/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hardin County Savings Bank v. Housing &amp; Redevelopment...">821 N.W.2d 184</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Minn. 2012) 
17   (element of Minnesota negligent misrepresentation claim is “failure to use reasonable care or 
18   competence in obtaining the [false] information”); Hammerschmidt v. Gen. Motors LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">583 F. 
19</span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d 1215, 1222 (D. Minn. 2022) (presale knowledge of alleged defect required to state a 
20   claim for fraudulent omission under MFSAA and MDTPA).                 
21        The Court also finds that Plaintiffs’ allegations are deficient because they do not identify 
22   whether the alleged defects are occurring within the warranty period or otherwise implicate a 
23   safety concern.  With respect to defects that manifested only after the warranty period, Plaintiffs 
24   must allege that the defect poses an unreasonable safety hazard.  See, e.g., Baranco v. Ford Motor 
25   Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7246757"><a href="/opinion/7328841/baranco-v-ford-motor-co/#960" aria-description="Citation for case: Baranco v. Ford Motor Co.">294 F. Supp. 3d 950, 960</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2018) (“The standard for materiality of the defect 
26   depends on whether it arises in-warranty or post-warranty.”).         
27        Plaintiffs have therefore failed to plead an omission to support their fraud-based claims. 
             3.  Identification of Alleged Defect

    particularity.  Mot. 9.  Plaintiffs respond that their allegations are sufficient because they “do not

19                                                                         
         B.  Negligent Misrepresentation Claim (Count 1)                  
20                                                                         
         HP argues that Plaintiffs’ negligent misrepresentation claim should also be dismissed 
21                                                                         
    because it is barred by the economic loss rule.  Mot. 16-17.  Plaintiffs respond that the economic 
22                                                                         
    loss doctrine does not bar their negligent misrepresentation claim because their claim sounds in 
23                                                                         
    fraud.  Opp’n 13-14.  HP replies that Plaintiffs’ negligent representation claim is barred here 
24                                                                         
    because it is premised on the same allegations as Plaintiffs’ breach of warranty claims.  Reply 11. 
25                                                                         
         “Where a purchaser’s expectations in a sale are frustrated because the product he bought is 
26                                                                         
    not working properly, his remedy is said to be in contract alone, for he has suffered only economic 
27                                                                         
    losses.”  Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9795512"><a href="/opinion/2623151/robinson-helicopter-co-inc-v-dana-corp/#988" aria-description="Citation for case: Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp.">34 Cal. 4th 979, 988</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2004) (modifications 
1   and internal quotations omitted).  Thus, “[a] person may not ordinarily recover in tort for the 
2   breach of duties that merely restate contractual obligations.”  Aas v. Superior Court, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2590151"><a href="/opinion/2590151/in-re-marriage-of-bonds/#627" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Marriage of Bonds">24 Cal. 4th 
3   627, 643</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2000), superseded by statute on other grounds.              
4        Another court in the Ninth Circuit has recently noted that neither the Ninth Circuit, in any 
5   published opinion, nor the California Supreme Court has analyzed how and if the economic loss 
6   doctrine applies to claims for negligent misrepresentation.  Dawood v. Gamer Advantage LLC, 
7   2:22-cv-00567 WBS KJN, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 3108846</span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. Aug. 4, 2022).  A court in this district 
8   has noted that the Ninth Circuit has reached “seemingly opposite conclusions” in unpublished 
9   opinions.  See Crystal Springs Upland Sch. v. Fieldturf USA, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7240722"><a href="/opinion/7322811/crystal-springs-upland-school-v-fieldturf-usa-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Crystal Springs Upland School v. Fieldturf USA, Inc.">219 F. Supp. 3d 962</a></span><pre class="inline">, 969 n.3 
10   (N.D. Cal. 2016).  Another court in this district noted that there are also numerous opinions from 
11   district courts within this circuit and that those opinions “vary in their reasoning and in their 
12   holdings.”  In re Big Heart Pet Brands Litig., No. 18-CV-00861-JSW, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2019 WL 8266869</span><pre class="inline">, at *22 
13   (N.D. Cal. Oct. 4, 2019).                                             
14        In In re Big Heart Pet Brands Litigation, the court adopted an approach that “evaluate[s] 
15   whether the allegations that support a negligent misrepresentation claim closely parallel a 
16   concurrent breach of contract claim so that the negligent misrepresentation claim is in actuality a 
17   breach of contract claim in disguise.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. at *23</span><pre class="inline"> (citation and internal quotations omitted).  The 
18   court granted a motion to dismiss a negligent misrepresentation claim because the claim relied on 
19   allegations that were “substantially similar” to the plaintiff’s breach of warranty claims.  Id.; see 
20   also Dawood, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 3108846</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (“It is possible that [the] cases may be reconcilable in that 
21   some negligent misrepresentation claims may sound more in fraud and others may sound more in 
22   negligence.”).                                                        
23        This Court adopts the approach of In re Big Heart Pet Brands Litigation.  Plaintiffs’ 
24   negligent misrepresentation claim and breach of warranty claims rely on substantially similar 
25   allegations: Plaintiffs allege that they are entitled to damages because HP advertised its devices as 
26   all-in-one printers and did not disclose that those devices would not scan or fax when they are low 
27   or out of ink.  Compl. ¶¶ 97-102, 125-133, 171-179.  The Court therefore finds that Plaintiffs’ 
1   claim for negligent misrepresentation (Count 1) is therefore DISMISSED WITH LEAVE TO 
2   AMEND on this independent basis.                                      
3        C.  Breach of Express Warranty (Counts 3 and 6)                  
4        HP argues that Plaintiffs breach-of-express-warranty claims should be dismissed because 
5   such claims apply to manufacturing defects, not design defects, and Plaintiffs have alleged only a 
6   design defect.  Mot. 17-19.  Plaintiffs respond that they need discovery to determine whether the 
7   defect is a design or manufacturing defect.  Opp’n 15-16.             
8        Claims for breach of express warranty generally cover manufacturing defects, not design 
9   defects.  See Bruce Martin Const., Inc. v. CTB, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2641077"><a href="/opinion/2641077/bruce-martin-construction-inc-v-ctb-inc/#752" aria-description="Citation for case: Bruce Martin Construction, Inc. v. CTB, Inc.">735 F.3d 750, 752-53</a></span><pre class="inline"> (8th Cir. 2013); Tharpe 
10   v. Hyundai Motor Am., Inc., No. SA CV 21-01428-DOC-JDE, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 3137453</span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. July 
11   12, 2022).  Plaintiffs argue that their breach-of-warranty claims should survive under this general 
12   rule because they do not yet know whether the alleged defect is a design defect or a manufacturing 
13   defect.  Opp’n 15-16.  But Plaintiffs named their alleged defect a “Design Flaw” and included no 
14   allegations to suggest that the alleged defect is a manufacturing defect.  See, e.g., Compl. ¶ 6.  
15   Plaintiffs have therefore failed to plead their claims for breach of express warranty. 
16        Furthermore, Plaintiffs failed to adequately identify the terms of the alleged warranty.  “In 
17   order to plead a cause of action for breach of express warranty, one must allege the exact terms of 
18   the warranty, plaintiff&#x27;s reasonable reliance thereon, and a breach of that warranty which 
19   proximately causes plaintiff injury.”  Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2139353"><a href="/opinion/2139353/williams-v-beechnut-nutrition-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp.">185 Cal. App. 3d 135</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
20   142 (1986).  To allege the exact terms of the warranty, the plaintiff must “‘identify a specific and 
21   unequivocal written statement’ about the product that constitutes an ‘explicit guarantee[ ].’”  
22   Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7245130"><a href="/opinion/7327214/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1092" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">273 F. Supp. 3d 1052, 1092</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017) (quoting Arroyo v. TP-
23   Link USA Corp., No. 5:14-CV-04999-EJD, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2015 WL 5698752</span><pre class="inline">, at *10 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 29, 2015)).  
24   Plaintiffs have failed to identify any such specific and unequivocal written statement.  Their 
25   general allegations that “HP expressly warranted both on product packaging and advertisements 
26   that the All-in-One Printers are multifunction devices with scanning and fax features” are too 
27   generic to plausibly allege a breach of an express warranty.  Instead, Plaintiffs must identify the 
1   Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1106" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1106</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017).                     
2        The Court finds that Plaintiffs’ breach-of-express-warranty claims are inadequately pled 
3   because Plaintiffs do not allege any facts to support that the alleged defect is a manufacturing 
4   defect and they do not identify the specific and unequivocal written statements comprising the 
5   alleged warranties.  Plaintiffs’ breach-of-warranty claims (Counts 3 and 6) are therefore 
6   DISMISSED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.                                        
7        If Plaintiffs seek to amend their allegations such that they include warranties that cover 
8   manufacturing defects, Plaintiffs should include factual allegations supporting any claims of 
9   manufacturing defects.                                                
10        D.  Claims Seeking Equitable Relief                              
11        HP argues that the court must dismiss Plaintiffs’ claims for equitable relief under Sonner v. 
12   Premier Nutrition Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">971 F.3d 834</span><pre class="inline"> (2020), because Plaintiffs have not alleged that they lack 
13   an adequate remedy at law.  Mot. 19-20.  Plaintiffs respond that their claims for equitable 
14   restitution should not be dismissed because they are allowed to plead their claims for equitable 
15   relief in the alternative to their claims for legal relief.  Opp’n 17.  Plaintiffs further argue that their 
16   claims for injunctive relief should not be dismissed because they have alleged threat of future 
17   harm.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. 17-18</span><pre class="inline">.  The Court first addresses Plaintiffs’ claims for equitable restitution and then 
18   addresses their claims for injunctive relief.                         
19            1.   Claims Seeking Equitable Restitution (Counts 2, 4, 5, 7, 8) 
20        Plaintiffs seek equitable restitution under the UCL, FAL, MDTPA, MFSAA and under a 
21   claim for unjust enrichment.  The Court agrees HP that these claims, as currently alleged, must be

2   law if she seeks equitable relief” (emphasis in original)).  Here, Plaintiffs must plead facts as to

4   remedies at law.  Plaintiffs claims for equitable restitution for unjust enrichment and violations of 
5   the UCL, FAL, MDTPA, MFSAA are therefore DISMISSED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND. 
6            2.  Claims Seeking Injunctive Relief (Counts 4, 5, 7, 8)     
7        Plaintiffs seek injunctive relief under the UCL, FAL, MDTPA, and MFSAA.  The Court 
8   finds that Plaintiffs have adequately plead their requests for injunctive relief.  Plaintiffs allege that 
9   they may purchase HP all-in-one printers again in the future.  See Compl. ¶¶ 72-73, 81-82.  This is 
10   sufficient to confer standing for injunctive relief, see Davidson v. Kimberly-Clark Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">889 F.3d 
11</span><pre class="inline">   956, 970 (9th Cir. 2018), and because the injunctive relief that Plaintiffs request is prospective and 
12   their remedy at law, damages, is retrospective, their claims for an injunctive relief are not barred

15   inadequate remedy at law with regard to their requests for injunctive relief. 
16        E.  Unjust Enrichment (Count 2)

21   Court may construe the claim as a quasi-contract claim seeking restitution that is plead in the 
22   alternative to their breach of contract claim.  Opp’n 19.

26</span><pre class="inline">   13039744 (N.D. Cal. June 19, 2019), Plaintiffs have failed to include the necessary alternative 
27   allegations.  Here, Plaintiffs have alleged that HP has issued a valid warranty.  To state a claim for

3        F.  Standing to Assert Claims Relating to Non-Purchased Products 
4        HP asserts that Plaintiffs lack standing to assert claims for products they did not purchase.  
5   Mot. 21-22.  Plaintiffs respond that they have standing to assert claims for products they did not 
6   purchase because they have alleged that “HP has made identical misrepresentations regarding all 
7   of the Products.”  Opp’n 19-20.                                       
8        “[A] plaintiff may have standing to assert claims for unnamed class members based on 
9   products he or she did not purchase so long as the products and alleged misrepresentations are 
10   substantially similar.”  Brown v. Hain Celestial Grp., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8704727"><a href="/opinion/8721534/brown-v-hain-celestial-group-inc/#890" aria-description="Citation for case: Brown v. Hain Celestial Group, Inc.">913 F. Supp. 2d 881, 890</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 
11   2012); see also Gisairo v. Lenovo (United States) Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">516 F. Supp. 3d 880</span><pre class="inline">, 887 (D. Minn. 2021).  
12   Here, Plaintiffs’ complaint contains no factual allegations that the non-purchased products are 
13   substantially similar to the purchased products.  At the hearing, Plaintiffs suggested that alleged 
14   defect is caused by software in the products.  But the Complaint contains no factual allegations 
15   about this software or that it is the same in the non-purchased products. 
16        The Court also notes that Plaintiffs have not alleged that either named Plaintiff purchased a 
17   device with fax capabilities, much less experienced the alleged defect when trying to use their 
18   device.  To the extent Plaintiffs seek to pursue their claims on behalf of purchasers of all-in-one 
19   printers with fax capabilities, Plaintiffs’ complaint will need to include factual allegations to 
20   suggest that the fax functionality suffers from a substantially similar defect. 
21        Plaintiffs’ claims are therefore DISMISSED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND to the extent 
22   they assert claims for non-purchased products.
