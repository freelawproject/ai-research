16        Plaintiffs GMC Semitech Co., Ltd. and Suzhou Cycas Microelectronics Co., Ltd. 
17   bring various contract and tort-based claims against Defendant Capital Asset Exchange 
18   and Trading, LLC based on three purchase agreements for equipment Plaintiffs allege they 
19   paid for but never received.  Defendant moves to dismiss five of Plaintiffs’ eight claims, 
20   arguing the non-contractual claims fail because Plaintiffs allege valid and enforceable 
21   contracts and fail to allege fraudulent, unlawful, or unfair conduct with particularity.  
22   Plaintiffs, in turn, argue they permissibly allege tort claims in the alternative to their 
23   contract claims and sufficiently allege unfair business practices and bad faith behavior by 
24   Defendant.  For the reasons below, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to dismiss the 
25   complaint.                                                            
26   I.   BACKGROUND                                                       
27        The Court takes the following facts as true for the purposes of the motion.  Plaintiffs 
1   refurbishment services for used legacy semiconductor equipment to domestic customers in 
2   China.”  ECF 1 (Compl.) ¶¶ 3–4.  Defendant CAET “is a global physical commodity trader 
3   addressing manufacturing needs in the semiconductor market.”  Compl. ¶ 5.   
4        In February and March 2024, the parties executed three agreements for the sale of 
5   equipment from Defendant to Plaintiffs.  Compl. ¶¶ 10, 12.  The agreements consisted of

10   Defendant notified Plaintiffs that it could not ship the purchased equipment to Plaintiffs in 
11   China.  Compl. ¶ 17.  Plaintiffs have never received the equipment they purchased through 
12   the agreements or refunds of their payments.  Compl. ¶ 18.            
13        Plaintiffs filed suit against Defendant.  Compl.  Defendant moved to dismiss claims 
14   from Plaintiffs’ complaint.  ECF 9 (Mot.).  Plaintiffs opposed, ECF 14 (Opp’n), and 
15   Defendant replied, ECF 15 (Reply).  All parties have consented to magistrate judge 
16   jurisdiction under </pre><span class="citation no-link">28 U.S.C. § 636</span><pre class="inline">(c).  ECF 8, 10.                    
17   II.  LEGAL STANDARD                                                   
18        A motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6) tests the legal 
19   sufficiency of a complaint.  Navarro v. Block, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6998032"><a href="/opinion/7092494/navarro-v-block/#732" aria-description="Citation for case: Navarro v. Block">250 F.3d 729, 732</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001).  “To 
20   survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as 
21   true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible on its face.’”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435369"><a href="/opinion/145901/bartlett-v-strickland/#662" aria-description="Citation for case: Bartlett v. Strickland">556 U.S. 
22   662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007)).  When 
23   reviewing a 12(b)(6) motion, a court “must accept as true all factual allegations in the 
24   complaint and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party.”  Retail 
25   Prop. Trust v. United Bd. of Carpenters &amp; Joiners of Am., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2736107"><a href="/opinion/2736107/retail-property-trust-v-united-brotherhood-of-carpenters-joiners-of/#945" aria-description="Citation for case: Retail Property Trust v. United Brotherhood of Carpenters...">768 F.3d 938, 945</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
26   2014).  A court, however, need not accept as true “allegations that are merely conclusory, 
27   unwarranted deductions of fact, or unreasonable inferences.”  In re Gilead Scis. Secs. 
1   the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct 
2   alleged.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2736107"><a href="/opinion/2736107/retail-property-trust-v-united-brotherhood-of-carpenters-joiners-of/" aria-description="Citation for case: Retail Property Trust v. United Brotherhood of Carpenters...">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  If a court grants a motion to dismiss, leave to amend should be granted 
3   unless the pleading could not possibly be cured by the allegation of other facts.  Lopez v. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">4   Smith, 203</span><pre class="inline"> F.3d 1122, 1127 (9th Cir. 2000).                           
5   III.  DISCUSSION                                                      
6        Defendant moves to dismiss the following claims from Plaintiffs’ complaint: money 
7   had and received (Third Claim); breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair 
8   dealing (Fourth Claim); unjust enrichment (Fifth Claim); conversion (Sixth Claim); and 
9   unfair business practices under California’s Unfair Competition Law (UCL) (Seventh 
10   Claim).                                                               
11        For the reasons below, the Court DISMISSES each of the challenged claims.  
12   Where Plaintiffs’ claims may be cured by the allegation of other facts, dismissal is with 
13   leave to amend.  See Lopez, 203 F.3d at 1227.                         
14      A.  Money Had and Received (Third Claim)                           
15        The parties agree that a claim for money had and received under California law is a 
16   quasi-contract claim and an alternative to claims premised on a valid contract.  Mot. 4; 
17   Opp’n 5–6; Reply 6.  “In an action on an express contract, a claim for money had and 
18   received is permitted where there has been a total failure of consideration.”  Rutherford 
19   Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2650741"><a href="/opinion/2650741/rutherford-holdings-llc-v-plaza-del-rey/#230" aria-description="Citation for case: Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey">223 Cal. App. 4th 221, 230</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. Ct. App. 2014).  But a 
20   claim for money had and received, as a quasi-contract claim, generally “does not lie when 
21   an enforceable, binding agreement exists defining the rights of the parties.”  Paracor Fin., 
22   Inc. v. Gen. Elec. Cap. Corp., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/96/1151/">96 F.3d 1151</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1167 (9th Cir. 1996); Block Sci., Inc. v. True 
23   Diagnostics, Inc., No. 21-cv-01118-RBM-JLB, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 27348</span><pre class="inline">, at *5 (S.D. Cal. Jan. 3, 
24   2023).                                                                
25        Defendant concedes in its reply brief that “CAET does not dispute that the parties 
26   entered into written contracts that were supported by consideration.”  Reply 6.  As a result, 
27   Plaintiffs’ claim for money had and received fails to state a claim because there is no 
1   Cal. App. 4th at 230; Paracor, 96 F.3d at 1167.  The Court need not reach the parties’ 
2   remaining arguments, and Plaintiffs’ claim for money had and received is DISMISSED 
3   without leave to amend.                                               
4      B.  Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing (Fourth 
           Claim)                                                         
5                                                                         
6        Defendant argues that Plaintiffs fail to state a claim for breach of the implied 
7   covenant of good faith and fair dealing because the claim both “merely restate[s] 
8   Plaintiffs’ breach of contract claims” and is premised on obligations that are not found in 
9   the agreements.  Mot. 5–6.                                            
10        Plaintiffs allege Defendant breached the covenant of good faith by failing to 
11   “acquire or make reasonable efforts to acquire and deliver the purchased equipment,” 
12   “resolve any export ‘compliance’ concerns,” and “act in good faith to fulfill its obligations 
13   under the Agreements.”  Compl. ¶ 36.  The Court finds that Plaintiffs fail to state a claim 
14   for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing on these bases. 
15        “Under California law, every contract ‘imposes upon each party a duty of good faith 
16   and fair dealing in its performance and its enforcement.’”  Rosenfeld v. JPMorgan</pre><span class="citation no-link"> Chase 
17</span><pre class="inline">   Bank, N.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2470639"><a href="/opinion/2470639/rosenfeld-v-jpmorgan-chase-bank-na/#968" aria-description="Citation for case: Rosenfeld v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.">732 F. Supp. 2d 952, 968</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2010) (citation omitted).  The covenant of 
18   good faith and fair dealing is violated by “a conscious and deliberate act, which unfairly 
19   frustrates the agreed common purposes and disappoints the reasonable expectations of the 
20   other party thereby depriving that party of the benefits of the agreement.”  Careau &amp; Co. v. 
21   Sec. Pac. Bus. Credit, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2159546"><a href="/opinion/2159546/careau-co-v-security-pacific-business-credit-inc/#1395" aria-description="Citation for case: Careau &amp; Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc.">222 Cal. App. 3d 1371, 1395</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Cal. Ct. App. 1990); see also </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2159546"><a href="/opinion/2159546/careau-co-v-security-pacific-business-credit-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Careau &amp; Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc.">id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> 
22   at 1393 (“Or, to put it another way, the ‘implied covenant imposes upon each party the 
23   obligation to do everything that the contract presupposes they will do to accomplish its 
24   purpose.’”).                                                          
25        Although “breach of a specific provision of the contract is not a necessary 
26   prerequisite” to state a claim, “the scope of conduct prohibited by the covenant of good 
27   faith is circumscribed by the purposes and express terms of the contract.”  Carma Devs. 
1   implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing ‘cannot impose substantive duties or limits 
2   on the contracting parties beyond those incorporated in the specific terms of their 
3   agreement.’”  Rosenfeld, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2470639"><a href="/opinion/2470639/rosenfeld-v-jpmorgan-chase-bank-na/#968" aria-description="Citation for case: Rosenfeld v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.">732 F. Supp. 2d at 968</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citation omitted).  At the same time, “if 
4   the allegations do not go beyond the statement of a mere contract breach and, relying on 
5   the same alleged acts, simply seek the same damages or other relief already claimed in a 
6   companion contract [cause] of action, they may be disregarded as superfluous as no 
7   additional claim is actually stated.”  Celador Int’l Ltd. v. Walt Disney Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2421945"><a href="/opinion/2421945/rambarrat-ex-rel-rambarrat-v-united-states/#846" aria-description="Citation for case: Rambarrat Ex Rel. Rambarrat v. United States">347 F. Supp. 2d 
8   846, 852</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 2004) (citation omitted).  One exception to this limitation is “where 
9   the plaintiff alleges that the defendant acted in bad faith to frustrate the contract’s 
10   benefits.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2421945"><a href="/opinion/2421945/rambarrat-ex-rel-rambarrat-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rambarrat Ex Rel. Rambarrat v. United States">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">                                                       
11        Plaintiffs fail to state a claim for breach of the covenant of good faith based on their 
12   allegations that Defendant failed to acquire and deliver equipment and acted in bad faith.  
13   First, Plaintiffs do not point to any provisions in the agreements requiring Defendant to 
14   “acquire or make reasonable efforts to acquire and deliver the purchased equipment,” and 
15   the Court identifies none.  In fact, the agreements appear to provide for the possibility that 
16   Defendant might not acquire the equipment.  See, e.g., Compl., Ex. A at 13 ¶ 5 (“All 
17   Equipment is offered to Buyer based on its prior sale to CAET.  Should such prior sale not 
18   occur, this invoice shall be null and void.”).  Without a contractual provision requiring 
19   Defendant to acquire the equipment, “the implied covenant has nothing upon which to act 
20   as a supplement.”  See Rosenfeld, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2470639"><a href="/opinion/2470639/rosenfeld-v-jpmorgan-chase-bank-na/#968" aria-description="Citation for case: Rosenfeld v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.">732 F. Supp. 2d at 968</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citation omitted).  Second, 
21   Plaintiffs’ allegation that Defendant violated the covenant of good faith by failing to make 
22   reasonable efforts to deliver the equipment relies on “the same course of conduct that 
23   underlies” Plaintiffs’ breach of contract claims.  See Bard v. GSV Asset Mgmt., No. 23-cv-
24   00488-WHO, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 8852756</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 21, 2023).  Specifically, Plaintiffs 
25   allege Defendant breached the agreements by failing to deliver the equipment and failing 
26   to refund Plaintiffs’ payments for the equipment.  Compl. ¶¶ 22, 27.  Plaintiffs’ claims for 
27   breach of contract and for breach of the covenant of good faith for failure to deliver 
1   No. 11-cv-03638-SC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 1657121</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (N.D. Cal. May 10, 2012).  Third, 
2   Plaintiffs’ allegation that Defendant failed “to act in good faith to fulfill its obligations 
3   under the Agreements” does not identify any acts by Defendant and is too generalized to 
4   plausibly state a claim.  See Compl. ¶ 36; Careau, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2159546"><a href="/opinion/2159546/careau-co-v-security-pacific-business-credit-inc/#1395" aria-description="Citation for case: Careau &amp; Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc.">222 Cal. App. 3d at 1395</a></span><pre class="inline">. 
5        Plaintiffs also fail to state a claim for breach of the covenant of good faith based on 
6   the allegation that Defendant failed to resolve export compliance concerns.  Defendant 
7   argues that it had no obligation to resolve export compliance concerns under the 
8   agreements such that Plaintiffs cannot base their claim on its alleged failure to do so.  Mot. 
9   5–6.  But Plaintiffs allege the purchase orders in each agreement specify that “Seller 
10   [CAET] is responsible to provide . . . Export Control/Permit documentations in time.”  
11   Compl. ¶ 5; see, e.g., Compl., Ex. A at 17 ¶ 5.  The purchase orders also include a 
12   provision stating, “CCIC Support in warehouse included,” which Plaintiffs argue indicates 
13   Defendant was to assist with ensuring compliance.  See, e.g., Compl., Ex. A at 17 ¶ 16; 
14   Opp’n 8.  At this stage, the Court finds these allegations sufficient to plausibly allege 
15   Defendant undertook at least some obligation to assist with facilitating export compliance 
16   under the agreements.  Nonetheless, Plaintiffs do not allege any actions by Defendant that 
17   violated this obligation—only that Plaintiffs provided the necessary information to assist 
18   Defendant with ensuring export compliance before Defendant stopped communication and 
19   refused to ship the purchased equipment.  See Compl. ¶ 17.  Plaintiffs instead “dispute that 
20   any export restrictions constrained delivery of the purchased items.”  Compl. ¶ 18. 
21        The Court therefore DISMISSES the claim for breach of the implied covenant of 
22   good faith and fair dealing with leave to amend.                      
23      C.  Unjust Enrichment (Fifth Claim)                                
24        Defendant moves to dismiss Plaintiffs’ claim for unjust enrichment, arguing that 
25   unjust enrichment does not constitute a standalone cause of action under California law 
26   and that the claim necessarily fails where a valid, enforceable agreement exists between 
27   the parties.  Mot. 6.  Plaintiffs respond that unjust enrichment is a proper standalone cause 
1        “While California case law appears unsettled on the availability of” unjust 
2   enrichment as a standalone cause of action, the Ninth “Circuit has construed the common 
3   law to allow an unjust enrichment cause of action through quasi-contract.”  ESG Cap. 
4   Partners, LP v. Stratos, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4014582"><a href="/opinion/4237321/esg-capital-partners-v-venable-llp/#1038" aria-description="Citation for case: Esg Capital Partners v. Venable LLP">828 F.3d 1023, 1038</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2016); see Bruton v. Gerber Prods. 
5   Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8683670"><a href="/opinion/8700517/bruton-v-gerber-products-co/#470" aria-description="Citation for case: Bruton v. Gerber Products Co.">703 F. App’x 468, 470</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2017); Astiana v. Hain Celestial Grp., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2788119"><a href="/opinion/2788119/united-states-v-habibi/#753" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Habibi">783 F.3d 
6   753, 762</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2015).  Following circuit precedent, the Court concludes Plaintiffs may 
7   allege a claim for unjust enrichment “as an independent cause of action or as a quasi-
8   contract claim for restitution.”  See Stratos, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4014582"><a href="/opinion/4237321/esg-capital-partners-v-venable-llp/#1038" aria-description="Citation for case: Esg Capital Partners v. Venable LLP">828 F.3d at 1038</a></span><pre class="inline">.      
9        However, “unjust enrichment is an action in quasi-contract, which does not lie when 
10   an enforceable, binding agreement exists defining the rights of the parties.”  Paracor, 96 
11   F.3d at 1167.  Because, as discussed above, Defendant appears to concede the validity of 
12   the agreements, Plaintiffs’ claim for unjust enrichment fails.  See supra III(A); Reply 6.  
13   The unjust enrichment claim is DISMISSED without leave to amend.      
14      D.  Conversion (Sixth Claim)                                       
15        Defendant argues Plaintiffs’ conversion claim fails because it is based on the same 
16   allegations as the breach of contract claims and is barred by the economic loss rule.  The 
17   Court agrees the claim is precluded by the economic loss rule and therefore does not 
18   address Defendant’s first argument.                                   
19        California’s economic loss rule functions to maintain separation between contract 
20   and tort law by generally limiting a party “to recover in contract for purely economic loss 
21   due to disappointed expectations, unless he can demonstrate harm above and beyond a 
22   broken contractual promise” from tortious conduct.  Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana 
23   Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9795512"><a href="/opinion/2623151/robinson-helicopter-co-inc-v-dana-corp/" aria-description="Citation for case: Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp.">34 Cal. 4th 979</a></span><pre class="inline">, 988–89 (Cal. 2004).  Most recently, in Rattagan v. Uber Techs., 
24   Inc., the California Supreme Court held that the economic loss rule bars recovery in tort 
25   for breach of a contract duty “unless two conditions are satisfied.  A plaintiff must first 
26   demonstrate the defendant’s injury-causing conduct violated a duty that is independent of 
27   the duties and rights assumed by the parties when they entered the contract.  Second, the 
1   reasonably contemplated by the parties when the contract was formed.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">17 Cal. 5th 1</span><pre class="inline">, 20–
2   21 (Cal. 2024).                                                       
3        For courts in the Ninth Circuit, whether the economic loss rule bars a conversion 
4   claim depends on “whether the ownership interest that formed the basis for the conversion 
5   claim preexisted the contract or arises from the contract.”  Markowitz v. JPMorgan</pre><span class="citation no-link"> Chase 
6</span><pre class="inline">   Bank, N.A., No. 23-cv-06528-ODW (MRWx), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2024 WL 5203062</span><pre class="inline">, at *5 (C.D. Cal. Dec. 
7   23, 2024) (citation omitted).  If the property right on which a plaintiff bases a claim for 
8   conversion was created by the contract, rather than independent of the contract, the 
9   economic loss rule bars the claim.  Id.; Textainer Equip. Mgmt. (U.S.) Ltd. v. TRS Inc., No. 
10   07-cv-01519-WHA, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2007 WL 1795695</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. June 20, 2007); Tsai v. Wang, 
11   No. 17-cv-00614-DMR, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 2587929</span><pre class="inline">, at *8–9 (N.D. Cal. June 14, 2017); Zeichner 
12   v. Nord Sec. Inc., No. 24-cv-02462-JSC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2024 WL 4951261</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 2, 
13   2024).                                                                
14        Here, Plaintiffs allege that after they “paid for the Equipment in full, they were 
15   entitled to take possession of the Equipment following prompt and timely delivery, or to a 
16   refund of the payments.”  Compl. ¶ 44.  Plaintiffs’ alleged property rights in the equipment 
17   therefore arose out of their agreements with Defendant because Plaintiffs allege that they 
18   were entitled to possession as a result of their payments, which were made under and 
19   governed by the agreements.  See Compl. ¶ 44; see, e.g., Ex. A at 13 ¶ 8 (“Title and risk of 
20   loss . . . pass to Buyer upon CAET’s receipt of the Purchase Price.”).  Plaintiffs’ alleged 
21   property right to their payments, in the form of refunds, also arose out of the agreements 
22   because Plaintiffs allege that they made the payments pursuant to the agreements, Compl. 
23   ¶¶ 14, 21, 26, and are entitled to refunds based on the terms of the agreements, Compl. ¶¶ 
24   13, 18, 22, 27.  Because Plaintiffs’ alleged ownership interests were created by the 
25   agreements, the conversion claim is barred by the economic loss rule.  See Zeichner, 2024 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">26   WL 4951261</span><pre class="inline">, at *8; Tsai, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2017 WL 2587929</span><pre class="inline">, at *9.                      
27        Plaintiffs’ conversion claim is therefore DISMISSED with leave to amend. 
1      E.  Unfair Business Practices (Seventh Claim)                      
2        California’s UCL prohibits “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or 
3   practice.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200</span><pre class="inline">.  A “plaintiff may proceed under the UCL” on 
4   any of these “three possible theories.”  Garcia v. Sony Comput. Ent. Am., LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">859 F. 
5</span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 2d 1056, 1061 (N.D. Cal. 2012).                                 
6        Plaintiffs allege Defendant engaged in the following unfair business practices: “(1) 
7   unjustifiably refusing to refund payments for undelivered products; (2) offering equipment 
8   for sale, and accepting and retaining payments for such equipment, despite claiming that 
9   such equipment cannot be shipped in compliance with export laws; (3) refusing to provide 
10   refunds despite its own failure or refusal to deliver equipment for which it demanded 
11   prepayment; and (4) falsely representing that it would assist with export compliance.”  
12   Compl. ¶ 50.                                                          
13        Plaintiffs appear to concede that they fail to state a claim under the unlawful or 
14   fraudulent prongs of the UCL, as they do not respond to Defendant’s arguments asserting 
15   as much.  See Mot. 8–10; Opp’n 11–12; Shagoofa v. Eshaqzi, No. 22-cv-01824-FWS-JDE, 
16   </pre><span class="citation no-link">2024 WL 1600657</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (C.D. Cal. Feb. 26, 2024) (“‘A failure to oppose [moving 
17   arguments in] a motion, as to a claim or an issue’ may be deemed ‘a waiver, abandonment, 
18   or concession of that claim or issue.’”).  The Court agrees with Defendant that Plaintiffs 
19   also fail to state a claim under the unfair prong of the UCL.         
20        The test for unfair conduct under the UCL varies depending on whether a plaintiff 
21   alleges the unfair conduct is among business competitors or injures consumers.  See 
22   Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1104" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1104</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017); Boris v. Wal-
23   Mart Stores, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7225783"><a href="/opinion/7307880/boris-v-wal-mart-stores-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Boris v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.">35 F. Supp. 3d 1163</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1170–71 (C.D. Cal. 2014).  Plaintiffs’ opposition 
24   brief suggests the Court should apply the test for injuries to consumers, Opp’n 11, though 
25   Defendant argues that the consumer test is inappropriate given the nature of the 
26   transactions alleged, Reply 14.  The Court need not decide as much at this juncture 
27   because “courts in this district have held that where the unfair business practices alleged 
    1  || the fraudulent and unlawful prongs of the UCL, the unfair prong of the UCL cannot 
    2  ||  survive if the claims under the other two prongs of the UCL do not survive.”  Hadley, 243 
    3  || F. Supp. 3d at 1104-05.  Here, Plaintiffs allege the same conduct by Defendant constituted 
    4  ||  “unfair, unlawful, and fraudulent business acts and practices” and seemingly concede that 
    5  || they fail to state a claim under the unlawful or fraudulent prongs of the UCL.  Compl. □□□□ 
    6  || 50-51; see Opp’n 11-12.  As such, Plaintiffs’ claim under the unfair prong of the UCL 
    7  || likewise fails.  See Eidmann v. Walgreen Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">522 F. Supp. 3d 634</span><pre class="inline">, 647 (N.D. Cal. 2021). 
    8          Plaintiffs’ claim for unfair business practices under the UCL is DISMISSED with 
    9  || leave to amend.
