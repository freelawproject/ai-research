The Benchmark

The 260410 benchmark (April 10th 2026 version) is our expert-labeled evaluation set: 383 citing clusters across 39 courts, yielding ~9,740 labeled (citing, cited) records. It will be the first open source citator ground truth at this scale. This page covers what it contains, how it’s curated, how to interpret the metrics it produces, where it’s lacking, and what we’re doing about it.

How it’s curated

1

Sample citing opinions

Citing clusters are drawn from CourtListener and weighted toward federal appellate courts where the bulk of consequential treatment language lives. State and federal district opinions are included to test cross-court generalization.

2

Multi-expert labeling

Independent of any other citator. Treatments come solely from each expert’s own reading of the opinion and the shared definitions — not from lookups in any other commercial citator.

Initial pass. At least 2 experts independently assign one of the 43 treatment labels for each (citing, cited) record, working from shared instructions and definitions.

Tiebreak. A 3rd expert resolves 2-vs-1 disagreements.

Adjudication. If disagreement still persists, a model prediction is brought in: if it matches one expert and the reasoning holds up under review, that label is taken. Otherwise a final expert sees all candidate labels (blind to their sources) and supplies a binding treatment with a supporting quote and rationale.

3

Derived severity + direction

Both severity (Stop/Warning/Caution/Neutral) and direction (Direct History / Citing Reference / Related Reference) are deterministically looked up from the treatment label. They are not separately annotated — which makes predictions and labels apples-to-apples after the same post-processing.

In progress — targeted curation for non-neutral treatments

  • Before: experts were presented with an opinion and its entire set of cited authorities and asked to label every one, regardless of likely treatment.
  • Now: a model first surfaces the cited cases likely to carry a non-neutral treatment, and experts label only those candidates — concentrating annotation effort on the rare Stop / Warning / Caution treatments so that we have more labeled examples to evaluate them honestly.

How to read the metrics

Under 94% class skew, most headline numbers look great while telling us nothing about how the pipeline does on the rare classes that matter. Below is what we report, what we look at diagnostically, and what we deliberately avoid.

Metrics we report

The three numbers we lead with. Read together — each one alone is incomplete.

Macro F1

0.47

What it measures. Scores 0–1 averaged equally across all 43 treatments. Since “Cited by” is just 1 of 43, predicting it everywhere would score near zero — the model has to actually identify the rare treatments to pull this number up.

Reading 0.47. Moderate. The pipeline handles common treatments well but struggles on the rare ones, dragging the average down. A perfect 1.0 means every treatment classified correctly; random noise would score <0.1.

Quadratic Weighted Kappa (QWK)

0.79

What it measures. Scores 0–1 by how close mistakes stay on the severity scale. A near-miss (predicting “Distinguished” when the answer was “Limited”) barely hurts the score; a big jump (predicting “Overruled” when the answer was “Cited”) is heavily penalized.

Reading 0.79. Strong. Most mistakes are close on severity (mainly Cited↔Distinguished confusion) rather than wild Stop↔Neutral inversions. Above 0.8 would be the “near-perfect” band; below 0.5 would suggest errors are essentially random.

Cohen’s κ

0.71

What it measures. Scores 0–1 by how much better than random guessing the model does. 0 = no better than chance, 1 = perfect.

Reading 0.71. Substantial agreement on the standard interpretive scale (<0.2 slight, 0.2–0.4 fair, 0.4–0.6 moderate, 0.6–0.8 substantial, >0.8 near-perfect). Still skew-sensitive, so read alongside macro F1.

Diagnostic metrics

Useful for drilling into where errors live, but each has caveats — don’t treat them as headline numbers.

Per-severity F1

0.61

What it measures. Macro F1 collapsed from 43 treatments to the 4 severity tiers (Stop / Warning / Caution / Neutral) — coarser but more stable when individual treatments are scarce.

Reading 0.61. The pipeline separates Stop/Warning/Caution/Neutral moderately — the most consequential distinction for end users (“is this case still good law?”). Higher than per-treatment macro F1 because many treatment-level mistakes still land in the right severity tier — those near-misses count as correct at this coarser level.

Per-class F1

19 / 43

What it measures. F1 calculated separately for each of the 43 treatments — the per-class quality view.

Reading 19 / 43. Only 19 treatments have enough labeled examples to score reliably. The other 24 are scarce and their per-class F1 swings wildly run-to-run — the per-class view is partial until those classes get more support.

Rank-distance distribution

96.0%

What it measures. When predictions are wrong, this distribution shows how far off they are on the severity scale — exact match, within-tier, or far-tier inversion.

Reading 96.0%. The headline is the exact-match share. For the wrong 4.0%, only ~0.7% are far-tier inversions (e.g. Stop predicted as Neutral) — most stay close on severity, which is what we want.

Metrics we don’t use

All inflated by the 94% “Cited by” majority. They tell you the model can predict the dominant class — nothing more.

Accuracy

0.96

The share of predictions that exactly match the truth. Under heavy skew, predicting the majority class on every row already scores near the ceiling.

Weighted F1

0.96

F1 of each class weighted by how often that class appears. Effectively accuracy in disguise — dominated by the “Cited by” majority.

Micro F1

0.96

F1 aggregated globally across all predictions. On single-label multi-class tasks like ours, this is mathematically equal to accuracy.

Where the benchmark is lacking

Minority treatments at near-zero support

Of the 36 non-neutral treatments (Stop, Warning, Caution combined), 22 are tagged scarce — including 12 with no labeled examples at all. Per-class F1 on these is noisy at best and undefined at worst, and these are precisely the treatments lawyers care most about.

Long-tail court coverage

The top six courts hold the overwhelming majority of clusters; ~30 state and district courts contribute only 1–3 clusters each — court-conditional metrics are noisy on the long tail, and the benchmark under-represents the kinds of citation language used in state appellate and trial-court opinions.

Treatment ambiguity at the margins

Treatments are interpretive, not deterministic — even expert reviewers disagree on edge cases (did the court “Limit” the cited holding, or merely “Distinguish” it?). The blinded adjudication workflow catches and resolves these, but it doesn’t eliminate the underlying ambiguity — some predictions the model gets “wrong” may reflect a different reasonable reading rather than a clear error.

Federal-heavy sampling

Federal appellate dominates by design (where most consequential treatment language lives), but the consequence is that we can’t cleanly separate model failures from corpus breadth: when the pipeline does worse on state-court text, it’s unclear whether the model genuinely struggles with that text or whether the benchmark simply has too few state-court examples to evaluate it honestly.

What’s already in motion & upcoming opportunities

Already in motion

  • Intentional oversampling of circuit-court cases. Subsequent benchmark expansion is weighted toward circuits we’re underrepresented on, to close the long-tail-court gap.
  • Quantifying label ambiguity. Measuring what share of (citing, cited) pairs trigger disagreement between two or more experts — gives us a concrete read on how much of the benchmark sits at the interpretive margins versus cleanly labeled, and establishes an honest ceiling on what per-class F1 we can actually expect.
  • Taxonomy review and possible expansion. Additional treatments outside the current 43-label taxonomy keep surfacing as the labeling effort proceeds — we’re evaluating which ones warrant adding to the canonical list versus mapping into existing labels.

Upcoming and future opportunities

  • Targeted sampling for rare classes. Stop, Warning, and Caution treatments need many more labeled examples before per-class F1 is statistically meaningful — the targeted curation pass (described above) is the primary lever here.
  • State-court breadth. Expand state appellate and federal district coverage so we can tell model failures from corpus blind spots.
  • Downstream-tolerance study. Some prediction errors matter much more to end users than others. Quantifying which confusions are tolerable in the legal-researcher UX and which actually mislead would tell us how much of the macro F1 gap is real-world quality versus metric pessimism.
