4                         II.  LEGAL STANDARD                                
5       Pursuant to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure (“Rule”) 12(b)(6), an action may be 
6  dismissed for failure to allege “enough facts to state a claim to relief that is plausible on its 
7  face.”   Bell Atl.  Corp.  v.  Twombly,  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S.  544,  570</a></span><pre class="inline">  (2007).    “A  claim  has  facial 
8  plausibility when the plaintiff pleads factual content that allows the court to draw the 
9  reasonable  inference  that  the  defendant  is  liable  for  the  misconduct  alleged.    The 
10  plausibility standard is not akin to a ‘probability requirement,’ but it asks for more than a 
11  sheer possibility that a defendant acted unlawfully.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662</a></span><pre class="inline">, 678 
12  (2009) (internal citations omitted).  For purposes of ruling on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, the 
13  Court “accept[s] factual allegations in the complaint as true and construe[s] the pleadings 
14  in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.”  Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine 
15  Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1194077"><a href="/opinion/1194077/manzarek-v-st-paul-fire-marine-insurance/#1031" aria-description="Citation for case: Manzarek v. St. Paul Fire &amp; Marine Insurance">519 F.3d 1025, 1031</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).                            
16       However, the Court is “not bound to accept as true a legal conclusion couched as a 
17  factual allegation.”  Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. at 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. 10                               III.  DISCUSSION                             
11       A.   Request for Judicial Notice                                     
12       A court generally cannot consider materials outside the pleadings on a motion to 
13  dismiss for failure to state a claim.  FED. R. CIV. P. 12(d).  A court may, however, consider 
14  materials subject to judicial notice without converting the motion to dismiss into one for 
15  summary judgment.  Barron v. Reich, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="660277"><a href="/opinion/660277/richard-l-barron-linda-m-barron-v-robert-reich-department-of-labor/#1377" aria-description="Citation for case: Richard L. Barron Linda M. Barron v. Robert Reich...">13 F.3d 1370, 1377</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1994).  Under Federal 
16  Rule of Evidence 201(b), a court may take judicial notice, either on its own accord or by a 
17  party’s request, of facts that are not subject to reasonable dispute because they are (1) 
18  “generally known within the trial court’s territorial jurisdiction; or (2) can be accurately 
19  and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned.”  
20  FED. R. EVID. 201(b).  A court may also take judicial notice of “matters of public record.”  
21  Lee v. City of Los Angeles, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6998020"><a href="/opinion/7092482/lee-v-city-of-los-angeles/#689" aria-description="Citation for case: Lee v. City of Los Angeles">250 F.3d 668, 689</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001) (internal citations omitted).  
22  Finally, under the incorporation by reference doctrine, courts may “take into account 
23  documents whose contents are alleged in a complaint and whose authenticity no party 
24  questions, but which are not physically attached to the [plaintiff’s] pleading.”  Davis v. 
25  HSBC Bank Nevada, N.A., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="807798"><a href="/opinion/807798/gary-davis-v-hsbc-bank-nevada-na/#1160" aria-description="Citation for case: Gary Davis v. Hsbc Bank Nevada, N.A.">691 F.3d 1152, 1160</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2012) (internal quotations and 
26  citations omitted).  The incorporation by reference doctrine “treats certain documents as 
27  though they are part of the complaint itself,” Khoja v. Orexigen Therapeutics, Inc., 899 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="9486999"><a href="/opinion/673517/united-states-v-omar-jahal-daggao-aka-omar-jahal-ali-daggao/#1002" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Omar Jahal Daggao, A.K.A. Omar Jahal...">28  F.3d 988, 1002</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018), so long as “the plaintiff refers extensively to the document 
1  or the document forms the basis of the plaintiff’s claim.”  United States v. Ritchie, </pre><span class="citation no-link">342 F.3d 
2</span><pre class="inline">  903, 907 (9th Cir. 2003).

4  labeling of a lotion version of Alba Botanica Hawaiian Sunscreen (Ex. 1); (2) images of

10  PharmaCare U.S., Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">567 F. Supp. 3d 1172</span><pre class="inline">, 1182 (S.D. Cal. 2021) (“courts addressing

13  judicial notice of copy of a webpage from the Hawaii State Legislature’s website titled 
14  “SB132 SD2 HD1” in Exhibit 3 as a publicly available material taken from a “source whose 
15  accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned.”  FED. R. EVID. 201(b); see U.S. ex rel. Modglin 
16  v. DJO Glob. Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7226841"><a href="/opinion/7308938/united-states-ex-rel-modglin-v-djo-global-inc/#1381" aria-description="Citation for case: United States ex rel. Modglin v. DJO Global Inc.">48 F. Supp. 3d 1362, 1381</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 2014), aff’d sub nom. United

DJO Global, Inc.">678 F. App’x 594</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2017) (“the court can take judicial

4       B.   Standing                                                        
5       In addition to Article III standing, a plaintiff must establish standing to bring UCL, 
6  CLRA, and FAL claims.  See CAL. BUS. &amp; PROF. CODE §§ 17204, 17535; CAL. CIV. CODE 
7  §  1780(a).    These  statutes  require  the  plaintiff  show  that  he  or  she  has  suffered  an 
8  “economic injury.”  See Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/#323" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">51 Cal. 4th 310, 323</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011).  The 
9  economic  injury  requirement  under  the  UCL  is  “substantially  narrower  than 
10  federal standing . . . which may be predicated on a broader range of injuries.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5452558"><a href="/opinion/5608107/kwikset-corp-v-superior-court/#324" aria-description="Citation for case: Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court">Id. at 324</a></span><pre class="inline">; 
11  see Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8704589"><a href="/opinion/8721396/miller-v-ghirardelli-chocolate-co/#868" aria-description="Citation for case: Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co.">912 F. Supp. 2d 861, 868</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2012). 
12       Defendant argues Plaintiff “lacks standing to sue as to the twelve products she did 
13  not purchase, as she cannot establish that she ‘experienced injury stemming from the 
14  purchase’ of those products.”  (Doc. 16–1 at 17 (quoting Granfield v. NVIDIA Corp., No. 
15  11-5403, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 2847575</span><pre class="inline">, at *6 (N.D. Cal. July 11, 2012).)  Defendant anticipates 
16  Plaintiff  will  argue  she  has  standing  to  assert  claims  based  on  products  that  are 
17  ‘substantially similar’ to those she purchased.  (Doc. 16–1 at 17); see Lorentzen v. Kroger 
18  Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">532 F. Supp. 3d 901</span><pre class="inline">, 908 (C.D. Cal. 2021) (“[s]ome district courts have held that a 
19  plaintiff may bring suit for any ‘substantially similar’ products not actually purchased”).  
20  However, Defendant claims Plaintiff will be unsuccessful because the absence of the 
21  phrase “Hawaiian Sunscreen” on the packaging “renders the majority of the challenged 
22  products fundamentally dissimilar from the two sunscreens Plaintiff allegedly purchased.”  
23  (Doc. 16–1 at 17.)  “[T]he use of the phrase ‘Hawaiian Sunscreen’ is significant because it 
24  clarifies that the sunscreen is free of ingredients that violate Hawaii law (i.e., oxybenzone 
25  and octinoxate) and can be used by beachgoers in Hawaii.”  (Id.)  Thus, Defendant requests 
26  the Court dismiss Plaintiff’s claims to the extent they seek to challenge products not labeled 
27  as “Hawaiian Sunscreen.”  (Id. at 18.)                                    
28       Plaintiff counters that she has standing to assert claims for each of Defendant’s 
1  Products because they are substantially similar.  (Doc. 19 at 20.)  “All of the Products are 
2  Defendant’s chemical sunscreen products that are labeled as ‘Reef Friendly,’” and “[a]ll of 
3  the Products similarly contain avobenzone, octocrylene, homosalate and/or octyl salicylate 
4  – harmful ingredients challenged in Plaintiff’s FAC.”  (Id. at 21.)  Plaintiff explains that 
5  “Defendant’s  argument  is  misguided,  as  Plaintiff  does  not  challenge  the  Products’ 
6  ‘Hawaiian Sunscreen’ claim.  Instead, Plaintiff alleges that the Products’ ‘Reef Friendly’ 
7  claim is false and misleading.”  (Id.)                                    
8       The  Court  notes  that  there  is  no  controlling  authority  on  whether  plaintiffs 
9  have standing for products they did not purchase.  See, e.g., Donohue v. Apple, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">871 F. 
10</span><pre class="inline">  Supp. 2d 913, 921–22 (N.D. Cal. 2012).  Some federal courts have held that a plaintiff 
11  lacks standing to assert such claims.  See, e.g., Granfield,  </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 2847575</span><pre class="inline">, at *6 (“when 
12  a plaintiff asserts claims based both on products that she purchased and products that she 
13  did not purchase, claims relating to products not purchased must be dismissed for lack 
14  of standing”); Mlejnecky v. Olympus Imaging America Inc., No. 2:10–CV–02630 JAM– 
15  KJN, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 19, 2011) (dismissing claims based on products not purchased 
16  for failure to allege economic injury under the UCL).  Others “hold that a plaintiff may 
17  have standing to assert claims for unnamed class members based on products he or she did 
18  not purchase so long as the products and alleged misrepresentations are  substantially 
19  similar.”  Miller, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8704589"><a href="/opinion/8721396/miller-v-ghirardelli-chocolate-co/#869" aria-description="Citation for case: Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co.">912 F. Supp. 2d at 869</a></span><pre class="inline">; see also Brazil v. Dole Food Co., Inc., No. 12-
20  CV-01831-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 WL 5312418</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 23, 2013) (quoting Lanovaz v. 
21  Twining’s N. Am., Inc., No. 12–2646, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 WL 2285221</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (N.D. Cal. May 23, 2013)) 
22  (“the substantially similar approach is also consistent with the Ninth Circuit’s warning that 
23  courts ‘should not be too rigid in applying standing requirements to proposed classes’”).  
24  Some courts have held that the standing inquiry is more appropriately resolved on a motion 
25  for class certification.  See e.g., Cardenas v. NBTY, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8699712"><a href="/opinion/8716519/cardenas-v-nbty-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cardenas v. NBTY, Inc.">870 F. Supp. 2d 984</a></span><pre class="inline">, 992–92 (E.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">26  Cal. 2012</span><pre class="inline">); Forcellati v. Hyland’s, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8700175"><a href="/opinion/8716982/essex-v-kobach/#1161" aria-description="Citation for case: Essex v. Kobach">874 F. Supp. 2d 1155, 1161</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 2012).   
27       In light of the foregoing, the Court is not inclined to dismiss Plaintiff’s claims for 
28  lack of standing at this time.  (See Doc. 13 at 14, 21.)  To the extent this matter proceeds, 
1  the issue may be revisited at the class certification stage.  See Forcellati, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8700292"><a href="/opinion/8717099/thomas-v-united-states-department-of-homeland-security/#1161" aria-description="Citation for case: Thomas v. United States Department of Homeland Security">876 F. Supp. 2d 
2  at 1161</a></span><pre class="inline"> (holding “we agree with the numerous recent decisions that have concluded that 
3  Defendants’  argument  is  better  taken  under  the  lens  of  typicality  or  adequacy  of 
4  representation, rather than standing”); see also Brazil, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2013 WL 5312418</span><pre class="inline">, at *8 (finding 
5  the plaintiff “has adequately demonstrated standing, at least for purposes of surviving a 
6  motion  to  dismiss,  to  assert  substantially  similar  claims  based  on  products  that  are 
7  substantially similar to the Purchased Products”); Koh v. S.C. Johnson &amp; Son, Inc., No. C-
8  09-00927 RMW, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2010 WL 94265</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 6, 2010) (denying defendant’s 
9  motion to dismiss for lack of standing and deferred ruling on the standing question until 
10  class certification).                                                     
11       C.   California Consumer Protection Statutes                         
12       The UCL, CLRA, and FAL, which are the basis of Plaintiff’s first, second, and third 
13  causes of action, are California consumer protection statutes.  The UCL prohibits “unfair 
14  competition,” which includes “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.”  
15  CAL. BUS. &amp; PROF. CODE § 17200.  California’s CLRA prohibits “unfair methods of 
16  competition  and  unfair  or  deceptive  acts  or  practices.”    CAL.  CIV.  CODE  §  1770.  
17  California’s FAL prohibits any “unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising.”  CAL. 
18  BUS. &amp; PROF. CODE § 17500.  “Because the same standard for fraudulent activity governs 
19  all three statutes, courts often analyze the three statutes together.”   Hadley v. Kellogg Sales 
20  Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1089" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1089</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017); see, e.g., In re Sony Gaming Networks 
21  &amp; Customer Data Sec. Breach Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8714620"><a href="/opinion/8731416/in-re-sony-gaming-networks-customer-data-security-breach-litigation/#985" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Sony Gaming Networks &amp; Customer Data Security...">996 F. Supp. 2d 942, 985</a></span><pre class="inline"> (S.D. Cal. 2014) (“[c]ourts 
22  often analyze these statutes together because they share similar attributes”); Consumer 
23  Advocates v. Echostar Satellite Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2278412"><a href="/opinion/2278412/consumer-advocates-v-echostar-satellite-corp/#1360" aria-description="Citation for case: Consumer Advocates v. Echostar Satellite Corp.">113 Cal. App. 4th 1351, 1360</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2003) (analyzing the 
24  UCL, CLRA, and FAL together).                                             
25            a.   “Reasonable Consumer” Standard                             
26       Claims made under the UCLA, CLRA, and FAL are governed by the “reasonable 
27  consumer” standard.  Williams v. Gerber Prods. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d 934, 938</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008); 
28  Ebner v. Fresh, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8414293"><a href="/opinion/8442970/ebner-v-fresh-inc/#965" aria-description="Citation for case: Ebner v. Fresh, Inc.">838 F.3d 958, 965</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2016).  Under this standard, a plaintiff 
1  must “show that ‘members of the public are likely to be deceived.’”  Ebner, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8414293"><a href="/opinion/8442970/ebner-v-fresh-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Ebner v. Fresh, Inc.">838 F.3d at 
2</a></span><pre class="inline">  965 (quoting Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d at 938</a></span><pre class="inline">).  This standard “requires more than a mere 
3  possibility that [the] label ‘might conceivably be misunderstood by some few consumers 
4  viewing it in an unreasonable manner.’”  Becerra v. Dr. Pepper/Seven Up, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">945 F.3d 
5</span><pre class="inline">  1225, 1228 (9th Cir. 2019) (quoting Lavie v. Procter &amp; Gamble Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2283563"><a href="/opinion/2283563/county-of-los-angeles-v-state-board-of-equalization/#496" aria-description="Citation for case: County of Los Angeles v. State Board of Equalization">105 Cal. App. 4th 
6  496, 508</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2003)).  Rather, it must be “probable that a significant portion of the general 
7  consuming public or of targeted consumers, acting reasonably in the circumstances, could 
8  be misled.”  Lavie, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2283563"><a href="/opinion/2283563/county-of-los-angeles-v-state-board-of-equalization/#508" aria-description="Citation for case: County of Los Angeles v. State Board of Equalization">105 Cal. App. 4th at 508</a></span><pre class="inline">; see also White v. Kroger Co., No. 21-CV-
9  08004-RS, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 888657</span><pre class="inline">, at *1 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 25, 2022).  In general, “[t]he question 
10  of whether a business practice is deceptive in most cases presents a question of fact not 
11  amenable to resolution on a motion to dismiss.”  Hairston v. S. Beach Beverage Co., No. 
12  CV  12-1429-JFW  DTBX,  </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012  WL  1893818</span><pre class="inline">,  at  *4  (C.D.  Cal.  May  18,  2012) 
13  (citing Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d at 938</a></span><pre class="inline">).                                       
14       In  the  Motion,  Defendant  argues  Plaintiff’s  FAC  should be  dismissed  because 
15  Plaintiff has not plausibly alleged that the term “Reef Friendly” is deceptive and would 
16  mislead a reasonable consumer.  (Doc. 16–1 at 10–11.)  Defendant contends that their 
17  labeling is not misleading because when viewed “as a whole” the packaging makes clear 
18  that “Reef Friendly” refers only to the absence of oxybenzone and octinoxate.  (Id.)  
19  Defendant states that “[w]hatever the term ‘Reef Friendly’ might mean in isolation, the 
20  packaging makes clear that this term—as used on the labeling of a product marketed as 
21  ‘Hawaiian  Sunscreen’—refers to the  absence of oxybenzone  and  octinoxate,  the  two 
22  chemicals specifically prohibited by Hawaii law.”  (Id.)  Moreover, “the small, stylized 
23  ‘Reef Friendly’ statement does not appear by itself on the packaging of the products 
24  Plaintiff purchased.  Instead, the packaging makes clear what ‘Reef Friendly’ means: ‘no 
25  oxybenzone [or] octinoxate.’”  (Id.)                                      
26       As an initial matter, Plaintiff explains that “whether or not a reasonable consumer is 
27  misled is ‘typically a question of fact not appropriate for determination [on a motion to 
28  dismiss].’”  (Doc. 19 at 13–14 (quoting Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d at 938</a></span><pre class="inline">).)  Thus, “whether 
1  reasonable consumers would understand ‘Reef Friendly’ to mean that (i) the Products do 
2  not contain ingredients that are harmful to reefs, or (ii) the Products do not contain two 
3  ingredients that are harmful to reefs but contain four other ingredients that are harmful to 
4  reefs,” is not a question to be decided at this time.  (Doc. 19 at 16.)  However, it is Plaintiff’s 
5  position  that  “Defendant  has  made  material  misrepresentations  and  omissions,  both 
6  directly and indirectly, related to their Products advertised as ‘Reef[]Friendly’” and that 
7  “Defendant’s conduct . . . is misleading, unfair, unlawful and is injurious to consumers who 
8  purchased the Products and were deceived by Defendant’s misrepresentations.”  (Doc. 13 
9  at 27.)  In particular, Plaintiff  argues “that the Products do not contain two harmful 
10  ingredients does not make the ‘Reef Friendly’ claim truthful where the Products contain 
11  four other ingredients that are harmful to reefs.”  (Doc. 19 at 15.)  Moreover, Plaintiff 
12  contends that “Defendant’s disclosure of the Products’ active ingredients on the Products’ 
13  rear label does not immunize it from liability for its false advertising that the Products are 
14  ‘Reef Friendly.’”  (Id. at 17.)                                           
15       The Court finds that whether or not a reasonable person would be misled by the 
16  “Reef Friendly” label on Defendant’s Products is not fit for determination on a motion to 
17  dismiss in this case.  See Linear Technology Corp. v. Applied Materials, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">152 Cal. 
18</span><pre class="inline">  App. 4th 115, 134–35 (2007) (quoting McKell v. Washington Mutual, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">142 Cal. App. 
19</span><pre class="inline">  4th  1457,  1472  (2006)) (“[w]hether  a  practice  is  deceptive,  fraudulent,  or  unfair  is 
20  generally a question of fact which requires ‘consideration and weighing of evidence from 
21  both sides’ and which usually cannot be made on demurrer”); see also Pelayo v. Nestle 
22  USA, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8713751"><a href="/opinion/8730551/pelayo-v-nestle-usa-inc/#978" aria-description="Citation for case: Pelayo v. Nestle USA, Inc.">989 F. Supp. 2d 973, 978</a></span><pre class="inline"> (C.D. Cal. 2013) (“[t]he question of whether a business 
23  practice is deceptive in most cases presents a question of fact not amenable to resolution 
24  on a motion to dismiss”); Rooney v. Cumberland Packing Corp., No. 12-CV-0033-H DHB, 
25  </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 1512106</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (S.D. Cal. Apr. 16, 2012) (quoting Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#939" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d at 939</a></span><pre class="inline">) 
26  (“it is a ‘rare situation’ where granting a motion to dismiss claims under the UCL is 
27  appropriate”); Pelayo, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8713751"><a href="/opinion/8730551/pelayo-v-nestle-usa-inc/#978" aria-description="Citation for case: Pelayo v. Nestle USA, Inc.">989 F. Supp. 2d at 978</a></span><pre class="inline"> (where a court “can conclude as a matter of 
28  law that members of the public are not likely to be deceived by the product packaging, 
1  dismissal is appropriate”).  Here, the Court cannot conclude, as a matter of law, that a 
2  reasonable consumer would not be deceived by the “Reef Friendly” label on Defendant’s 
3  packaging.  See Allred v. Frito-Lay N. Am., Inc., No. 17-CV-1345 JLS (BGS), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WL 
4</span><pre class="inline">  1185227, at *5 (S.D. Cal. Mar. 7, 2018) (“[t]he Court cannot conclude as a matter of law 
5  that a reasonable consumer would not be deceived by the packaging and ingredient list”); 
6  see also Allred v. Frito-Lay N. Am., Inc., No. 17-CV-1345 JLS (BGS), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2018 WL 1185227</span><pre class="inline">, 
7  at *5 (S.D. Cal. Mar. 7, 2018) (holding that “the Court cannot determine at [the motion to 
8  dismiss] stage whether the Product’s advertising was false or misleading”).   
9       Accepting as true all allegations in the FAC, it is plausible that statements on the 
10  Products’  packaging  could  deceive  a  reasonable  consumer.    Accordingly,  the  Court 
11  concludes that this does not amount to the “rare situation” in which dismissing Plaintiff’s 
12  UCL, CLRA, and FAL claims is appropriate.  See Williams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#939" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d at 939</a></span><pre class="inline">.  
13            b.   “Unlawful, Unfair, or Fraudulent” under the UCL            
14       As noted previously, the UCL prohibits “unfair competition,” which includes “any 
15  unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.”2  CAL. BUS. &amp; PROF. CODE §17200.  
16  In  addition  to  arguing  that  their  labeling  is  not  deceptive,  Defendant  contends  that 
17  Plaintiff’s claims also fail under the “unlawful” and “unfair” prongs of the UCL.  (Doc. 
18  16–1 at 15–16.)                                                           
19       The FAC alleges that Defendant’s business practices violate the unfair prong of the 
20  UCL because Defendant’s “conduct is substantially injurious to consumers, offends public 
21  policy, and is immoral, unethical, oppressive, and unscrupulous, as the gravity of the 
22  conduct outweighs any alleged benefits.”  (Doc. 19 at 26–27.)  Moreover, as discussed in 
23  detail supra, the FAC contains sufficient allegations that Defendant has made material

   on denial of reh’g (Feb. 24, 2012).  Whether or not Defendant’s labeling is likely to deceive

11       D.   Breach of Express Warranty                                      
12       Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action alleges breach of express warranty.  (Doc. 13 at 32–
13  33.)  Defendant argues that “Plaintiff’s failure to allege a plausible claim of deception is 
14  also fatal to her claim[] for breach of express [] warranty” and that this cause of action 
15  should be dismissed with prejudice.  (Doc. 16–1 at 18, 23.)               
16       California Commercial Code § 2313 provides that “(a) [a]ny affirmation of fact or 
17  promise made by the seller to the buyer which relates to the goods and becomes part of the 
18  basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods shall conform to the 
19  affirmation or promise,” and “(b) [a]ny description of the goods which is made part of the 
20  basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods shall conform to the 
21  description.”  CAL. COM. CODE § 2313.  To plead a claim for breach of express warranty, 
22  the plaintiff “must allege the exact terms of the warranty, plaintiff’s reasonable reliance 
23  thereon, and a breach of that warranty which proximately causes plaintiff injury.”  Williams 
24  v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2139353"><a href="/opinion/2139353/williams-v-beechnut-nutrition-corp/#142" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp.">185 Cal. App. 3d 135, 142</a></span><pre class="inline"> (Ct. App. 1986).   
25       Plaintiff’s FAC alleges that: (1) Defendant expressly warranted on the packaging of 
26  the  Products  that  they  are  “Reef  Friendly,”  (2)  Plaintiff  and  members  of  the  class 
27  reasonably and justifiably relied on the express warranty, (3) Defendant breached the 
28  express warranty by selling the Products, which contain ingredients that are not reef 
1  friendly, and (4)  Plaintiff and members of the class paid a premium price for the Products 
2  but did not obtain the full value of the Products as represented.”  (Doc. 13 at 33.)  It is 
3  Plaintiff’s position that, had she and the class members “known of the true nature of the 
4  Products, they would not have purchased the Products or would not have been willing to 
5  pay the premium price associated with the Products.”  (Id.)               
6       The Court finds the allegations in the FAC sufficient to state a claim for breach of 
7  express warranty.  See Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. at 678</a></span><pre class="inline">.  Therefore, the Court DENIES Defendant’s 
8  Motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action for breach of express warranty. 
9       E.   Breach of Implied Warranty                                      
10       Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action alleges breach of implied warranty.  (Doc. 13 at 33–
11  34.)  Defendant argues that “Plaintiff has not stated a plausible breach of implied warranty 
12  claim” because Plaintiff does not allege the Products are unfit for use “[n]or does she 
13  dispute that the sunscreen delivers all of the sun protection advertised on the labeling.”  
14  (Doc. 16–1 at 18, 20.)  Rather, Plaintiff alleges “the sunscreen does not conform to the 
15  representation that it is ‘Reef Friendly.’”  (Doc. 16–1 at 20.)           
16       California Commercial Code § 2314 provides that “a warranty that the goods shall 
17  be merchantable is implied in a contract for their sale if the seller is a merchant with respect 
18  to goods of that kind.”  CAL. COM. CODE § 2314(1).  It also explains that “[g]oods to be 
19  merchantable must be at least such as . . . (f) [c]onform to the promises or affirmations of 
20  fact made on the container or label if any.”  CAL. COM. CODE § 2314(2)(f).   
21       The FAC alleges that Defendant is a merchant with respect to the sale of sunscreen 
22  products and, therefore, a warranty of merchantability is implied in every contract for sale.  
23  (Doc. 13 at 34.)  Moreover, “[b]y advertising the Products with their current labeling, 
24  Defendant  made  a promise on  the  label  of  the  Products that  the  Products  are  ‘Reef 
25  Friendly.’”  (Id.)  However, the Products do not conform to the promises made on the label 
26  because the Products are not “Reef Friendly” and contain ingredients harmful to reefs.  (Id.)  
27  Thus, Plaintiff explains that, had she and the class members “known that the products were 
28  not ‘Reef Friendly,’ they would not have been willing to pay the premium price associated 
1  with them or would not have purchased them at all.”  (Id.)                
2       The Court find Plaintiff’s pleading sufficiently states a claim for relief as the statute 
3  provides that goods shall “[c]onform to the promises or affirmations of fact made on the 
4  container or label . . . .”  CAL. COM. CODE § 2314(2)(f); see White, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 888657</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 
5  (finding the plaintiff adequately pled breach of implied warranty when alleging contents of 
6  product did not conform to the promises made on the packaging and labeling).  Therefore, 
7  the Court DENIES Defendant’s Motion to dismiss Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for 
8  breach of implied warranty.                                               
9       F.   Equitable Relief                                                
10       Lastly, Defendant argues that “even if this Court does not dismiss Plaintiff’s lawsuit 
11  outright, it should nonetheless dismiss her claims to the extent they seek restitution, an 
12  injunction, and other equitable relief.”  (Doc. 16–1 at 21.)  It is Defendant’s position that 
13  Plaintiff  cannot  seek  equitable  relief  under  California’s  consumer  protection  statutes 
14  without establishing that she lacks an adequate remedy at law.  (Id. (citing Sonner v. 
15  Premier Nutrition Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">971 F.3d 834</span><pre class="inline">, 844 (9th Cir. 2020).)  Defendant explains that 
16  “[s]everal of Plaintiff’s claims permit her to recover money damages, and those damages 
17  would  adequately  compensate  her  for  her  alleged  injury—i.e.,  the  supposed  ‘price 
18  premium’ she allegedly paid due to [Defendant’s] use of the phrase ‘Reef Friendly’ on the 
19  labeling . . . .”  Thus, Plaintiff is barred from seeking equitable relief. 
20       Plaintiff  counters  that  “the  gravamen  of  Plaintiff’s  claims  is  that  Defendant’s 
21  marketing and advertising of the Products as ‘Reef Friendly’ misleads consumers.”  (Doc. 
22  19 at 28.)  Thus, “[t]he injury to consumers cannot be fully compensated through an award 
23  of  monetary  damages  because  the  Products  as  currently  labeled  are  deceptive  and 
24  misleading and injunctive relief is necessary to put a stop to the continuing harm.”  (Id.)  
25  “A legal remedy is not adequate in these circumstances, and it is appropriate to permit 
26  Plaintiff to assert the equitable remedy of an injunction.”  (Id.)        
27       Here, the Court finds Plaintiff may lack an adequate legal remedy for future harm 
28  such that barring equitable remedies is unjustified at this time.  See Deras v. Volkswagen
