<opinion data-order="11" data-type="opinion" id="x999-1" type="majority">
<author id="b887-19">HEANEY, Circuit Judge.</author>
<p id="b887-20">The principal question<footnotemark>1</footnotemark> on appeal is whether the trial judge impermissibly commented on the evidence in his charge to the jury, thus, in effect, directing a verdict. We hold that it did so, and reverse the defendant’s conviction.</p>
<p id="b887-21">The defendant was convicted on Counts I, II, III and XI of the indictment which charged him with violating the mail fraud statute, <span class="citation no-link">18 U.S.C. § 1341</span>.<footnotemark>2</footnotemark> The evidence showed that the de<page-number citation-index="1" label="832">*832</page-number>fendant operated the Midwest Mutual Casualty Company and its management company, Gibraltar Management Corporation. The essence of the charge was that the defendant had fraudulently misrepresented the financial status of the insurance company so that it could continue doing business for the defendant’s benefit, despite its failure to maintain the assets required by state law. The first three counts charged that the defendant had mailed fraudulent financial reports to the State of Missouri Division of Insurance. Count XI charged that he had caused the Division of Insurance to mail fraudulent information concerning the financial status of the company to an individual who had made inquiries.</p>
<p id="b888-4">The trial judge correctly instructed the jury that the following essential elements of the offense must be established:</p>
<blockquote id="b888-5">“First, the defendant’s act or acts of having devised and having intended to devise a scheme or artifice to defraud and to obtain money and property from Gibraltar Management Corporation, and Midwest Mutual Casualty Company and its policyholders and claimants by means of the false and fraudulent pretenses, representations and promises as submitted to you in this charge; and</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b888-6">“Second, the defendant’s act or acts of using or causing the use of the mails as submitted to you; and “Third, the defendant’s wilful and knowing doing of said act or acts with intent to execute the scheme or artifice to defraud, as submitted to you.”</blockquote>
<p id="akm-dedup-0">However, at the close of his instructions, the court made the following comments to the jury with respect to the defendant’s intent to defraud and execute the scheme to defraud:</p>
<blockquote id="b888-7">“Let’s take the cross-checks which occurred at the end of each month in respect to the particular statements which are involved in Counts One, Two, Three and Eleven. <em>It seems to me that this evidence shows beyond a reasonable doubt that under an arrangement which was dictated by the defendant, </em>and recorded by the witness Doris Howard, about drawing a check on Gibraltar account at the end of the month payable to Midwest, and holding it up until a check was thrown back from Midwest to Gibraltar on the first of the month, or the first business day following the close of the last month, <em>that this arrangement was designed to and did misrepresent the financial condition of Midwest Casualty Company on the books of the company and on the statements submitted to the Insurance Department.</em></blockquote>
<blockquote id="b888-9">“In the first place, I read you the paragraph in the management contract which provided that the management fees would not be due and payable to Gibraltar from the premiums written during the month until sometime on or before the 15th day of the month following that in which the business was written. And you have heard the evidence which, in my judgment is clear, and shows beyond a reasonable doubt that Gibraltar was in fact in violation of that contract drawing premiums before they were due on estimates in amounts which were quite substantially higher than when they became due. And this would adversely affect the financial position of Midwest if it were honestly reported in the financial statement.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b888-10">“And to show you why I believe the evidence establishes this beyond a reasonable doubt, I am going to refer to the fact that the arrangement, as shown by the document in evidence that Mrs. Howard identified with her manuscript on it that she was told this procedure by the defendant, to follow it, there was a specific direction that the Gibraltar check which was drawn on the 31st and which was supposed to put back this overdraw and for which there was no money in the bank, was not to be deposited until the first when Midwest had given it back another check which created a new overdraw on the management fees <page-number citation-index="1" label="833">*833</page-number>and for which Midwest had money in the bank.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b889-5">“The net result of this transaction is that Midwest was itself paying back, restoring to Gibraltar, the overdraw which it pretended to have paid off by drawing a check and dating it on the 31st of the month and then holding it up and not depositing it in the bank. “A good way to test that in your mind is to just assume that Midwest had drawn a check on the 31st, since the end of the month is said to be a normal accounting period, and was so used by Gibraltar for the amount that it drew on the first. It would have reduced Midwest’s cash balance by that amount on the 31st. So in this way, a check which was never deposited, which was not intended to be deposited on the 31st of a month, was misrepresented in the statements as cash, a deposit in transit, when as a matter of fact it had not been deposited under the understanding and was not deposited until the first of the month. And I am sure that the evidence shows this beyond a reasonable doubt.” (Emphasis added.)</blockquote>
<p id="b889-6">The trial court may express its opinion on the guilt of the accused only where all facts are admitted by the accused and only a question of law remains. United States v. Murdock, <span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">290 U.S. 389</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">54 S.Ct. 223</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">78 L.Ed. 381</a></span> (1933); Billeci v. United States, 87 U. S.App.D.C. 274, <span class="citation" data-id="225722"><a href="/opinion/225722/billeci-v-united-states-lewis-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Billeci v. United States. Lewis v. United States">184 F.2d 394</a></span> (1950). See, United States v. Spica, <span class="citation" data-id="285598"><a href="/opinion/285598/united-states-v-patricia-antoinette-spica/#133" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Patricia Antoinette Spica">413 F.2d 129, 133</a></span> (8th Cir. 1969). Where the question of intent to commit the offense charged is disputed, an expression of opinion on the guilt of the accused is reversible error. United States v. <span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">Murdock, supra;</a></span> United States v. Musgrave, <span class="citation" data-id="297534"><a href="/opinion/297534/united-states-v-kenneth-l-musgrave-and-jack-bryant/#763" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Kenneth L. Musgrave and Jack Bryant">444 F.2d 755, 763</a></span> (5th Cir. 1971); United States v. Smith, <span class="citation" data-id="281446"><a href="/opinion/281446/united-states-v-james-harrison-smith/#899" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. James Harrison Smith">399 F.2d 896, 899</a></span> (6th Cir. 1968).</p>
<p id="b889-13">In the present case, the trial judge instructed the jury that the evidence showed “beyond a reasonable doubt” that the defendant had “dictated” an arrangement which “was designed to and did misrepresent the financial condition of Midwest Casualty Company on the books of the company and on the statements submitted to the Insurance Department.” This statement made it clear to the jury that, in the trial judge’s opinion, the government had produced proof beyond a reasonable doubt of two of the three essential elements of the offense — that the defendant had acted with specific intent to defraud, and that he had, with intent to execute his scheme to defraud, submitted reports to the Division of Insurance. Yet, the issue of intent was disputed by the defendant in cross-examination, through defense witnesses and in his argument to the jury. In our view, the trial judge’s comments were equivalent to a partial directed verdict and expression of guilt.</p>
<p id="b889-14">The government urges, however, that in this case, the trial judge’s comments are not reversible under <em><span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">Murdock</a></span> </em>because the trial judge did not actually use the term “guilt” in stating its opinion.<footnotemark>3</footnotemark> We find this distinction to be <page-number citation-index="1" label="834">*834</page-number>without merit. Where the essential element of intent is in dispute, the defendant is just as effectively prejudiced whether the court states, as in <em><span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">Murdock</a></span>, </em>that the defendant is “guilty,” or if it states, as here, that the government has established the disputed element of intent beyond a reasonable doubt. See, McBride v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="259778"><a href="/opinion/259778/jack-d-mcbride-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Jack D. McBride v. United States">314 F.2d 75</a></span> (10th Cir. 1963); Bennett v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="244188"><a href="/opinion/244188/william-lee-bennett-v-united-states/#99" aria-description="Citation for case: William Lee Bennett v. United States">252 F.2d 97, 99</a></span> (10th Cir. 1958); Davis v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="237946"><a href="/opinion/237946/robert-edward-davis-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Robert Edward Davis v. United States">227 F.2d 568</a></span> (10th Cir. 1955). <em>Cf., </em>United States v. Ornstein, <span class="citation" data-id="270365"><a href="/opinion/270365/united-states-v-howard-ornstein/#224" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Howard Ornstein">355 F.2d 222, 224</a></span> (6th Cir. 1966).</p>
<p id="b890-4">In McBride v. United States, <em>supra, </em>the defendant was also charged with mail fraud. He argued that there was no intent to defraud. At the close of that trial, the judge also commented on the evidence. He did not state that he believed the defendant to be “guilty,” but made it clear that he believed that fraudulent intent had been established.<footnotemark>4</footnotemark> The Court of Appeals reversed, relying on United States v. <span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">Murdock, <em>supra, </em></a></span>and stated:</p>
<blockquote id="b890-13">“ * * * The comments must * * * be taken as a statement of the court that the accused was guilty, and this was not warranted under the circumstances. * * *”</blockquote>
<p id="b890-15">McBride v. United States, <em>supra, </em><span class="citation" data-id="259778"><a href="/opinion/259778/jack-d-mcbride-v-united-states/#77" aria-description="Citation for case: Jack D. McBride v. United States">314 F. 2d at 77</a></span>.</p>
<p id="b890-16">While we believe that the trial judge’s statement of opinion with regard to the element of intent constitutes reversible error under United States v. <span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">Murdock, <em>supra, </em></a></span>we believe his other comments provide additional reasons for reversing this case.<footnotemark>5</footnotemark> As Judge Stephenson recently said:</p>
<blockquote id="b890-17">“* * * ^he trial court commands the attention and respect of the jury. <page-number citation-index="1" label="835">*835</page-number>Great care must be exercised so as to avoid the appearance of advocacy for a particular party. * * *”</blockquote>
<p id="b891-5">Scruggs v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="299755"><a href="/opinion/299755/carios-lamont-scruggs-v-united-states-of-america-reginald-coleman/#363" aria-description="Citation for case: Carios Lamont Scruggs v. United States of America,...">450 F.2d 359, 363</a></span> (8th Cir. 1971), cert. denied, Chambers v. United States, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/U.S./405/1071/">405 U.S. 1071</a></span>, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/S.Ct./92/1521/">92 S.Ct. 1521</a></span>, <span class="citation no-link">31 L.Ed.2d 804</span> (1972). Accord, United States v. Dunmore, <span class="citation" data-id="298452"><a href="/opinion/298452/united-states-v-tommie-lee-dunmore-united-states-of-america-v-ronald/#1218" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Tommie Lee Dunmore, United States of...">446 F. 2d 1214, 1218</a></span> (8th Cir. 1971), cert. denied, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/U.S./404/1041/">404 U.S. 1041</a></span>, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/S.Ct./92/726/">92 S.Ct. 726</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="8978505"><a href="/opinion/8986477/sandoval-v-superior-court-of-los-angeles-county/" aria-description="Citation for case: Sandoval v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County">30 L.Ed.2d 734</a></span> (1972).</p>
<p id="b891-6">The Supreme Court has recognized:</p>
<blockquote id="b891-7">“* -» * iphg influence of the trial judge on the jury ‘is necessarily and properly of great weight’ and ‘his lightest word or intimation is received with deference, and may prove controlling’. * * *”</blockquote>
<p id="b891-11">Quercia v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="102106"><a href="/opinion/102106/quercia-v-united-states/#470" aria-description="Citation for case: Quercia v. United States">289 U.S. 466, 470</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="102106"><a href="/opinion/102106/quercia-v-united-states/#699" aria-description="Citation for case: Quercia v. United States">53 S.Ct. 698, 699</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="102106"><a href="/opinion/102106/quercia-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Quercia v. United States">77 L.Ed. 1321</a></span> (1933).</p>
<p id="b891-12">In Weare v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="1506974"><a href="/opinion/1506974/weare-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Weare v. United States">1 F.2d 617, 618</a></span> (8th Cir. 1924), rev’d, <span class="citation no-link">276 U.S. 599</span>, <span class="citation no-link">48 S.Ct. 321</span>, <span class="citation no-link">72 L.Ed. 724</span> (1928), we stated:</p>
<blockquote id="b891-13">“The instructions, however, should not be argumentative. The court cannot direct a verdict of guilty in criminal cases, even if the facts are undisputed. * * * It should not be permitted to do indirectly what it cannot do directly, and by .its instructions to in effect argue the jury into a verdict of guilty.” (Citation omitted.)</blockquote>
<p id="b891-15">The trial judge’s analysis of the evidence was, by its terms, an argument for his view of the evidence. See, Har<page-number citation-index="1" label="836">*836</page-number>dy v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="265183"><a href="/opinion/265183/zebedee-hardy-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Zebedee Hardy v. United States">118 U.S.App.D.C. 253</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="265183"><a href="/opinion/265183/zebedee-hardy-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Zebedee Hardy v. United States">335 F.2d 288</a></span> (1964). Throughout his comments, he made argumentative statements which emphasized his belief that a fraudulent intent had been proven. Furthermore, while the trial judge summarized the evidence favorable to the government, it failed to do the same with respect to the evidence submitted by the defendant. This was error, United States v. <span class="citation" data-id="298452"><a href="/opinion/298452/united-states-v-tommie-lee-dunmore-united-states-of-america-v-ronald/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Tommie Lee Dunmore, United States of...">Dunmore, <em>supra, </em></a></span><span class="citation" data-id="298452"><a href="/opinion/298452/united-states-v-tommie-lee-dunmore-united-states-of-america-v-ronald/#1218" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Tommie Lee Dunmore, United States of...">446 F.2d at 1218</a></span>; Boatright v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="1543391"><a href="/opinion/1543391/boatright-v-united-states/#739" aria-description="Citation for case: Boatright v. United States">105 F.2d 737, 739</a></span> (8th Cir. 1939), even though the evidence supporting the defendant was insubstantial.<footnotemark>6</footnotemark> The only mention of evidence produced by the defendant was a negative one:</p>
<blockquote id="b892-4">“Mr. Burgee’s testimony [the defendant’s accountant], in some respects, left a great deal wanting, in my mind. He assumed some things were loans from Able that were shown on the books to be not loans. He also tried a cut-off date for his computation to show how much of the agents’ collections had been realized at the time Gibraltar made this statement. And even then, his own evidence, making those assumptions, showed that at the time the $150,000.00 was completely paid, that there was a large part of those agents’ collections which went into the payments. And I think you should scrutinize that transaction very carefully.”</blockquote>
<p id="b892-11">Moreover, the prejudice to the defendant from these erroneous comments was not cured by the trial judge’s instructions to the jury that they were the sole judge of the facts, and that they must determine whether there was an intent to defraud beyond a reasonable doubt.<footnotemark>7</footnotemark> United States v. <span class="citation" data-id="281446"><a href="/opinion/281446/united-states-v-james-harrison-smith/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. James Harrison Smith">Smith, <em>supra, </em></a></span><page-number citation-index="1" label="837">*837</page-number><span class="citation" data-id="281446"><a href="/opinion/281446/united-states-v-james-harrison-smith/#899" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. James Harrison Smith">399 F.2d at 899</a></span>; McBride v. United States, supra; Davis v. United States, <em>supra. </em>A similar instruction was given by the trial judge in the <em><span class="citation" data-id="102166"><a href="/opinion/102166/united-states-v-murdock/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Murdock">Murdock</a></span> </em>case. Nevertheless, the Supreme Court reversed. This Court has recognized that “a mere withdrawal of words and a direction to the jury that the question is for them, is not always sufficient.” Rudd v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="8772748"><a href="/opinion/8788826/rudd-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Rudd v. United States">173 F. 912</a></span> (8th Cir. 1909). See, United States v. <span class="citation" data-id="298452"><a href="/opinion/298452/united-states-v-tommie-lee-dunmore-united-states-of-america-v-ronald/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Tommie Lee Dunmore, United States of...">Dunmore, <em>supra, </em></a></span><span class="citation" data-id="298452"><a href="/opinion/298452/united-states-v-tommie-lee-dunmore-united-states-of-america-v-ronald/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Tommie Lee Dunmore, United States of...">446 F.2d at 1217</a></span> n. 2; Cook v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="1479903"><a href="/opinion/1479903/cook-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Cook v. United States">18 F.2d 50</a></span> (8th Cir. 1927). Furthermore, in giving the alleged curative instruction, the trial judge emphasized his authority by pointing out his long legal experience and indicating that he was more likely than the jury to understand the “crucial” or “primary” matters on which he commented.</p>
<p id="b893-13">We regret the necessity of reversing this judgment on the grounds that the experienced, able and fair-minded judge exceeded permissible comment on the evidence. The ease against the defendant was a strong one, but the court’s statement that the defendant’s fraudulent intent had been proved beyond a reasonable doubt, along with his comments on the evidence, substantially interfered with the defendant’s right to trial by jury, and require that the defendant be given a new trial. See, United States v. Hayward, <span class="citation" data-id="287865"><a href="/opinion/287865/united-states-v-milton-l-hayward/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Milton L. Hayward">136 U.S.App.D.C. 300</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="287865"><a href="/opinion/287865/united-states-v-milton-l-hayward/#145" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Milton L. Hayward">420 F. 2d 142, 145</a></span> (1969); United States v. Porter, <span class="citation" data-id="277967"><a href="/opinion/277967/united-states-v-harold-porter/#276" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Harold Porter">386 F.2d 270, 276</a></span> (6th Cir. 1967); United States v. <span class="citation" data-id="270365"><a href="/opinion/270365/united-states-v-howard-ornstein/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Howard Ornstein">Ornstein, <em>supra; </em></a></span>Davis v. United States, <em>supra, </em><span class="citation" data-id="237946"><a href="/opinion/237946/robert-edward-davis-v-united-states/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Robert Edward Davis v. United States">227 F.2d at 570</a></span>; United States v. Link, <span class="citation" data-id="231235"><a href="/opinion/231235/united-states-v-link/#595" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Link">202 F.2d 592, 595</a></span> (3rd Cir. 1953); Rudd v. United States, <em>supra.</em><footnotemark><em>8</em></footnotemark></p>
<p id="b893-14">Reversed and remanded.</p>

<p id="b887-14">The defendant argues that the trial judge did not adequately instruct with respect to the evidence of the defendant’s actions prior to the statute of limitations, and that he did not adequately instruct with respect to those actions which constituted violations of state law. This argument is without merit. The instructions taken as a whole were quite sufficient in these respects.</p>
<p id="b887-15">The defendant argues that the proof of mailing was insufficient. The evidence as to mailing, particularly with respect to Counts I and XI, was clear. With respect to Count I, there was evidence of a practice of mailing in the defendant’s office, and the envelope containing the defendant’s financial report was produced. The envelope contained a prepaid postage mark and certified mail tags. With respect to Count XI, there was evidence of an unvaried practice of mailing at the Department of Insurance, and there was testimony by the individual who had mailed the letter and by the recipient that it had been mailed. With respect to Counts III and IV, the evidence was not so strong but was, nonetheless, sufficient. Compare, United States v. Brickey, <span class="citation" data-id="290224"><a href="/opinion/290224/united-states-v-william-c-brickey-jr/#684" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. William C. Brickey, Jr.">426 F.2d 680, 684</a></span> (8th Cir.), cert. denied, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/U.S./400/828/">400 U.S. 828</a></span>, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/S.Ct./91/55/">91 S.Ct. 55</a></span>, <span class="citation no-link">27 L.Ed.2d 57</span> (1970); Winel v. United States, <span class="citation" data-id="272997"><a href="/opinion/272997/vincent-edward-winel-jr-v-united-states/#648" aria-description="Citation for case: Vincent Edward Winel, Jr. v. United States">365 F.2d 646, 648</a></span> (8th Cir. 1966). But see, United States v. Baker, <span class="citation" data-id="6846034"><a href="/opinion/6949014/united-states-v-baker/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Baker">50 F.2d 122</a></span> (2nd Cir. 1931). In oral argument before this Court, the defendant appeared to suggest that the only fundamental issue in the case was that of the defendant’s intent.</p>
<p id="b887-23">Finally, the defendant contends that the evidence was insufficient to show that his insurance company was insolvent, or that he had fraudulently misrepresented the company’s financial status. These contentions are also without merit.</p>

In <em><span class="citation" data-id="1549626"><a href="/opinion/1549626/hartzell-v-united-states/" aria-description="Citation for case: Hartzell v. <p id="b889-16">In United States v. This Court sustained the conviction even though the trial judge had instructed the jury:</p>
<blockquote id="b889-17">“And as I told you, under the law I think it would be a fair finding that the government has proved beyond a reasonable doubt that he deliberately left Norborne, Missouri, absented himself <page-number citation-index="1" label="834">*834</page-number>from there where — the only, address the government had for him, and did not return to the Western District of Missouri for further proceedings in this case.”</blockquote>
<p id="b890-6">In <em><span class="citation" data-id="9456244"><a href="/opinion/293270/united-states-v-robert-bolivar-depugh/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. <p id="b893-20">Commentators have warned that the right to comment should be exercised with great caution. See, E. Devitt &amp; C. Blackmar, Federal Jury Practice &amp; Instructions, §§ 8.04, 10.04 (1970); Smith Jury Instructions in Seminars for Newly Appointed United States District Judges, 128 (Committee on Pre-Trial Procedure Judicial Conference of the United States, 1953). We think that the ABA Standards, Trial by Jury (Tentative Draft, May 1968) § 4.7, provide some sound standards for the trial judge who does feel a commentary is necessary in a particular case:</p>
<blockquote id="b893-21">“4.7 Summary of and comment on evidence.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="ASb"><page-number citation-index="1" label="838">*838</page-number>“(a) The court, at the time it instructs the jury, may summarize and comment on the evidence, provided the jury is clearly and unequivocally instructed that it is the exclusive judge of the facts, that it is to determine the weight of the evidence and the credibility of witnesses, and that it is not bound by the comments of the court.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b894-15">“(b) The summary and comment permitted in subsection (a) is governed by the following principles:</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b894-16">“(i) The court may analyze the evidence, draw the attention of the jury to important portions of the evidence, and fairly and accurately summarize the contentions of both the prosecution and the defense.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b894-17">“(ii) The court may not suggest a verdict of guilty or not guilty, nor may the court directly express an opinion on the guilt or innocence of the defendant.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b894-18">“(iii) The court may not present any item of evidence as a proven or undisputed fact unless the matter has been affirmatively conceded or is the subject of judicial notice.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b894-19">“(iv) The court may state the law and comment on matters in evidence bearing on the credibility of any witness, but may not directly express an opinion that certain testimony is worthy or unworthy of belief.”</blockquote>
<p id="b894-10">The tentative draft of the ABA Standards, Trial by Jury were submitted to the House of Delegates of the American Bar Association along with the recommendation of the Council of the Section of Criminal Law that § 4.7 be omitted. The commentary accompanying this recommendation stated in part:</p>
<blockquote id="b894-11">“Some who opposed the standard have done so on traditional grounds (as set forth at p. 125 of the original report), <em>e. g., </em>the danger of abuse and the difficulty in drawing the line between proper and improper comment. Others in opposition were primarily motivated, however, by recent developments which would make such comment particularly hazardous in cases where the defendant does not take the stand, and by the fact that permitting it only when the defendant does take the stand might pose other problems with respect to the defendant’s right to testify which outweigh the value of such comment.”</blockquote>
