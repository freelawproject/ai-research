<opinion type="majority">
<author id="b1223-31">Justice KOURLIS</author>
<p id="A7G">delivered the Opinion of the Court.</p>
<p id="b1223-32">We granted certiorari<footnotemark>1</footnotemark> to review issues in <em>People v. Altman, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1172660"><a href="/opinion/1172660/people-v-altman/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Altman">940 P.2d 1009</a></span> (Colo.App.<page-number citation-index="1" label="1166">*1166</page-number>1996), concerning application of the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule. We now conclude that the affidavit supporting the search warrant in this case was more than a “bare bones” affidavit. Thus, the officer reasonably relied upon the warrant in conducting the search, and the evidence is admissible under the good faith exception to the exclusionary .rule. Accordingly, we reverse the decision of the court of appeals and remand the case with directions to reinstate the district court’s ruling.</p>
<p id="b1224-4">I.</p>
<p id="b1224-5">On June 13, 1994, two federal Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) agents reported to Officer Paul Landolt of the Fort Collins Police Department that two white males had purchased hydroponic growing equipment, of the type commonly used for indoor marijuana cultivation, and had transported the equipment in a rental car to 508 Maple Street in Fort Collins.</p>
<p id="b1224-6">Officer Landolt commenced an investigation, and over .the next three weeks uncovered additional facts. The rental car used to transport the growing equipment had been rented for ten days, beginning June 10, to Robert Newman who listed an address on Yount Street in Fort Collins. When one of the DEA agents, acting undercover, called the telephone number Newman had given, to the rental car agency, a man identifying himself as Newman told the agent that he now lived at 508 Maple. Landholt observed the rental car in the driveway at 508 Maple on June 14 and June 15. During the course of his investigation, Landolt.also observed that Newman had two cars of his own registered in his name, which were parked at the Maple Street residence.</p>
<p id="b1224-7">Landolt checked the utility records for 508 Maple and discovered that respondent Damian Altman had been paying the bills since October of 1993. Landolt learned that since the utilities at that address had been under Altman’s name, the electrical usage had been two to three times higher than that for surrounding residences and two to three times higher than the previous resident’s usage.</p>
<p id="b1224-11">Landolt is a fourteen-year veteran of the Fort Collins Police Department with over five years’ experience in the Special Investigations Unit and training at the federal, state and local level in the area of controlled substance investigations. He has investigated indoor marijuana cultivation on several previous occasions. Based on the purchase of this indoor growing equipment in June at the height .of the outdoor growing season, the high electrical usage, and the suspicious circumstances surrounding the apparently unnecessary use of a rental ear, Landolt suspected that marijuana was being cultivated at the Maple Street residence.</p>
<p id="b1224-12">Landolt continued his investigation. He checked police department records and found that Altman had several prior contacts with Fort Collins police. Landolt also discovered an outstanding warrant for Altman’s arrest on misdemeanor charges.<footnotemark>2</footnotemark> Landolt checked city sales tax records to ascertain whether a business was registered at the Maple Street address that might account for the high electrical usage. No such record existed.</p>
<p id="b1224-13">Believing that there was probable cause to search the Maple Street residence for evidence of a marijuana growing operation, Landolt executed an affidavit in support of a search warrant. The affidavit related the facts we have set forth above.</p>
<p id="b1224-14">The magistrate agreed that the affidavit established probable cause and issued a search warrant for 508 Maple. A search of the premises uncovered, among other things, a 96-plant marijuana “farm” in the basement of the residence, operating on hydroponic technology with moving grow lights mounted on ceiling tracks and a carbon dioxide producing machine. On the ground floor, police found psilocybin mushrooms (a Schedule I controlled substance), some 268 grams of marijuana, additional marijuana plants growing in a closet and various documents connecting Altman to the residence.</p>
<p id="b1225-3"><page-number citation-index="1" label="1167">*1167</page-number>The state charged Altman with cultivation of marijuana, a class 4 felony under section 18-18—406(8)(a), 6 C.R.S. (1997), and unlawful possession of a Schedule I controlled substance, a class 3 felony under sections 18-18-405(2)(A)(I) and 18-18-203, 6 C.R.S. (1997). Prior to trial, Altman moved to suppress the evidence from the search alleging that Lan-dolt’s affidavit did not establish probable cause to search the residence at 508 Maple Street.</p>
<p id="b1225-4">The trial judge assigned to the case was, coincidentally, the magistrate who had issued the warrant. After hearing argument on the matter, and upon further reflection, the judge decided that the facts in the affidavit, while creating a certain amount of suspicion, did not rise to the level of probable cause. The judge did not suppress the evidence, however, because of the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule for warrant-based searches contained in section 16-3-308, 6 C.R.S. (1997).</p>
<p id="b1225-5">The court convicted Altman of both charges in a bench trial, and Altman appealed his conviction to the court of appeals. Altman argued that the good faith exception was inapplicable because Landolt’s affidavit was so lacking in indicia of probable cause as to render the officers’ reliance on it objectively unreasonable.</p>
<p id="b1225-6">The court of appeals held that under the totality of the circumstances the affidavit did not contain any facts clearly indicating that Altman might have been engaged in criminal activity, and that the degree of suspicion related to the non-criminal activity described was insufficient to establish probable cause. The court further held that the good faith exception to exclusion was inapplicable in this case because this was a “bare bones” affidavit, devoid of facts indicating current or previous illegal drug activity. We granted certiorari to consider the propriety of this ruling and now reverse.</p>
<p id="b1225-7">II.</p>
<p id="b1225-8">The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution and article II, section 7 of the Colorado Constitution protect persons from unreasonable searches and seizures and prohibit the issuance of a search warrant except upon probable cause supported by oath or affirmation particularly describing the place to be searched and objects to be seized. <em>See </em>U.S. Const. amend. IV; Colo. Const. art. 2, § 7. To establish probable cause, an affidavit in support of a warrant must allege facts sufficient to cause “a person of reasonable caution to believe that contraband or evidence of criminal activity is located at the place to be searched.” <em>People v. Quintana, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9536458"><a href="/opinion/1138456/people-v-quintana/#937" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Quintana">785 P.2d 934, 937</a></span> (Colo.1990). The analysis of probable cause under both the state and federal constitutions looks at the totality of the circumstances. <em>See People v. Turcotte-Schaeffer, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2604322"><a href="/opinion/2604322/people-v-turcotte-schaeffer/#660" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Turcotte-Schaeffer">843 P.2d 658, 660</a></span> (Colo.1993) (noting that we have adopted the standard set by the United States Supreme Court in <em>Illinois v. Gates, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429232"><a href="/opinion/110959/illinois-v-gates/" aria-description="Citation for case: Illinois v. Gates">462 U.S. 213</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429232"><a href="/opinion/110959/illinois-v-gates/" aria-description="Citation for case: Illinois v. Gates">103 S.Ct. 2317</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429232"><a href="/opinion/110959/illinois-v-gates/" aria-description="Citation for case: Illinois v. Gates">76 L.Ed.2d 527</a></span> (1983), for probable cause analysis under the Colorado Constitution). The probable cause standard does not lend itself to mathematical certainties and should not be laden with hy-pertechnical interpretations or rigid legal rules. <em>See People v. Leftwich, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/#1266" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">869 P.2d 1260, 1266</a></span> (Colo.1994); <em>People v. Atley, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1204600"><a href="/opinion/1204600/people-v-atley/#378" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Atley">727 P.2d 376, 378</a></span> (Colo.1986). Rather, judges, considering all of the circumstances, must make a practical, .common-sense decision whether a fair probability exists that a search of a particular place will reveal contraband or evidence of a crime. <em>See Atley, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1204600"><a href="/opinion/1204600/people-v-atley/#377" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Atley">727 P.2d at 377-78</a></span>.</p>
<p id="b1225-12">Because there is no precise formula for determining probable cause, “[r]easonable minds frequently may differ on the question whether a particular affidavit establishes probable ■ cause.” <em>United States v. Leon, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#914" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. 897, 914</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">82 L.Ed.2d 677</a></span> (1984); <em>see also Turcotte-Schaeffer, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2604322"><a href="/opinion/2604322/people-v-turcotte-schaeffer/#662" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Turcotte-Schaeffer">843 P.2d at 662</a></span> (acknowledging that the “facts here present a very close case of probable cause and a different issuing judge may have required more information, before issuing a warrant”); <em>United States v. Cancelmo, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9488470"><a href="/opinion/703163/united-states-v-benjamin-g-cancelmo-jr/#807" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Benjamin G. Cancelmo, Jr.">64 F.3d 804, 807</a></span> (2d Cir.1995) (recognizing that “the question of whether probable cause existed in the instant case is a close one”).</p>
<p id="b1225-13">Here, the facts present a vivid illustration of the principle that reasonable minds may <page-number citation-index="1" label="1168">*1168</page-number>differ on the issue of whether an affidavit sets forth sufficient information to comprise probable cause. A magistrate issued the warrant; that same judicial officer then concluded upon further review that the affidavit was insufficient.</p>
<p id="b1226-4"><em>We </em>now address our certiorari questions in inverse order, beginning with the question of whether the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule protects the search at issue in this case.</p>
<p id="b1226-5">III.</p>
<p id="b1226-6">A.</p>
<p id="b1226-7">The exclusionary rule “is a judicially created remedy designed to safeguard Fourth Amendment rights generally through its ■ deterrent effect, rather than a personal constitutional right of the party aggrieved.” <em>Leon, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#907" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. at 907</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span> (quoting <em>United States v. Calandra, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9425486"><a href="/opinion/108898/united-states-v-calandra/#348" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Calandra">414 U.S. 338, 348</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9425486"><a href="/opinion/108898/united-states-v-calandra/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Calandra">94 S.Ct. 613</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9425486"><a href="/opinion/108898/united-states-v-calandra/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Calandra">38 L.Ed.2d 561</a></span> (1974)); <em>see also People v. Deitchman, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9546177"><a href="/opinion/1169333/people-v-deitchman/#1160" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Deitchman">695 P.2d 1146, 1160</a></span> (Colo.1985) (Dubofsky, J., concurring). It is police officers, not judges and magistrates, whose conduct we seek to influence through application of the exclusionary rule. <em>See Leon </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#917" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. at 917</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span> (“Imposition of the exclusionary sanction is not necessary meaningfully to inform judicial officers of their errors, and we cannot conclude that admitting evidence obtained pursuant to a warrant while at the same time declaring that the warrant was somehow defective "will in any way reduce judicial officers’ professional incentives to comply with the Fourth Amendment.”).</p>
<p id="b1226-8">Thus, the exclusionary rule is intended to deter improper police conduct and should not be applied in eases where the “deterrence purpose is not served, or where the benefits associated with the rule are minimal in comparison to the costs associated with the exclusion of probative evidence.” <em>Deitchman, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9546177"><a href="/opinion/1169333/people-v-deitchman/#1160" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Deitchman">695 P.2d at 1160</a></span> (Dubofsky, J., concurring); <em>United States v. Chaar, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9490991"><a href="/opinion/751791/united-states-v-oussama-mohamed-chaar/#361" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Oussama Mohamed Chaar">137 F.3d 359, 361</a></span> (6th Cir.1998) (noting that the exclusionary rule is intended to deter police misconduct not to punish mistakes of judges and magistrates); <em>United States v. McKneely, </em><span class="citation" data-id="654640"><a href="/opinion/654640/united-states-v-dracy-lamont-mckneely-andrew-ellis-and-alandis-bennett/#1454" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Dracy Lamont McKneely Andrew Ellis, and...">6 F.3d 1447, 1454</a></span> (10th Cir.1993) (quoting <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span> </em>that “penalizing the officer for the magistrate’s error, rather than his own, cannot logically contribute to the deterrence - of Fourth Amendment violations”).</p>
<p id="b1226-10">B.</p>
<p id="b1226-11">Based on this rationale, Colorado created a statutory good faith exception to the exclusionary rule, codified at section 16-3-308, 6 C.R.S. (1997). It provides in pertinent part:</p>
<blockquote id="b1226-13">(2) As used in this subsection (1) of this section:</blockquote>
<blockquote id="Aql">(a) “Good faith mistake” means a reasonable judgmental error concerning the existence of facts or law which if true would be sufficient to constitute probable cause.</blockquote>
<blockquote id="Aa6r">[[Image here]]</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b1226-15">(4)(a) It is hereby declared to be the public policy of the state of Colorado that, when evidence is sought to be excluded from the trier of fact in a criminal proceeding because of the conduct of a peace officer leading to its discovery, it will be open, to the proponent of the evidence to urge that the conduct in question was taken in a reasonable, good faith belief that it was proper, and in such instances the evidence so discovered should not be kept from the trier of fact if otherwise admissible. ...</blockquote>
<blockquote id="b1226-16">(b) It shall be prima facie evidence that the conduct of the peace officer was performed in the reasonable good faith belief that it was proper if there is a showing that the evidence was obtained pursuant to and within the scope of a warrant....</blockquote>
<p id="b1226-17">§ 16-3-308, 6 C.R.S. (1997).</p>
<p id="b1226-18">Our General Assembly enacted the statutory good faith exception following the Fifth Circuit’s formal recognition of such an exception in <em>United States v. Williams, </em><span class="citation" data-id="378896"><a href="/opinion/378896/united-states-v-jo-ann-williams/#846" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Jo Ann Williams">622 F.2d 830, 846-47</a></span> (5th Cir.1980), <em>cert. denied, </em><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/U.S./449/1127/">449 U.S. 1127</a></span>, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/S.Ct./101/946/">101 S.Ct. 946</a></span>, <span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/L.Ed.2d/67/114/">67 L.Ed.2d 114</a></span> (1981), but prior to the United States Supreme Court’s decision in <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span>.</em></p>
<p id="b1226-19">The statute creates a presumption that an officer was acting in good faith if he or she was acting pursuant to a warrant. However, the ultimate question under the statute must <page-number citation-index="1" label="1169">*1169</page-number>still be whether the officer undertook the search in the reasonable, good faith belief that it was proper. Using somewhat different language, in <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span> </em>the Supreme Court focused on whether the officer’s rebanee on the warrant was objectively reasonable. <em>See Leon, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#919" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. at 919</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span> (stating that the exclusionary rule “cannot be expected, and should not be applied, to deter objectively reasonable law enforcement activity”).</p>
<p id="b1227-4">We have concluded that the <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span> </em>standard and the statutory standard are “substantially similar.” <em>Leftwich, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/#1272" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">869 P.2d at 1272</a></span>. Hence, under either test, our inquiry must be whether it was objectively reasonable for the officer to rely upon the warrant. If no reasonable officer would have rebed upon the warrant, then objective good faith is absent and the good faith exception offers no shelter.</p>
<p id="b1227-5">Specifically, in <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span>, </em>the Court listed four circumstances in which an officer’s reliance on a warrant would not be objectively reasonable: (1) where the issuing magistrate or judge was misled by a knowing or recklessly made falsehood; (2) where the issuing magistrate wholly abandoned his or her judicial role; (3) where the warrant is so faciaby deficient that the officers cannot reasonably determine the particular place to be searched or things to be seized; and (4) where the warrant is based on an affidavit “so lacking in indicia of probable cause as to render official belief in its existence entirfely unreasonable.” <em>Leon, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#923" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. at 923</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span> (quoting <em>Brown v. Illinois, </em>422 U.S. at 610-611, <span class="citation" data-id="9426178"><a href="/opinion/109304/brown-v-illinois/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brown v. Illinois">95 S.Ct. 2254</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9426178"><a href="/opinion/109304/brown-v-illinois/" aria-description="Citation for case: Brown v. Illinois">45 L.Ed.2d 416</a></span> (1975)).</p>
<p id="b1227-6">It is this latter circumstance, also referred to as a “bare bones” affidavit, which is at issue in this ease. The state claims that because the search was conducted pursuant to a warrant, good faith is presumed and the evidence against Altman is admissible.<footnotemark>3</footnotemark> Moreover, the state contends that Landolt’s affidavit was not a bare bones affidavit and thus the officers’ reliance on the warrant was objectively reasonable.</p>
<p id="b1227-10">The question then is whether Landolt’s affidavit was so deficient that no reasonable officer could have relied upon it as supporting a valid warrant. It is important to note at the outset that we are presuming an invalid warrant. Obviously, the good faith exception is not called into play when the warrant is found to be valid. The Court in <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span> </em>concluded that there is a middle ground between an affidavit setting forth probable cause and a bare bones affidavit: the middle ground where a police officer could have reasonably relied upon the validity of the warrant, because the affidavit was not “so lacking ... as to render official belief in its existence entirely unreasonable.” <em>Leon, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#923" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. at 923</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span>; <em>see also Leftwich, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/#1268" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">869 P.2d at 1268</a></span> (determining that an affidavit was lacking in probable cause and then examining whether it might nonetheless merit the good faith exception); <em>United States v. Savoca, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9473417"><a href="/opinion/451731/united-states-v-thomas-james-savoca/#294" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Thomas James Savoca">761 F.2d 292, 294-98</a></span> (6th Cir.1985) (finding no probable cause in the affidavit supporting a warrant but applying the good faith exception because the affidavit was not “so lacking in indicia of probable cause”).</p>
<p id="b1227-11">In this middle ground, an officer’s reliance upon the warrant is protected, even if the warrant is later overturned. The officer’s good faith in executing a signed warrant <page-number citation-index="1" label="1170">*1170</page-number>can only be impugned, for purposes of our analysis, by a showing that.it would have been “entirely unreasonable” for the .officer to have believed that the affidavit supported issuance of a warrant. <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span> </em>directs that an affidavit can indeed support; the good faith exception even if it is later determined to be insufficient, provided that it is not so deficient. that no reasonable officer could have relied upon it.<footnotemark>4</footnotemark> See 1 Wayne R. LaFave, <em>Search and Seizure, </em>§ 1.3(f), at 86 (3d ed. 1996) (averring that “súrély the Court would not have gone to the bother of adopting this new and controversial doctrine if it merely gave courts an alternative basis for reaching a conclusion already available to them under <em>[Illinois v. Gates, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429232"><a href="/opinion/110959/illinois-v-gates/" aria-description="Citation for case: Illinois v. Gates">462 U.S. 213</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429232"><a href="/opinion/110959/illinois-v-gates/" aria-description="Citation for case: Illinois v. Gates">103 S.Ct. 2317</a></span> (1983) ]”).</p>
<p id="b1228-4">Police officers- are not appellate judges. The determination by an appellate court that a warrant is invalid does not mean a police officer’s reliance upon that warrant was objectively unreasonable. The courts encourage officers to obtain warrants before invading individual privacy, and it is police misconduct that the exclusionary rule seeks to deter. Hence, both by operation of precedent and common sense, a warrant that, has failed appellate scrutiny can nonetheless form the basis for good faith execution by a reasonable police officer. Only if no reasonable officer would have relied upon that warrant should we defeat that officer’s good faith and hence the good faith exception.</p>
<p id="b1228-5">C.</p>
<p id="b1228-6">We now turn to an examination of Landolt’s affidavit to determine whether it was, in fact, a bare bones affidavit that would render the good faith exception inapplicable. As we have previously stated, this test considers:</p>
<blockquote id="b1228-7">whether a reasonably well-trained officer in [the officer’s] position would have known that his affidavit failed to establish probable cause and that he should not have applied for the warrant. If such was the case, the officer’s application for a warrant was not objectively reasonable, because it created the unnecessary danger of an unlawful arrest. It is true that in an ideal system an unreasonable request for a warrant would be harmless, because no judge would approve it. But ours is not an ideal system, and it is possible that a magistrate, working under docket pressure, will fail to perform as a magistrate should. We find it reasonable to require the officer applying for the warrant to minimize this danger by exercising reasonable professional judgment.</blockquote>
<p id="b1228-10"><em>Leftwich, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">869 P.2d at 1269</a></span> (quoting <em>Malley v. Briggs, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9430379"><a href="/opinion/111611/malley-v-briggs/#345" aria-description="Citation for case: Malley v. Briggs">475 U.S. 335, 345-46</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9430379"><a href="/opinion/111611/malley-v-briggs/" aria-description="Citation for case: Malley v. Briggs">106 S.Ct. 1092</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9430379"><a href="/opinion/111611/malley-v-briggs/" aria-description="Citation for case: Malley v. Briggs">89 L.Ed.2d 271</a></span> (1986)).</p>
<p id="b1228-11">A bare bones affidavit is said to contain wholly conelusory statements devoid of facts from which a magistrate can independently determine probable cause. <em>See, e.g., United States v. Laury, </em><span class="citation" data-id="600365"><a href="/opinion/600365/united-states-v-felmon-lakeith-laury-aka-felmon-keith-ashley-aka/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Felmon Lakeith Laury, A/K/A Felmon Keith...">985 F.2d 1293</a></span>, 1311 n. 23 (5th Cir.1993); <em>United States v. Medlin, </em><span class="citation" data-id="474635"><a href="/opinion/474635/united-states-v-arvle-edgar-medlin/#409" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Arvle Edgar Medlin">798 F.2d 407, 409</a></span> (10th Cir.1986). In <em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">Leftwich</a></span>, </em>this court rejected application of the good faith exception because the underlying affidavit was bare bones in nature. <em>Leftwich, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/#1270" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">869 P.2d at 1270</a></span>. <em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">Leftwich</a></span> </em>concerned an unidentified informant who made- only vague allegations of both criminal and noncriminal activity. An anonymous letter sent to police alleged that a person named “Jeff’ of a certain physical description was a drug dealer. The letter described his car and stated that he was a student in Boulder. The only specifics regarding criminal activity were that drugs were “collected at a music store located in Kansas City just North of the intersection of 39th and Main” and then taken to Boulder. <span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/#1264" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich"><em>Id. </em>at 1264</a></span>. The police officer was only able to corroborate innocuous, easily obtainable facts reported by the unknown informant. We also noted that the verified facts alone were not the least suspicious. More importantly, and of paramount concern to this court in <em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">Leftwich</a></span>, </em>was the complete lack of a nexus between the facts as reported in the affidavit and the defendant’s <page-number citation-index="1" label="1171">*1171</page-number>residence. We noted that “[n]owhere in the letter does the informant indicate that' Left-wieh’s residence is the site of alleged drug activity.” <span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/#1267" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich"><em>Id. </em>at 1267</a></span>. We emphasized the lack of “a substantial basis to determine if probable cause existed <em>to search Leftwich’s residence.” Id. </em>at 1268 (emphasis in original). Thus, we concluded that the affidavit contained wholly conclusory allegations of an unsubstantiated informant and a reasonable officer should have known that probable cause was lacking to search Leftwich’s residence.</p>
<p id="b1229-5">Here, by contrast, the affidavit does not rest upon unsupported allegations of criminal activity made by an unknown informant. Indeed, all facts recited in the affidavit were observed by law enforcement officers. There is also no problem connecting the evidence to the place to be searched. The DEA agents observed firsthand the transport of the hydroponic equipment in a rental ear to 608 Maple Street. Landolt cheeked the utility records for evidence of excessive electrical usage and also checked city records for a legitimate business purpose.</p>
<p id="b1229-6">In <em><span class="citation" data-id="9571780"><a href="/opinion/1229937/people-v-leftwich/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Leftwich">Leftwich</a></span> </em>driving to Kansas City during spring break was not a suspicious activity absent a reliable informant’s allegation attaching significance to this fact. Here hydroponic growing equipment and excessive electrical usage can be inherently suspicious activities known to indicate indoor marijuana cultivation. <em>See, e.g., Turcotte-Schaeffer, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2604322"><a href="/opinion/2604322/people-v-turcotte-schaeffer/#660" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Turcotte-Schaeffer">843 P.2d at 660</a></span> (noting that district court suppressed evidence because “[t]here is nothing in this case that supports any hint that [defendants were] involved in unlawful activity [such as, for example] surges of electricity”); <span class="citation" data-id="9536458"><a href="/opinion/1138456/people-v-quintana/#939" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Quintana"><em>Quintana, 785 </em>P.2d at 939-40</a></span> (finding that excessive electricity usage partially supports affidavit for search warrant); <em>People v. Dunkin, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1347370"><a href="/opinion/1347370/people-v-dunkin/#309" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Dunkin">888 P.2d 305, 309</a></span> (Colo.App.1994) (holding that daily one-hour visits to house with no apparent residents, covered windows, high electrical consumption and officer’s opinion supported probable cause); <em>People v. Beckstrom, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9789762"><a href="/opinion/2604298/people-v-beckstrom/#35" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Beckstrom">843 P.2d 34, 35</a></span> (Colo.App.1992) (stating that defendant’s purchase of hydroponic equipment prompted DEA officers to seek consent to search for indoor marijuana cultivation); <em>People v. Wilson, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1453271"><a href="/opinion/1453271/people-v-wilson/#515" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Wilson">819 P.2d 510, 515</a></span> (Colo.App.1991) (holding that high electrical consumption, frequent visits to and from office, condensation on. covered windows and officer’s opinion constituted probable cause); <em>United States v. Robinson, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9488415"><a href="/opinion/701846/united-states-v-theodore-robinson-sr/#1331" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Theodore Robinson, Sr.">62 F.3d 1325, 1331</a></span> (11th Cir.1995) (finding probable cause to search based upon purchase of hydroponic growing equipment and high pressure lights with cashier’s cheek, excessive use of electricity and fact that defendant lived in expensive home yet did not pay state income taxes); <em>People v. Miller, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2461279"><a href="/opinion/2461279/state-v-miller/#30" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Miller">815 S.W.2d 28, 30</a></span> (Mo.Ct.App.1991) (holding that DEA tip concerning defendant’s purchase of hydroponic equipment, excessive electrical usage, large dogs on premises, non-drug related felony convictions and past success with similar DEA tips supported probable.cause).</p>
<p id="b1229-8">The fact that the affidavit details activities that are lawful does not cause it to be a bare bones affidavit. A combination of otherwise lawful circumstances may well lead to a legitimate inference of criminal activity. <em>See, e.g., Gates, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429232"><a href="/opinion/110959/illinois-v-gates/" aria-description="Citation for case: Illinois v. Gates">462 U.S. at 243</a></span> n. 13, <span class="citation" data-id="9429232"><a href="/opinion/110959/illinois-v-gates/" aria-description="Citation for case: Illinois v. Gates">103 S.Ct. 2317</a></span> (“[I]nnocerit behavior frequently will provide the basis for a showing of probable cáuse; to require otherwise would be to <em>sub silentio </em>impose a drastically more rigorous definition of probable cause than the security of our citizens demands.”); <em>United States v. Muniz-Melchor, 894 F.2d </em>1430, 1438 (5th Cir.1990) (“A succession of otherwise ‘innocent’ circumstances or events ... may constitute probable cause when viewed as a whole.”).</p>
<p id="b1229-9">Of course, these same activities might also be ‘ consistent with innocent behavior, but probable cause deals with probabilities, not certainties. Here, the inference of criminal activity is “stronger than a mere uninformed and unconfirmed guess.” <em>Massachusetts v. Upton, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429595"><a href="/opinion/111172/massachusetts-v-upton/#734" aria-description="Citation for case: Massachusetts v. Upton">466 U.S. 727, 734</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429595"><a href="/opinion/111172/massachusetts-v-upton/" aria-description="Citation for case: Massachusetts v. Upton">104 S.Ct. 2085</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429595"><a href="/opinion/111172/massachusetts-v-upton/" aria-description="Citation for case: Massachusetts v. Upton">80 L.Ed.2d 721</a></span> (1984) (“It is enough that the [officer’s] inference was a reasonable one and conformed with the other piece's of evidence making up the total showing of probable cause.”). In a quite similar case, the Missouri Court of Appeals found that the purchase of gardening equipment, high electrical usage, ownership of large dogs and past non-drug related felony convictions supported probable cause to search for an indoor mari<page-number citation-index="1" label="1172">*1172</page-number>juana cultivation. <em>See Miller, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2461279"><a href="/opinion/2461279/state-v-miller/#32" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Miller">815 S.W.2d at 32</a></span>. That court rejected a similar argument regarding innocent explanations:</p>
<blockquote id="b1230-4">Appellants’ argument that there are equally plausible, noncriminal explanations for-appellants’ purchase of gardening equipment, high utility usage, and ownership of dogs mischaracterizes the State’s burden of proof in obtaining a search warrant. The State need not prove that marihuana was in fact being grown in appellants’ home. Only the probability of criminal activity not a prima facie showing, is the standard of probable cause.</blockquote>
<p id="b1230-5"><em>Miller, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2461279"><a href="/opinion/2461279/state-v-miller/#32" aria-description="Citation for case: State v. Miller">815 S.W.2d at 32</a></span>. <em>See also Turcotte-Schaeffer, </em><span class="citation" data-id="2604322"><a href="/opinion/2604322/people-v-turcotte-schaeffer/#660" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Turcotte-Schaeffer">843 P.2d at 660-61</a></span> (“In making a determination of probable cause the relevant inquiry is not whether particular conduct is ‘innocent’ or ‘guilty’ but the degree of suspicion that attaches to particular types of noncriminal acts.”); <em>Alley, </em><span class="citation" data-id="1204600"><a href="/opinion/1204600/people-v-atley/#377" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Atley">727 P.2d at 377</a></span> (noting that informant’s observations were also consistent with lawful mushroom growing, but added fact that defendant misrepresented to landlord that she lived at residence supported probable cause); <em>United States v. Fama, </em><span class="citation" data-id="450379"><a href="/opinion/450379/united-states-v-barbara-fama/#838" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Barbara Fama">758 F.2d 834, 838</a></span> (2d Cir.1985) (“The fact that an innocent explanation may be consistent with the facts, alleged ... does not negate probable cause.”).</p>
<p id="AHqv">We must also give credence to Officer Landolt’s experience and training in evaluating the significance of the facts reported in his affidavit relative to probable cause. <em>See, e.g., Henderson v. People, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9541194"><a href="/opinion/1159015/henderson-v-people/#392" aria-description="Citation for case: Henderson v. People">879 P.2d 383, 392</a></span> (Colo.1994); <em>Cancelmo, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9488470"><a href="/opinion/703163/united-states-v-benjamin-g-cancelmo-jr/#807" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Benjamin G. Cancelmo, Jr.">64 F.3d at 807</a></span>; <em>Robinson, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9488415"><a href="/opinion/701846/united-states-v-theodore-robinson-sr/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Theodore Robinson, Sr.">62 F.3d at 1331</a></span> n. 9. In <em><span class="citation" data-id="9488470"><a href="/opinion/703163/united-states-v-benjamin-g-cancelmo-jr/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Benjamin G. Cancelmo, Jr.">Cancelmo</a></span>, </em>the affidavit supporting a search warrant reported only cryptic phone conversations between the defendant and a known narcotics dealer. <em>See Cancelmo, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9488470"><a href="/opinion/703163/united-states-v-benjamin-g-cancelmo-jr/#806" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Benjamin G. Cancelmo, Jr.">64 F.3d at 806-07</a></span>, Nothing in these conversations would indicate to a lay person that drugs were being discussed.., However, the officer asserted that the conversations regarding “chimneys” and “decks” were actually coded discussions about drug deals. <em>See <span class="citation" data-id="9488470"><a href="/opinion/703163/united-states-v-benjamin-g-cancelmo-jr/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Benjamin G. Cancelmo, Jr.">id.</a></span> </em>The court found that probable cause existed even though, as the dissent pointed out, the defendant was in the construction business. <em>See <span class="citation" data-id="9488470"><a href="/opinion/703163/united-states-v-benjamin-g-cancelmo-jr/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Benjamin G. Cancelmo, Jr.">id.</a></span></em></p>
<p id="b1230-8">Giving credence to Landolt’s experience and training and considering all of the facts indicating possible criminal activity, we find his reliance upon the affidavit to be reasonable. The principles and rationale behind the exclusionary rule do not support excluding evidence in a case where law enforcement officers act in reasonable and good faith reliance upon a warrant signed by a magistrate. <em>See Leon, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#921" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. at 921</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span> (“Once the warrant issues, there is literally nothing more the policeman can. do in seeking to comply with the law.”); <em>United States v. Murphy, </em><span class="citation" data-id="706960"><a href="/opinion/706960/united-states-v-michael-d-murphy/#241" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Michael D. Murphy">69 F.3d 237, 241</a></span> (8th Cir.1995) (stating that “[ojrdinarily a police officer cannot be expected to question a judge’s probable cause determination”); <em>United States v. Johnson, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9486670"><a href="/opinion/668574/united-states-v-lee-erwin-johnson/#681" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Lee Erwin Johnson">22 F.3d 674, 681</a></span> (6th Cir.1994) (holding that this was not “one of those rare cases in which an officer should have known that the search was illegal despite the magistrate’s authorization”); <em>United States v. Cardall, </em><span class="citation" data-id="8935259"><a href="/opinion/8944701/united-states-v-cardall/#1133" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Cardall">773 F.2d 1128, 1133</a></span> (10th Cir.1985) (noting <em>Leon’s </em>mandate that evidence be suppressed “only in those unusual cases in which exclusion will further the purposes of the exclusionary rule”).</p>
<p id="b1230-10">Here, Officer Landolt’s experience and training led him to believe that the purchase of equipment commonly associated with indoor, marijuana cultivation at the height of the outdoor growing season, the high electrical usage, the potentially suspicious use of a rental car, and the defendant’s previous brushes with the law all created a reasonable inference of marijuana cultivation. We do not view this as an affidavit so devoid of legitimate basis that no reasonable officer could have relied upon it as supporting a valid warrant.. Rather,' reliance was objectively reasonable, thereby confirming application of the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule.</p>
<p id="b1230-11">IV.</p>
<p id="b1230-12">In our analysis, we have proceeded on the assumption that the affidavit was lacking in probable cause, and have concluded that nonetheless the good faith exception permits evidence obtained.from the search to be admissible. Hence, -we need not proceed beyond that assumption to a specific determination of whether probable cause existed. <em>See Leon, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/#925" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">468 U.S. at 925</a></span>, <span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">104 S.Ct. 3405</a></span> <page-number citation-index="1" label="1173">*1173</page-number>(“[C]ourts could reject suppression motions posing no important Fourth Amendment questions by turning immediately to a consideration of the officers’ good faith.”); <em>Chaar, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9490991"><a href="/opinion/751791/united-states-v-oussama-mohamed-chaar/#363" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Oussama Mohamed Chaar">137 F.3d at 363</a></span> (“We need not engage in [a probable cause analysis] because there is a simpler and relatively non-speculative reason to conclude that this search was constitutional: the‘good-faith <em>exception; Cancelmo, </em><span class="citation" data-id="9488470"><a href="/opinion/703163/united-states-v-benjamin-g-cancelmo-jr/#807" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Benjamin G. Cancelmo, Jr.">64 F.3d at 807</a></span> (“[W]e need not resolve the issue [of probable cause] because we conclude that the good faith exception applies.”); <em>McKneely, </em><span class="citation" data-id="654640"><a href="/opinion/654640/united-states-v-dracy-lamont-mckneely-andrew-ellis-and-alandis-bennett/#1453" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Dracy Lamont McKneely Andrew Ellis, and...">6 F.3d at 1453</a></span> (“We hold under the circumstances of this ease, however, that [the existence of probable cause] is insignificant because the district court’s finding on probable cause is trumped by the good faith exception....”); <em>United States v. Craig, </em><span class="citation" data-id="514595"><a href="/opinion/514595/united-states-v-bruce-l-craig/#820" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Bruce L. Craig">861 F.2d 818, 820</a></span> (5th Cir.1988) (“Principles of judicial restraint and precedent dictate that, in most cases, we should not reach the probable cause issue if a decision on the admissibility of the evidence under the good-faith exception of <em><span class="citation" data-id="9429766"><a href="/opinion/111262/united-states-v-leon/" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Leon">Leon</a></span> </em>will resolve the matter.”).</p>
<p id="b1231-5">In resolving this ease on the basis of the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule, we address all matters genuinely at issue,<footnotemark>5</footnotemark> and decline to reach the probable cause question.</p>
<p id="b1231-6">V.</p>
<p id="b1231-7">We conclude that the good faith exception to the exclusionary rule protects the officer’s search pursuant to warrant. The affidavit was sufficient to support reasonable reliance. Hence, we reverse the court of appeals and remand with directions to reinstate the district court’s order.</p>
