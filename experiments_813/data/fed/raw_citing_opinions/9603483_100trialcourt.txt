8                               ANALYSIS                                  
9   A.   Legal Standard Applicable to Motions to Compel Arbitration.      
10        Pursuant to the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), arbitration agreements “shall be valid, 
11   irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds that exist at law or in equity for the 
12   revocation of any contract.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">9 U.S.C. § 2</span><pre class="inline">.  Once the Court has determined that an arbitration 
13   agreement involves a transaction involving interstate commerce, thereby falling under the FAA, 
14   the Court’s only role is to determine whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and whether the 
15   scope of the parties’ dispute falls within that agreement.  United Computer Systems v. AT&amp;T 
16   Corp., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/298/756/">298 F.3d 756</a></span><pre class="inline">, 766 (9th Cir. 2002); Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Sys., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="198855"><a href="/opinion/198855/united-states-v-dumas/#1126" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Dumas">207 F.3d 
17   1126, 1130</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2000); </pre><span class="citation no-link">9 U.S.C. § 4</span><pre class="inline">.                             
18        The FAA represents the “liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements” and “any 
19   doubts concerning the scope of arbitrable issues should be resolved in favor of arbitration.”  Moses 
20   H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Constr. Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9429079"><a href="/opinion/110873/moses-h-cone-memorial-hospital-v-mercury-construction-corp/#24" aria-description="Citation for case: Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction...">460 U.S. 1, 24-25</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1983).  Under the FAA, 
21   “once [the Court] is satisfied that an agreement for arbitration has been made and has not been 
22   honored,” and the dispute falls within the scope of that agreement, the Court must order 
23   arbitration.  Prima Paint Corp. v. Flood &amp; Conklin Mfg. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9423492"><a href="/opinion/107492/prima-paint-corp-v-flood-conklin-mfg-co/#400" aria-description="Citation for case: Prima Paint Corp. v. Flood &amp; Conklin Mfg. Co.">388 U.S. 395, 400</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1967).   
24        Notwithstanding the liberal policy favoring arbitration, by entering into an arbitration 
25   agreement, two parties enter into a contract.  Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees 
26   of Leland Stanford Junior University, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9431597"><a href="/opinion/112212/volt-info-sciences-inc-v-bd-of-trustees-of-leland-stanford-jr-u/#479" aria-description="Citation for case: Volt Info. Sciences, Inc. v. Bd. of Trustees of Leland...">489 U.S. 468, 479</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1989) (noting that arbitration “is a 
27   matter of consent, not coercion.”).  The principles of state contract law are applied in determining 
1   944 (1995); Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="776478"><a href="/opinion/776478/circuit-city-stores-inc-a-virginia-corporation-v-saint-clair-adams-a/#892" aria-description="Citation for case: Circuit City Stores, Inc. A Virginia Corporation v. Saint...">279 F.3d 889, 892</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2002).  A party seeking 
2   to compel arbitration must prove by a preponderance of the evidence the existence of an 
3   arbitration agreement, and a party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving by a 
4   preponderance of evidence any fact necessary to its defense.  Olvera v. El Pollo Loco, Inc., 173

14   B.   Coinbase’s Motion to Compel.                                     
15        Here, the parties do not dispute that: (1) Plaintiffs agreed to Coinbase’s User Agreement; 
16   (2) Coinbase’s User Agreement contains a valid arbitration agreement; and (3) Plaintiffs 
17   subsequently agreed to the Dogecoin sweepstakes’ Official Rules; and (4) the Dogecoin 
18   sweepstakes’ Official Rules provides that California courts have exclusive jurisdiction over any 
19   controversies regarding the sweepstakes.  Plaintiffs also do not dispute that their claims would fall 
20   within the scope of Coinbase’s User Agreement arbitration provision, had they not agreed to the 
21   subsequent exclusive jurisdiction provision in the Dogecoin sweepstakes’ Official Rules.  The 
22   issues are thus which contract (Coinbase’s User Agreement or the Dogecoin sweepstakes’ Official 
23   Rules) governs this dispute and who decides which contract applies (this Court or the arbitrator).   
24        1.   Who Decides Which Contract Governs.                         
25        Whether the Court or the arbitrator determine which contract applies “is an issue for 
26   judicial determination unless the parties clearly and unmistakably provide otherwise.”  Goldman, 
27   Sachs &amp; Co. v. City of Reno, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9799195"><a href="/opinion/2658913/goldman-sachs-co-v-city-of-reno/#738" aria-description="Citation for case: Goldman, Sachs &amp; Co. v. City of Reno">747 F.3d 733, 738</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014) (emphasis in original) (quoting 
1   is a presumption that courts will decide which issues are arbitrable.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9799195"><a href="/opinion/2658913/goldman-sachs-co-v-city-of-reno/" aria-description="Citation for case: Goldman, Sachs &amp; Co. v. City of Reno">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Coinbase argues that the 
2   arbitration provisions in the Coinbase User Agreements clearly delegate the issue of arbitrability 
3   to the arbitrator.  Three of the four Plaintiffs agreed to the arbitration provision in the Coinbase 
4   User Agreement, which provides:                                       
5             This Arbitration Agreement includes, without limitation, disputes 
              arising out of or related to the interpretation or application of the 
6             Arbitration  Agreement,  including  the  enforceability,  revocability, 
              scope, or validity of the Arbitration Agreement or any portion of the 
7             Arbitration Agreement.  All  such matters shall be decided by  an 
              arbitrator and not by a court or judge.                     
8                                                                         
    (Dkt. Nos. 33-8, 33-9, 33-10 (Exhibits 7, 8, 9 to the Declaration of McPherson-Evans) (emphasis 
9                                                                         
    omitted).)  For Suski, the User Agreement explicitly incorporated and adopted the American 
10                                                                         
    Arbitration Association’s (“AAA”) Consumer Arbitration Rules (and included a link to the text of 
11                                                                         
    those rules) to govern any dispute between Coinbase and the user.  (Dkt. No. 33-7 (Ex. 6 to the 
12                                                                         
    McPherson-Evans Decl.).)  Rule 14(a) of the AAA Rules (titled “Jurisdiction”) states that the 
13                                                                         
    “arbitrator shall have the power to rule on his or her own jurisdiction, including any objections 
14                                                                         
    with respect to the existence, scope, or validity of the arbitration agreement or to the arbitrability 
15                                                                         
    of any claim or counterclaim.”  See AAA Consumer Arbitration Rules,   
16                                                                         
    https://www.adr.org/sites/default/files/Consumer%20Rules.pdf (effective September 1, 2014).    
17                                                                         
         While disagreements over the scope of the arbitration provisions were delegated to the 
18                                                                         
    arbitrator, the dispute here is not over the scope of the arbitration provision, but rather whether the 
19                                                                         
    agreement was superseded by another separate contract.  In other words, Plaintiffs do not dispute 
20                                                                         
    that their claims would fall within the scope of the arbitration provision if they had not agreed to 
21                                                                         
    the Official Rules of the Dogecoin sweepstakes.  Moreover, because Plaintiffs agreed to a 
22                                                                         
    subsequent agreement with an exclusive jurisdiction provision, the dispute over how to address the 
23                                                                         
    interaction between two separate contracts is not clearly and unmistakably delegated in the 
24                                                                         
    arbitration provision to the arbitrator.  Or, as another district court explained, the required “clear 
25                                                                         
    and unmistakable evidence of intent to arbitrate arbitrability does not exist where an arbitration 
26                                                                         
    provision has been excluded from superseding agreements.”  Ingram Micro Inc. v. Signeo Int’l, 
27                                                                         
    Ltd., </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 3721197</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (C.D. Cal. July 22, 2014).  In light of the presumption that the 
1   Court address this issue, the Court will determine which contract applies.

3        2.   Which Contract Governs.                                     
4        “[A]rbitration is a matter of contract,” Rent-A-Center, W., Inc. v. Jackson, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/U.S./561/63/">561 U.S. 63</a></span><pre class="inline">, 67 
5   (2010).  “Where the arbitrability of a dispute is contested, we must decide whether the parties are 
6   contesting the existence or the scope of an arbitration agreement.  If the parties contest the 
7   existence of an arbitration agreement, the presumption in favor of arbitrability does not apply.”  
8   Goldman, Sachs &amp; Co. v. City of Reno, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9799195"><a href="/opinion/2658913/goldman-sachs-co-v-city-of-reno/#742" aria-description="Citation for case: Goldman, Sachs &amp; Co. v. City of Reno">747 F.3d 733, 742</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014) (emphasis in original).  
9   When determining whether parties have agreed to submit to arbitration, courts apply general state-
10   law principles of contract interpretation.  Mundi v. Union Sec. Life Ins. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208907"><a href="/opinion/1208907/mundi-v-union-security-life-insurance/" aria-description="Citation for case: Mundi v. Union Security Life Insurance">555 F.3d 1042</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1044 
11   (9th Cir. 2009).                                                      
12        Here, after agreeing to the Coinbase User Agreement with the arbitration provision, 
13   Plaintiffs agreed to the Official Rules for the Dogecoin sweepstakes, which contains an exclusive 
14   forum selection clause designating California courts for all disputes regarding the sweepstakes.  
15   The arbitration clause and the forum selection provision in the two contracts are conflicting.  As in 
16   Applied Energetics, Inc. v. NewOak Cap. Markets, LLC, the language in the sweepstakes Official 
17   Terms “that ‘[a]ny dispute’ between the parties ‘shall be adjudicated’ by specified courts stands in 
18   direct conflict with the [Coinbase User] Agreement’s parallel language that ‘any dispute . . . shall 
19   be resolved through binding arbitration.’  Both provisions are all-inclusive, both are mandatory, 
20   and neither admits the possibility of the other.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208907"><a href="/opinion/1208907/mundi-v-union-security-life-insurance/" aria-description="Citation for case: Mundi v. Union Security Life Insurance">Id.,</a></span><pre class="inline"> </pre><span class="citation" data-id="220856"><a href="/opinion/220856/applied-energetics-inc-v-newoak-capital-markets-llc/#525" aria-description="Citation for case: Applied Energetics, Inc. v. NewOak Capital Markets, LLC">645 F.3d 522, 525</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2d Cir. 2011) (finding the 
21   adjudication clause specifically precludes and, thus, supersedes the arbitration provision).  
22   Although Coinbase tries to reconcile the two, arguing that the sweepstakes Official Rules only 
23   applies to non-Coinbase users, there is no support in the contract language for this distinction.  
24   The Official Rules does not limit to whom it applies.  Instead, by its terms, it applies to all 
25   sweepstakes’ “entrants.”  (Dkt. No. 22-1, Ex. A, ¶¶ 1, 10.)           
26        Because the arbitration provision and the forum selection clause conflict, the subsequent 
27   contract supersedes the first.  Goldman, Sachs &amp; Co. v. City of Reno, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9799195"><a href="/opinion/2658913/goldman-sachs-co-v-city-of-reno/#742" aria-description="Citation for case: Goldman, Sachs &amp; Co. v. City of Reno">747 F.3d 733, 742-43</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th 
1   subsequent agreement); see also Applied Energetics, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="220856"><a href="/opinion/220856/applied-energetics-inc-v-newoak-capital-markets-llc/#525" aria-description="Citation for case: Applied Energetics, Inc. v. NewOak Capital Markets, LLC">645 F.3d at 525-26</a></span><pre class="inline"> (same); Capili v. Finish 
2   Line, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7232447"><a href="/opinion/7314542/capili-v-finish-line-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Capili v. Finish Line, Inc.">116 F. Supp. 3d 1000</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1004 n. 1 (N.D. Cal. 2015) (Under California law, “[t]he general 
3   rule is that when parties enter into a second contract dealing with the same subject matter as their 
4   first contract without stating whether the second contract operates to discharge or substitute for the 
5   first contract, the two contracts must be interpreted together and the latter contract prevails to the 
6   extent they are inconsistent.”) (quoting 17A C.J.S. Contracts § 574).   
7        Therefore, the Court DENIES Coinbase’s motion to compel arbitration and, thus, turns to 
8   the alternative motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim.       
9   C.   Applicable Legal Standard on Motion to Dismiss.                  
10        A motion to dismiss is proper under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) where the 
11   pleadings fail to state a claim upon which relief can be granted.  On a motion to dismiss under 
12   Rule 12(b)(6), the Court construes the allegations in the complaint in the light most favorable to 
13   the non-moving party and takes as true all material allegations in the complaint.  Sanders v. 
14   Kennedy, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8942325"><a href="/opinion/8951512/sanders-v-kennedy/#481" aria-description="Citation for case: Sanders v. Kennedy">794 F.2d 478, 481</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1986).  Even under the liberal pleading standard of Rule 
15   8(a)(2), “a plaintiff’s obligation to provide the ‘grounds’ of his ‘entitle[ment] to relief’ requires 
16   more than labels and conclusions, and a formulaic recitation of the elements of a cause of action 
17   will not do.”  Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#555" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 555</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007) (citing Papasan v. Allain, 
18   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9430664"><a href="/opinion/111742/papasan-v-allain/#286" aria-description="Citation for case: Papasan v. Allain">478 U.S. 265, 286</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1986)).  Rather, a plaintiff must instead allege “enough facts to state a claim to 
19   relief that is plausible on its face.”  Id. at 570.                   
20        “The plausibility standard is not akin to a probability requirement, but it asks for more than 
21   a sheer possibility that a defendant has acted unlawfully. . . . When a complaint pleads facts that 
    are merely consistent with a defendant’s liability, it stops short of the line between possibility and 
22                                                                         
    plausibility of entitlement to relief.”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting 
23                                                                         
    Twombly, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#557" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. at 557</a></span><pre class="inline">) (internal quotation marks omitted).  If the allegations are insufficient to 
24                                                                         
    state a claim, a court should grant leave to amend, unless amendment would be futile.  See, e.g. 
25                                                                         
    Reddy v. Litton Indus., Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8983777"><a href="/opinion/8991601/reddy-v-litton-industries-inc/#296" aria-description="Citation for case: Reddy v. Litton Industries, Inc.">912 F.2d 291, 296</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1990); Cook, Perkiss &amp; Lieche, Inc. v. N. 
26                                                                         
    Cal. Collection Serv., Inc., </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.2d/911/242/">911 F.2d 242</a></span><pre class="inline">, 246-47 (9th Cir. 1990).    
27                                                                         
         As a general rule, “a district court may not consider material beyond the pleadings in ruling 
1   on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.”  Branch v. Tunnell, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="660713"><a href="/opinion/660713/jerry-l-branch-valenna-branch-colby-branch-v-dale-l-tunnell/#453" aria-description="Citation for case: Jerry L. Branch, Valenna Branch, Colby Branch v. Dale L....">14 F.3d 449, 453</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1994), overruled on 
2   other grounds, Galbraith v. Cnty. of Santa Clara, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/307/1119/">307 F.3d 1119</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2002) (citation omitted).  
3   However, documents subject to judicial notice, such as matters of public record, may be 
4   considered on a motion to dismiss.  See Harris v. Cnty of Orange, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="801961"><a href="/opinion/801961/harris-v-county-of-orange/#1132" aria-description="Citation for case: Harris v. County of Orange">682 F.3d 1126, 1132</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
5   2011).  In doing so, the Court does not convert a motion to dismiss to one for summary judgment.  
6   See Mack v. S. Bay Beer Distrib., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="474911"><a href="/opinion/474911/henry-g-mack-v-south-bay-beer-distributors-inc-dba-bay-beer/#1282" aria-description="Citation for case: Henry G. Mack v. South Bay Beer Distributors, Inc., Dba...">798 F.2d 1279, 1282</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1986), overruled on other 
7   grounds by Astoria Fed. Sav. &amp; Loan Ass’n v. Solimino, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="112618"><a href="/opinion/112618/astoria-federal-savings-loan-assn-v-solimino/" aria-description="Citation for case: Astoria Federal Savings &amp; Loan Ass&#x27;n v. Solimino">501 U.S. 104</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1991).  “The court need 
8   not . . . accept as true allegations that contradict matters properly subject to judicial notice . . . .”  
9   Sprewell v. Golden State Warriors, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7006046"><a href="/opinion/7100340/sprewell-v-golden-state-warriors/#988" aria-description="Citation for case: Sprewell v. Golden State Warriors">266 F. 3d 979, 988</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001). 
10   D.   Coinbase’s Motion to Dismiss.                                    
11        1.   California Penal Code § 320.                                
12        Plaintiffs allege that the Dogecoin sweepstakes violates California Penal Code § 320.  
    Coinbase argues that the Dogecoin sweepstakes was not an illegal lottery under California law 
13                                                                         
    because it provided free alternative methods of entry.  As a result, Coinbase argues that Plaintiffs’ 
14                                                                         
    UCL claims, predicated on violation of the lottery law, fail as a matter of law.  
15                                                                         
         Lotteries are illegal under California law.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Penal Code § 320</span><pre class="inline">.  California law 
16                                                                         
    defines a lottery as:                                                 
17                                                                         
              any scheme for the disposal of property by chance, among persons 
18             who have paid or promised to pay any valuable consideration for the 
              chance  of  obtaining  such  property  .  .  .  upon  any  agreement, 
19             understanding or expectation that it is to be distributed or disposed of 
              by lot or chance.                                           
20                                                                         
    </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Pen. Code §319</span><pre class="inline">.  This statute is strictly construed.  Haskell v. Time, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2139267"><a href="/opinion/2139267/haskell-v-time-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Haskell v. Time, Inc.">965 F. Supp. 1398</a></span><pre class="inline">, 
21                                                                         
    1404 (E.D. Cal. 1997) (“A penal statute is strictly construed.”).  The essential elements of a lottery 
22                                                                         
    are chance, consideration, and the prize.  People v. Cardas, </pre><span class="citation no-link">137 Cal. App. Supp. 788</span><pre class="inline">, 790 (1933); 
23   Cal. Gasoline Retailers v. Regal Petroleum Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1126972"><a href="/opinion/1126972/california-gasoline-retailers-v-regal-petroleum-corp-of-fresno-inc/#851" aria-description="Citation for case: California Gasoline Retailers v. Regal Petroleum Corp. of...">50 Cal. 2d 844, 851</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1958).  If any one of the 
24   three elements is missing, the game or scheme at issue is not a lottery.  Haskell, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2139267"><a href="/opinion/2139267/haskell-v-time-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Haskell v. Time, Inc.">965 F. Supp. at 
25</a></span><pre class="inline">   1403.                                                                 
26        In Cardas, tickets for a promotional scheme were distributed with programs in the 
27   neighborhood of the theater, with two thousand distributed to passing motorists and others handed 
1   unnecessary to buy an admission ticket to secure a prize ticket or to claim the prize.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2139267"><a href="/opinion/2139267/haskell-v-time-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Haskell v. Time, Inc.">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  The court 
2   held there was no lottery because “those who purchased admission tickets and received price 
    tickets, . . ., could not be said to have paid a consideration for the prize tickets since they could 
3                                                                         
    have received them free.”  Id. at 791.  In People v. Carpenter, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1140316"><a href="/opinion/1140316/people-v-carpenter/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Carpenter">141 Cal. App. 2d 884</a></span><pre class="inline">, 889-90 
4                                                                         
    (1956), the court found that the movie theater’s contest was not a lottery because tickets were 
5                                                                         
    offered to customers and non-customers and no consideration was paid for the chance of winning.  
6                                                                         
    Anyone who wanted to participate could do so for free.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1140316"><a href="/opinion/1140316/people-v-carpenter/" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Carpenter">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  Similarly, in Regal, the participating 
7                                                                         
    gas stations did not conduct a lottery where they distributed tickets for free before and after 
8                                                                         
    purchases at the gas stations and elsewhere, including homes, drive-in theaters, and baseball 
9                                                                         
    games.  The Court clarified that, as long as any person could have received a ticket without paying 
10   anything for it, it did not matter how many tickets were distributed with a purchase.  Regal, 50 
11   Cal. App. 2d at 858-59.                                               
12        In contrast, in People v. Gonzales the court held that a promotion was a lottery because 
13   “[t]here was no general or indiscriminate distribution of the drawing tickets to persons irrespective 
14   of whether they paid admission.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1131139"><a href="/opinion/1131139/people-v-gonzales/#279" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Gonzales">62 Cal. App. 2d 274, 279</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1944).  Instead, a person had to 
15   purchase at least one admission ticket in order to participate in the drawing.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1131139"><a href="/opinion/1131139/people-v-gonzales/#280" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Gonzales">Id. at 280</a></span><pre class="inline">.   
         Summarizing the “implicit holdings” of these leading lottery cases, the court in People v. 
16                                                                         
    Shira explained:                                                      
17                                                                         
18             in order for a promotional giveaway scheme to be legal any and all 
              persons must be given a ticket free of charge and without any of them 
19             paying for the opportunity of a chance to win a prize.  Conversely, a 
              promotional scheme is  illegal  where  any and  all persons cannot 
20             participate in a chance for the prize and some of the participants who 
              want a chance to win must pay for it.                       
21                                                                         
    </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2106581"><a href="/opinion/2106581/people-v-shira/#459" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Shira">62 Cal. App. 3d 442, 459</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1976); see also Haskell v. Time, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2139267"><a href="/opinion/2139267/haskell-v-time-inc/#1404" aria-description="Citation for case: Haskell v. Time, Inc.">965 F. Supp. 1398, 1404</a></span><pre class="inline"> (E.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">22   Cal. 1997</span><pre class="inline">) (“California courts have consistently held that business promotions are not lotteries so 
23   long as tickets to enter are not conditioned upon a purchase.”).      
24        Although a close case, the Court finds that, as currently alleged in the Second Amended 
25   Complaint, the Dogecoin sweepstakes was not an illegal lottery.  In the California cases finding no 
26   consideration, the tickets were clearly and widely distributed for free.  Cardas; 137 Cal. App. 
    Supp. at 789; Regal, 50 Cal. App. 2d at 852-53; Carpenter, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1140316"><a href="/opinion/1140316/people-v-carpenter/#889" aria-description="Citation for case: People v. Carpenter">141 Cal. App. 2d at 889-90</a></span><pre class="inline">.  However, 
27                                                                         
1   Although Plaintiffs may not have been aware of it when they made a trade of Dogecoins, they 
2   were not actually required to trade Dogecoins in order to enter the sweepstakes and have a chance 
    to win.  Because California penal statutes are construed strictly and because no California court 
3                                                                         
    has held that being unaware of the free method of entry is sufficient to demonstrate the required 
4                                                                         
    consideration, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have not and cannot allege a violation of California 
5                                                                         
    Penal Code § 320.  Therefore, the Court GRANTS Coinbase’s motion to dismiss as to Plaintiffs’ 
6                                                                         
    first claim (violation of </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200</span><pre class="inline">) in full and Plaintiffs’ second claim 
7                                                                         
    (violation of </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code §§ 17200</span><pre class="inline">, 17539.15) and sixth claim (violation of Cal. Civ. 
8                                                                         
    Code § 1750) to the extent they are is premised on a violation of Penal Code § 320.  At oral 
9                                                                         
    argument, Plaintiffs advanced a theory that they conceded they had not explicitly pleaded in the 
10   Second Amended Complaint, and the Court GRANTS leave to amend to advance this theory.  
11        2.   Disclosure and Misrepresentation Claims.                    
12        That many people may not have been aware that there was a free method of entry is 
13   significant for Plaintiffs’ claims for disclosure and misrepresentation under the UCL, FAL, and 
14   CLRA.  Under the FAL, the CLRA, and the fraudulent prong of the UCL, conduct is considered 
15   deceptive or misleading if the conduct is “likely to deceive” a “reasonable consumer.”  Williams v. 
16   Gerber Prods. Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1243130"><a href="/opinion/1243130/williams-v-gerber-products-co/#938" aria-description="Citation for case: Williams v. Gerber Products Co.">552 F.3d 934, 938</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008).  Because the same standard for false 
    advertising or misrepresentations governs all three statutes, courts often analyze the three statutes 
17                                                                         
    together.  Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242812"><a href="/opinion/7324900/hadley-v-kellogg-sales-co/#1089" aria-description="Citation for case: Hadley v. Kellogg Sales Co.">243 F. Supp. 3d 1074, 1089</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2017).  Upon 
18                                                                         
    review of Coinbase’s advertising materials as alleged in the Second Amended Complaint, the 
19                                                                         
    Court finds that Plaintiffs state a claim that the materials were likely to deceive a reasonable 
20                                                                         
    consumer that they needed to make a trade to participate in the sweepstakes.  While Coinbase may 
21                                                                         
    have actually disclosed the free method in the Dogecoin sweepstakes’ Official Rules, its 
22                                                                         
    advertising methods heavily directed people to make a trade in order to participate in this 
23   sweepstakes.  Additionally, Coinbase’s statements regarding “no purchase necessary” were 
24   ambiguous in light of the other statements regarding the need to “buy or sell” Dogecoin.  Persons 
25   could have reasonably believed they were required to buy or sell Dogecoin to participate, which 
26   would have been consistent with not making a purchase but still requiring them to make a trade. 
27        Additionally, California law requires sweepstakes sponsors to include a “clear and 
   1    materials.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17539.15</span><pre class="inline">(b).”  The statute defines the “no-purchase-or- 
   2  || payment-necessary” statement to mean a statement substantially similar to: “No purchase or 
   3  || payment of any kind is necessary to enter or win this sweepstakes.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 
   4</span><pre class="inline">    17539.15(k)(1).  There are no cases construing this statute.  Therefore, the Court considers the 
   5  || language of the statute, which requires a “clear and conspicuous statement” that “no purchase or 
   6  || payment of any kind” is required to enter or win.  The Court finds that Plaintiffs have alleged 
   7    sufficient facts to show that Coinbase’s advertisements were not “clear and conspicuous” as to 
   8  || whether all persons could enter for free. 
   9          Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have alleged sufficient facts as to the remainder 
  10  || of their claims and DENIES Coinbase’s motion to dismiss as to Plaintiffs’ second through seventh 
  11    claims to the extent they are not premised on a violation of California Penal Code § 320.
