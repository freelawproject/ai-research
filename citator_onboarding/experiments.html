Experiments

The citator has gone through five broad phases of work since early 2025. This page shows the direction of travel — what we tried, what we learned, and why each phase pointed us at the next. It is not a metric dump: for most experiments the relevant story is what changed and why, not the F1 numbers, because the metrics aren’t directly comparable across phases.

Why metrics jump around. Eval setups varied across phases. The Phase 1 binary (overrule vs not-overrule) work was scored on a ~1,000-record dataset derived from Congress.gov’s Decisions Overruled list. The multi-treatment work that followed was scored on small hand-curated sets (8–18 cases) until the full-taxonomy expert labels were ready — useful as a directional signal, not for honest evaluation. Apples-to-apples comparison across the multi-treatment work only begins with the first full-benchmark run on the 260410 benchmark.

The five phases at a glance

Phase 1 · Feb–Sep 2025

Binary classification — “overruled” vs “not overruled”

The starting question

Begin with the simplest possible reduction of the citator: — has an earlier decision been overruled by a later decision? Build a dataset using Congress.gov’s Decisions Overruled list as positives and CourtListener case law database for sampled negatives.

Model bake-off across providers

Run the same prompt-and-data setup through every text-capable generative LLM available in Bedrock plus the major OpenAI models. Score with precision / recall / F1; track latency and estimated cost so the eventual production choice is grounded in price-per-opinion at scale, not just accuracy.

What we learned. Claude 3.5 Sonnet was the best all-around balance of precision and recall; GPT 4o was a close second; Mistral Large showed surprisingly strong precision; reasoning models (o1, o3-mini) didn’t beat the base models for this task and were 10× the cost. Most smaller models (Nova, Cohere, Haiku at the time) were not capable enough.

Excerpt quality is upstream of everything

Many failures traced back to the input text — not the model. Reference citations (“Id.”, “supra”) weren’t being captured, so the language that actually overruled the cited case was missing from the excerpt. We rebuilt the excerpt pipeline to include reference citations, split lead opinion from concurrences/dissents, and use sentence boundaries.

What we learned. Treating the input as a fixed thing was wrong. Better excerpts moved every model. Text quality and citation resolution are the leverage points the model sits on top of.

Whole-opinion classification

Switch from per-excerpt to passing the entire citing opinion plus the list of cited cases in one prompt, asking the model to analyze each cited case’s treatment. This eliminates excerpt-extraction failures entirely and let one inference cover all cited cases.

What we learned. Quality went up (0.94 recall, 0.87 F1) and per-opinion cost dropped to roughly a third of the excerpt approach. The shape of the citator started looking like “one pass per opinion, batch-classify all cites at once.”

First fine-grained classification

Expand from binary to a preliminary taxonomy of eight labels — Overruled, Reversed, Criticized, Questioned, Limited, Distinguished, Cited, Explained — plus an Uncertain class.

What we learned. The 8-label space surfaced exactly the long-tail problem that still defines the project: the rare classes (Overruled, Questioned, Limited) didn’t have enough labeled examples to evaluate honestly. Hence the expert volunteer call that began the expert-labeling effort that eventually produced the 260410 benchmark.

Dataset expansion + appellate-chain wiring

Generate larger sample sets for SCOTUS, federal appellate & district courts, state courts, and bankruptcy courts. Kick off the first round of volunteer annotation; refine label definitions; begin linking appellate chains for Direct History.

Phase 2 · Oct 2025 – Jan 2026

Building the expert benchmark

Volunteer-expert onboarding

The June 2025 call brought ~30 volunteer-expert responses, with more experts continuing to express interest and join every week since. Built shared instructions and definitions, ran calibration rounds, and refined the multi-expert workflow that anchors the benchmark today.

Multi-expert labeling pass

Selected citing clusters across federal appellate, federal district, and state/misc courts. Each (citing, cited) pair was labeled by at least 2 experts independently; tie-break and blinded adjudication workflows resolved disagreements. Hundreds of cluster-level reviews accumulated into the eventual 9,740-row labeled set.

Taxonomy stabilization

Edge cases surfaced during labeling drove iteration on the canonical treatment list — cert. denials, “as recognized by” constructions, partial dispositions, and the difference between Direct History, Citing Reference, and Related Reference. Over the period, the taxonomy stabilized at 43 treatments (22 base + 21 “as recognized by” variants).

What we learned. The interpretive layer of citator work needs a structured, expert-driven labeling protocol — not just a label list. The disagreement-and-adjudication workflow that emerged here is what makes the benchmark defensible as open ground truth.

Phase 3 · Feb–Mar 2026

Productionizing the pipeline — pagination, re-evaluation, and the full taxonomy

Instruction iteration on the full 43-treatment taxonomy

Move from the 8-label sketch to the full 43-treatment taxonomy. Iterate on the prediction prompt across versions, adding cert. treatments, partial-disposition language, “as recognized by” rules, and explicit Acting Case ≠ Target Case guards.

What we learned. Treatment quality improved with each prompt revision, but specific axes regressed when new classes were added (direction F1 dropped when cert. treatments expanded the Related Reference class). Asking a single model call to do everything — find cites and classify them across all 43 treatments — has structural ceilings, which later motivated splitting the work across stages.

Broader model comparison

Test 17 candidate models on a citation-extraction + negative-treatment-flagging task. Track match rate, F1 on the flagged class, and per-case cost in dollars.

What we learned. Claude Sonnet 4.6 was the strongest overall — the only model that returned every citation in the eval set, making it the obvious anchor for any extraction-heavy stage. Kimi K2.5 produced the best F1 on the negative-treatment flag itself (0.70), but missed ~11% of citations — its strength is classification, not extraction. That split-of-strengths later shaped the two-stage architecture: one model extracts, a different one classifies. The other models failed in distinct ways: most cheaper models (Llama 3.3 70B, Gemma 3 27B, DeepSeek-R1, GPT 4o Mini, Llama 4 Maverick) missed a majority of citations entirely — an extraction-completeness failure that ruled them out before classification quality even mattered; a few (Qwen 3 235B, Haiku 4.5, GPT 4.1 Mini, Qwen 3 Next 80B) over-flagged, producing dozens of false positives; GPT 4.1, GPT 5.4 Mini, and GPT OSS 120B couldn’t reach a usable F1 on the flagged-class judgment; and the two Gemini Previews plus DeepSeek V3.2 were competitive on F1 but missed too many citations to displace Sonnet.

Pagination + post-processing + a re-evaluator pass

Add pagination for long opinions; add post-processing for deduplication and keyword-based re-evaluation of high-risk treatments (cert. denials, “Reversed by” vs “Reversed and remanded by”). The re-evaluator is a cheap second model that only looks at flagged predictions.

What we learned. A Kimi K2.5 re-evaluator on top of a Sonnet base lifted direction quality substantially at only ~6% extra cost. Re-evaluation is a high-leverage hook for the specific error modes the base model gets wrong.

Per-court production runs

Parameterize the run by court so the same pipeline can crawl whole jurisdictions. Add citation normalization and treatment canonicalization for label-matching reliability.

Phase 4 · Mar–Apr 2026

Architecture exploration — batch inference and a two-stage pipeline

Move to AWS Bedrock batch inference

Switch the pathway from on-demand inference to Bedrock batch jobs for faster and cheaper reiterations.

What we learned. Batch is correctness-equivalent (within ±0.05 of on-demand on the same data) and roughly halves per-case cost — the right default for corpus-scale runs.

Two-stage extract → classify exploration

Split the pipeline into a cheap extraction stage that finds cited cases and proposes section context, followed by a focused classification stage that reads only the section context for each cited case — not the whole opinion.

What we learned. On the 8-case eval, the two-stage configuration matched the one-stage Sonnet on treatment quality and direction at ~77% lower cost. Adding a third Sonnet re-evaluator on top didn’t help — once the classifier prompt is well-tuned, additional re-evaluation is dead weight.

Phase 5 · May 2026 · current

Full-benchmark evaluation and live iteration

Haiku + Kimi two-stage full-benchmark run — the baseline

The first end-to-end batch run of the production two-stage pipeline on the full 260410 benchmark. Haiku 4.5 handles citation extraction; Kimi K2.5 handles treatment classification (best classification F1 from the Phase 3 bake-off). This is the cost-efficient configuration that all subsequent experiments measure against.

What we learned. Treatment macro F1 0.38, QWK 0.70, Cohen’s κ 0.58 — the first honest, full-taxonomy number for the project. Coverage 321 clusters / 8,856 matched rows; per-case cost ~$0.05 batch. The two-stage split lets Stage 2 read only the section around each cited case, which keeps cost low at corpus scale — the natural default for cost-sensitive jurisdiction-wide runs.

Sonnet single-stage full-benchmark run — current best on quality

Sonnet 4.6 does both citation extraction and treatment classification in one pass per opinion, with the full opinion in a single attention window. Tested against the Haiku + Kimi baseline to see whether a single-model architecture could close the long-tail gap.

What we learned. Treatment macro F1 0.47, QWK 0.79, Cohen’s κ 0.71 — beats the baseline on every agreement metric (+0.09 macro F1, +0.10 QWK, +0.12 κ) and on coverage (327 clusters / 9,558 matched rows). A Kimi K2.5 re-evaluator pass was tried on top and did more harm than good — it routes correct Sonnet predictions to Kimi, which then over-flips them. Re-eval is dropped; the recommended configuration is Sonnet alone, at ~1.9× the baseline’s per-case cost.

Eyecite-anchored pipeline experiment — abandoned

Hypothesis: pre-tag every citation in the opinion with FLP’s deterministic eyecite parser, then have a cheap LLM stage just confirm / split / merge those groupings into distinct cited cases. The goal was to leverage eyecite’s high-recall, deterministic citation finding while having the LLM compensate for eyecite’s known limitations — particularly its unreliable case-identity matching across short forms (Id., supra) and reference citations, where chains get mischained or missed.

What we learned. The tagged-text assembly worked (Phase 1 validated cleanly across the full benchmark). The grouping stage did not. The model was just as unreliable at grouping pre-tagged citations as it had been at extracting them — cross-chunk inconsistency, attention dilution on dense SCOTUS-class opinions, case fragmentation, and ~3× the cost of the Haiku + Kimi baseline. Grouping pre-tagged citations is roughly as hard as extracting them; the LLM’s bottleneck is holding the whole opinion in mind, not which extractor produced the candidates.
