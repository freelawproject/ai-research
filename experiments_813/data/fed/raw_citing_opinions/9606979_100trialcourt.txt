22    I.  BACKGROUND                                                       
23        As alleged in the Complaint, Defendant Bayer markets and sells the Mirena intrauterine 
24   device (“Mirena” or “the device”).  ECF No. 1 (“Compl.”) ¶ 1.  Mirena is a hormonal IUD, and 
25   specifically a levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system.  Id. ¶ 2.  The device is inserted into a 
26   woman’s uterus, where it releases progestin, a hormone that thickens mucus in the cervix to stop 
27   sperm from fertilizing an egg; it also thins the uterus lining and partially suppresses ovulation.  Id.  
1   Bayer markets the device as birth control.  Id. ¶ 1.                  
2        Sidhu alleges that Mirena “significantly increase[s] the risk of breast cancer in users,” and 
3   that this information is not disclosed on the packaging, the list of “safety considerations” on the 
4   website, or in any other materials that Bayer distributes to doctors or consumers.  Compl. ¶ 1, 3-5.  
5   She further alleges that Bayer “has long known that the Product significantly increases the risk of 
6   breast cancer.”  Id. ¶ 6.  Plaintiff cites to several studies, which she alleges show a “significantly 
7   increased” risk of breast cancer.  See id. ¶¶ 7-11.                   
8        Sidhu was prescribed and used Mirena between February 2019 and February 2022 in 
9   California.  Compl. ¶ 15.  She paid $50 out-of-pocket for the device.  Id.  She alleges that she 
10   reviewed the patient brochure upon first using Mirena, and she further alleges that she would not 
11   have purchased Mirena, or would have “paid significantly less,” if Bayer had disclosed that 
12   Mirena “carried with it a significantly elevated risk of developing breast cancer.”  Id.  She finally 
13   alleges that Bayer never warned her or her doctor about the elevated breast cancer risk.  Id. 
14        This lawsuit was filed on March 14, 2022.  See Compl.  The Complaint asserts claims for 
15   (1) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability, Compl. ¶¶ 31-40; (2) unjust enrichment, 
16   Compl. ¶¶ 41-45; (3) fraud, Compl. ¶¶ 46-53; (4) negligence, Compl. ¶¶ 54-57; (5) violation of all 
17   three prongs of the California Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”), </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17200</span><pre class="inline"> 
18   et seq., Compl. ¶¶ 58-71; (6) violation of the California Consumer Legal Remedies Act 
19   (“CLRA”), </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Civ. Code § 1750</span><pre class="inline"> et seq., Compl. ¶¶ 72-92; and (7) violation of the California 
20   False Advertising Law (“FAL”), </pre><span class="citation no-link">Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code § 17500</span><pre class="inline"> et seq., Compl. ¶¶ 93-99.  Sidhu

1   “generally known” or that “can be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy

5   IV.  FAILURE TO STATE A CLAIM – RULE 12(B)(6)                         
6        “A motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a 
7   claim upon which relief can be granted ‘tests the legal sufficiency of a claim.’”  Conservation 
8   Force v. Salazar, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="221641"><a href="/opinion/221641/conservation-force-v-salazar/#1241" aria-description="Citation for case: Conservation Force v. Salazar">646 F.3d 1240, 1241-42</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (quoting Navarro v. Block, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="199405"><a href="/opinion/199405/havercombe-v-department-of-education-of-puerto-rico/#729" aria-description="Citation for case: Havercombe v. Department of Education of Puerto Rico">250 F.3d 
9   729, 732</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001)).  When determining whether a claim has been stated, the Court accepts 
10   as true all well-pled factual allegations and construes them in the light most favorable to the 
11   plaintiff.  Reese v. BP Expl. (Alaska) Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="219976"><a href="/opinion/219976/reese-v-bp-exploration-alaska-inc/#690" aria-description="Citation for case: Reese v. BP Exploration (Alaska) Inc.">643 F.3d 681, 690</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011).  But the Court need 
12   not “accept as true allegations that contradict matters properly subject to judicial notice” or 
13   “allegations that are merely conclusory, unwarranted deductions of fact, or unreasonable 
14   inferences.”  In re Gilead Scis. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1400355"><a href="/opinion/1400355/in-re-gilead-sciences-securities-litigation/#1055" aria-description="Citation for case: In Re Gilead Sciences Securities Litigation">536 F.3d 1049, 1055</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008) (internal quotation 
15   marks and citations omitted).  While a complaint need not contain detailed factual allegations, it 
16   “must contain sufficient factual matter, accepted as true, to ‘state a claim to relief that is plausible 
17   on its face.’”  Ashcroft v. Iqbal, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435339"><a href="/opinion/145875/ashcroft-v-iqbal/#678" aria-description="Citation for case: Ashcroft v. Iqbal">556 U.S. 662, 678</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009) (quoting Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 
18   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/#570" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">550 U.S. 544, 570</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2007)).  A claim is facially plausible when it “allows the court to draw the 
19   reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the misconduct alleged.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435068"><a href="/opinion/145730/bell-atlantic-corp-v-twombly/" aria-description="Citation for case: Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  On a motion to 
20   dismiss, the Court’s review is limited to the face of the complaint and matters judicially 
21   noticeable.  MGIC Indem. Corp. v. Weisman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="478039"><a href="/opinion/478039/mgic-indemnity-corporation-v-lawrence-i-weisman-thomas-p-dunn-ln/#504" aria-description="Citation for case: Mgic Indemnity Corporation v. Lawrence I. Weisman, Thomas...">803 F.2d 500, 504</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1986); N. Star Int’l v. 
22   Ariz. Corp. Comm’n, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="426434"><a href="/opinion/426434/north-star-international-a-nevada-corporation-plaintiff-appellant-v-the/#581" aria-description="Citation for case: NORTH STAR INTERNATIONAL, a Nevada Corporation,...">720 F.2d 578, 581</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 1983).

24        Bayer argues that the learned intermediary doctrine bars all of Sidhu’s claims.  MTD at 5-
25   7.  “Under California law, when drugs or medical devices are supplied in the context of the 
26   physician-patient relationship, the learned intermediary doctrine applies.”  Himes v. Somatics, 
27   LLC, No. 21-55517, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 989469</span><pre class="inline">, at *1 (9th Cir. Apr. 1, 2022) (citing Webb v. Special Elec. 
1   duty to warn physicians of risks that are known or scientifically knowable at the time of the drug’s 
2   distribution.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Wendell v. GlaxoSmithKline LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4174106"><a href="/opinion/4396853/stephen-wendell-v-glaxosmithkline-llc/#1238" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephen Wendell v. Glaxosmithkline LLC">858 F.3d 1227, 1238</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
3   2017)).  Therefore, “the duty to warn runs to the physician, not to the patient.”  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4174106"><a href="/opinion/4396853/stephen-wendell-v-glaxosmithkline-llc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Stephen Wendell v. Glaxosmithkline LLC">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Carlin 
4   v. Superior Ct., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9561597"><a href="/opinion/1205523/carlin-v-superior-court/#1354" aria-description="Citation for case: Carlin v. Superior Court">920 P.2d 1347, 1354</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1996)).                         
5        Bayer argues that all of Sidhu’s claims should be dismissed because the learned 
6   intermediary doctrine applies.  MTD at 5-7.  Sidhu argues that (1) the learned intermediary 
7   doctrine does not apply at the pleadings stage, (2) the learned intermediary doctrine does not apply 
8   to its design defect claims, and (3) Bayer did not provide an adequate warning to Sidhu’s 
9   physician.  Opp. at 2-4.  The Court will first address whether the learned intermediary doctrine 
10   applies at this phase of the proceedings, and it will then turn to the issue of design defect. 
11        Pleadings                                                        
12        Sidhu argues that the learned intermediary doctrine is an affirmative defense that should 
13   not be applied at the pleadings stage.  Opp. at 2.  Bayer argues that the Court can apply the learned 
14   intermediary doctrine in deciding the motion to dismiss, and it points the Court to several 
15   examples.  Reply at 4.

17   Plaintiff here, noting that “federal courts in California routinely apply the learned intermediary 
18   doctrine in dismissing complaints upon motion of defendants.”  Kamlade v. LEO Pharma Inc., No. 
19   1:21-cv-00522-DAD-EPG, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 358429</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 n.3 (E.D. Cal. Feb. 7, 2022).  In that case, the 
20   court decided that because the learned intermediary doctrine applied, plaintiff would need to 
21   provide “allegations as to what defendants did or did not tell plaintiff’s prescribing physician” in 
22   order “to adequately assert” the claim for breach of implied warranty of merchantability.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. at *4</span><pre class="inline">.

26</span><pre class="inline">   5698751, at *4 (N.D. Cal. Sept. 29, 2015).  In another, the court held that if a doctor had 
27   prescribed the product at issue, then plaintiffs would be required to “properly allege a failure to

4   [product] to Plaintiff.”  Tapia v. Davol, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7232453"><a href="/opinion/7314548/tapia-v-davol-inc/#1158" aria-description="Citation for case: Tapia v. Davol, Inc.">116 F. Supp. 3d 1149, 1158-59</a></span><pre class="inline"> (S.D. Cal. 2015).  
5   Relatedly, this Court previously held that “a plaintiff alleging failure to warn regarding a medical

9   facts as to the failure to warn the doctor.  See Baker v. Bayer Healthcare Pharms., No. C13-0490

10        B.  Preemption                                                   
11        Bayer argues that Sidhu’s claims are preempted by the federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic 
12   Act (“FDCA”).  MTD at 11-14.  The Supreme Court explained the relevant FDCA provisions as 
13   follows:                                                              

14             The FDA’s premarket approval of a new drug application includes the 
              approval of the exact text in the proposed label.  See </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 U.S.C. § 355</span><pre class="inline">; 
15             </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 CFR § 314.105</span><pre class="inline">(b) (2008).  Generally speaking, a manufacturer 
              may only change a drug label after the FDA approves a supplemental 
16             application.  There is, however, an FDA regulation that permits a 
              manufacturer to make certain changes to its label before receiving the 
17             agency’s  approval.    Among  other  things,  this  “changes  being 
              effected”  (CBE)  regulation  provides  that  if  a  manufacturer  is 
18             changing a label to “add or strengthen a contraindication, warning, 
              precaution,  or  adverse  reaction”  or  to  “add  or  strengthen  an 
19             instruction  about  dosage  and  administration  that  is  intended  to 
              increase the safe use of the drug product,” it may make the labeling 
20             change upon filing its supplemental application with the FDA; it need 
              not wait for FDA approval.  §§ 314.70(c)(6)(iii)(A), (C).   
21                                                                         
    Wyeth v. Levine, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9435372"><a href="/opinion/145902/wyeth-v-levine/#568" aria-description="Citation for case: Wyeth v. Levine">555 U.S. 555, 568</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2009).  “[A] 2008 amendment [to the CBE regulation] 
22                                                                         
    provides that a manufacturer may only change its label ‘to reflect newly acquired information.’”

         Failure to Warn                                                  
11                                                                         
         Bayer argues that the failure to warn claims are preempted because it could not have 
12                                                                         
    changed the warnings on Mirena under the CBE regulation.  MTD at 11-14.  As discussed above, 
13                                                                         
    the CBE regulation provides when a manufacturer can independently change a drug label.  The 
14                                                                         
    regulation allows a manufacturer to change “the labeling to reflect newly acquired information” in 
15                                                                         
    certain circumstances, including “[t]o add or strengthen a contraindication, warning, precaution, or 
16                                                                         
    adverse reaction for which the evidence of a causal association satisfies the standard for inclusion 
17                                                                         
    in the labeling under § 201.57(c).”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 CFR § 314.70</span><pre class="inline">(c)(6)(iii)(A).  In turn, § 201.57(c) requires a 
18                                                                         
    change in labeling in accordance with § 314.70 “to include a warning about a clinically significant 
19                                                                         
    hazard as soon as there is reasonable evidence of a causal association with a drug; a causal 
20                                                                         
    relationship need not have been definitely established.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 CFR § 201.57</span><pre class="inline">(c)(6)(i).  Finally, the 
21                                                                         
    regulations define “newly acquired information” as follows:           
22                                                                         
              data, analyses, or other information not previously submitted to the 
23             Agency, which may include (but is not limited to) data derived from 
              new clinical studies, reports of adverse events, or new analyses of 
24             previously submitted data (e.g., meta-analyses) if the studies, events, 
              or analyses reveal risks of a different type or greater severity or 
25             frequency than previously included in submissions to FDA.   
26   </pre><span class="citation no-link">21 CFR § 314.3</span><pre class="inline">(b).                                                    
27        Bayer argues that (1) the studies cited by Sidhu are not “newly acquired information” and 
1   MTD at 11-14.  Sidhu argues that the post-2010 studies she cites do constitute “newly acquired

3        The Court will first address whether the identified studies constitute “newly acquired 
4   information.”  In Holley, the court noted that it could not tell from the allegations in the complaint 
5   “whether – and, if so, when – any such information was provided to the FDA,” and it therefore 
6   could not tell whether there existed newly acquired information (“data, analyses, or other 
7   information not previously submitted to the [FDA]”) such that the defendant could have changed 
8   the label under the CBE regulation.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7253766"><a href="/opinion/7335846/holley-v-gilead-scis-inc/#829" aria-description="Citation for case: Holley v. Gilead Scis., Inc.">379 F. Supp. 3d at 829-30</a></span><pre class="inline"> (emphasis in original).  The court 
9   therefore dismissed the claims for preemption.                        
10        The Court here follows that approach.  Sidhu has not alleged what information was 
11   provided to the FDA and when, and the Court therefore cannot determine whether the post-2010

13   “plausibly alleged that [Bayer] could have made, under the CBE regulation or any other federal

24        C.  Fraud                                                        
25            1.  Rule 9(b)                                                
26        When a party pleads a cause of action for fraud or mistake, it is subject to the heightened 
27   pleading requirements of Rule 9(b).  “In alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with 
1   added).  “Malice, intent, knowledge, and other conditions of a person’s mind may be alleged 
2   generally.”  Id.  Rule 9(b) requires that the circumstances constituting any alleged fraud be pled 
3   “specific[ally] enough to give defendants notice of the particular misconduct . . . so that they can 
4   defend against the charge and not just deny that they have done anything wrong.”  Kearns v. Ford 
5   Motor Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208595"><a href="/opinion/1208595/kearns-v-ford-motor-co/#1124" aria-description="Citation for case: Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.">567 F.3d 1120, 1124</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2009) (quoting Bly-Magree v. California, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="185335"><a href="/opinion/185335/jenkins-v-washington-convention-center/#1014" aria-description="Citation for case: Jenkins v. Washington Convention Center">236 F.3d 
6   1014, 1019</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001)).  Claims of fraud must be accompanied by the “who, what, when, 
7   where, and how” of the misconduct alleged.  </pre><span class="citation" data-id="185335"><a href="/opinion/185335/jenkins-v-washington-convention-center/" aria-description="Citation for case: Jenkins v. Washington Convention Center">Id.</a></span><pre class="inline">  If a “claim is said to be ‘grounded in fraud’ or to 
8   ‘sound to fraud,’ [then] the pleading of that claim as a whole must satisfy that particularity 
9   requirement of Rule 9(b).”  Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1103" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d 1097, 1103-04</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 
10   2003).                                                                
11        The applicability of Rule 9(b) hinges not on the elements of the claim but rather on the 
12   nature of the allegations themselves: “Rule 9(b) applies to ‘averments of fraud’ in all civil cases in 
13   federal district court,” including “particular averments of fraud” even when fraud is not an 
14   essential element of the claim.  Vess, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1103" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d at 1103</a></span><pre class="inline">; see also Kearns, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1208595"><a href="/opinion/1208595/kearns-v-ford-motor-co/#1124" aria-description="Citation for case: Kearns v. Ford Motor Co.">567 F.3d at 1124</a></span><pre class="inline"> (“Where 
15   fraud is not an essential element of a claim, only those allegations of a complaint which aver fraud 
16   are subject to Rule 9(b)&#x27;s heightened pleading standard.”).  Fraud can thus be averred “by 
17   specifically alleging fraud, or by alleging facts that necessarily constitute fraud (even if the word 
18   ‘fraud’ is not used).”  Vess, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/#1105" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">317 F.3d at 1105</a></span><pre class="inline"> (citations omitted).   
19            2.  UCL fraud prong, CLRA, FAL, and common law fraud         
20        Plaintiff brings several common law and consumer protection claims that sound in fraud: 
21   (1) common law fraud, Compl. Claim III ¶¶ 46-53; (2) violation of the fraud prong of the UCL, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> 
22   Claim V ¶¶ 58-71; (3) violation of the CLRA, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> Claim VI ¶¶ 72-92; and (4) violation of the FAL, 
23   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7016609"><a href="/opinion/7110394/vess-v-ciba-geigy-corp-usa/" aria-description="Citation for case: Vess v. Ciba-Geigy Corp. USA">id.</a></span><pre class="inline"> Claim VII ¶¶ 93-99.                                               
24        “Broadly stated: The UCL prohibits ‘any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or 
25   practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising and any act prohibited by the 
26   FAL’ ([Cal. Bus. &amp; Prof. Code] § 17200); the FAL prohibits advertising ‘which is untrue or 
27   misleading, and which is known, or which by the exercise of reasonable care should be known, to 
1   competition and unfair or deceptive acts or practices’ ([Cal.] Civ. Code § 1770, subd. (a)).”  Hill v. 
2   Roll Int’l Corp., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="5665723"><a href="/opinion/5810290/hill-v-roll-international-corp/#1301" aria-description="Citation for case: Hill v. Roll International Corp.">195 Cal. App. 4th 1295, 1301</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2011) (alterations omitted).  All three statutes 
3   prohibit fraudulent misrepresentations and omissions.  See In re Seagate Tech. LLC Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8799596"><a href="/opinion/8815120/the-coastwise/" aria-description="Citation for case: The Coastwise">233 F. 
4</a></span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d 776, 788 (N.D. Cal. 2017).                                   
5        Bayer argues that Sidhu’s fraud-based claims should be dismissed because she does not 
6   plead an affirmative misrepresentation or an actionable omission.  MTD at 14-17.  In her 
7   Opposition, Sidhu makes clear that her fraud is based on an omission theory.  Opp. at 10-14.  The 
8   Court therefore focuses on fraud by omission.                         
9        “Under California law, a claim of fraud by omission requires a showing of (1) the 
10   concealment or suppression of material fact, (2) a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) 
11   intentional concealment with intent to defraud, (4) justifiable reliance, and (5) resulting damages.”  
12   Edwards v. FCA US LLC, No. 22-cv-01871-WHO, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 1814144</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. June 2, 
13   2022) (quoting Lewis v. Google LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">851 F. App’x 723</span><pre class="inline">, 725 (9th Cir. 2021)).  Bayer argues that 
14   (1) there is no undisclosed fact, as there is no substantially elevated risk of breast cancer; (2) Sidhu 
15   has not adequately alleged Bayer’s knowledge; (3) Sidhu has not adequately alleged Bayer’s 
16   intent; and (4) Sidhu has not alleged reliance by her physician.  MTD at 15-16. 
17        The Court already addressed Bayer’s arguments as to whether there is an increased risk of 
18   breast cancer.  See supra Section III.A.  The Court will address each element in turn. 
19        Knowledge and Intent                                             
20        Bayer argues that Sidhu has not shown its knowledge of the alleged increased risk of breast 
21   cancer.  MTD at 15.  The Court begins by stating, as already noted by Bayer, that Sidhu need not 
22   show exclusive knowledge, as the duty to disclose is not at issue here.  See Reply at 12.  But Sidhu 
23   does need to show actual knowledge.  See Andren v. Apple Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">411 F. Supp. 3d 541</span><pre class="inline">, 564-65 
24   (N.D. Cal. 2019) (“To state a California fraudulent concealment and UCL claim based on an 
25   omission, a defendant must have known of the defect at the time of sale for a plaintiff to state a 
26   claim for fraud by omission.” (internal quotation marks and citation omitted)); see also In re 
27   Nexus 6P Prod. Liab. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7246673"><a href="/opinion/7328757/in-re-nexus-6p-prods-liab-litig/#926" aria-description="Citation for case: In re Nexus 6P Prods. Liab. Litig.">293 F. Supp. 3d 888, 926-27</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2018) (“California law 
1   unaware.” (internal quotation marks and citation omitted)).  She also needs to show “intentional 
2   concealment with intent to defraud.”  See Edwards, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 1814144</span><pre class="inline">, at *3.  Sidhu counters that 
3   she has adequately pled Bayer’s knowledge based on the identified scientific publications, and she 
4   argues that Bayer’s failure to provide a warning to consumers despite this knowledge is sufficient 
5   to show intent.  Opp. at 11-13.  Under Rule 9(b), a plaintiff need only allege knowledge and intent 
6   generally.  And Sidhu has done so.  She alleges that Bayer had knowledge based on certain 
7   identified studies and that Bayer still failed to share this information.  Compl. ¶¶ 49.  Those 
8   allegations are sufficient at this stage.                             
9        Reliance                                                         
10        Bayer argues that Sidhu has not pled reliance on the omission by her physician.  MTD at 
11   16.  Sidhu asserts that she has pled reliance because the learned intermediary doctrine does not 
12   apply, and she has pled reliance as to herself.  Opp. at 13-14.       
13        As discussed above, the learned intermediary doctrine does apply.  Sidhu must therefore 
14   plead reliance as to her physician.  See Tapia, 116 F. Supp. at 1164 (dismissing fraud claim where 
15   plaintiff did not “assert facts as to causation alleging that his own prescribing physician would not 
16   have used the device” if defendants had provided the information1); Fischer v. Boston Sci. Corp., 
17   No. SACV 19-02106 JVS (DFM), </pre><span class="citation no-link">2020 WL 2300138</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (C.D. Cal. Mar. 25, 2020) (dismissing 
18   fraud claim because “it is unclear when the representations were made to [plaintiff’s] unnamed 
19   physician, where the representations took place, or how they were made”).   
20        Conclusion                                                       
21        Bayer’s motion to dismiss Sidhu’s common law fraud claim, as well as her claims under 
22   the fraud prong of the UCL, the CLRA, and the FAL, is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

25   LLC, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7229057"><a href="/opinion/7311153/ladore-v-sony-computer-entertainment-america-llc/#1075" aria-description="Citation for case: Ladore v. 27   “[T]he ‘economic loss rule has been applied to bar a plaintiff’s tort recovery of economic damages 
1   property damage).’”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> (quoting N. Am. Chem. Co. v. 6    V.  EQUITABLE JURISDICTION                                           
7        Even if a district court has subject matter jurisdiction, “[t]here remains the question of 
8   equitable jurisdiction.”  Schlesinger v. Councilman, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9426042"><a href="/opinion/109224/schlesinger-v-councilman/#753" aria-description="Citation for case: Schlesinger v. Councilman">420 U.S. 738, 753-54</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1975).  Both are 
9   required for a federal court to hear the merits of an equitable claim.  Guzman v. Polaris Indus. 
10   Inc., No. 21-55520, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2022 WL 4543709</span><pre class="inline">, at *5 (9th Cir. Sept. 29, 2022).  “Subject matter 
11   jurisdiction regards ‘whether the claim falls within the limited jurisdiction conferred on the federal 
12   courts’ by Congress, while equitable jurisdiction regards ‘whether consistently with the principles 
13   governing equitable relief the court may exercise its remedial powers.’”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Schlesinger, 
14   </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9426042"><a href="/opinion/109224/schlesinger-v-councilman/#754" aria-description="Citation for case: Schlesinger v. Councilman">420 U.S. at 754</a></span><pre class="inline">).  For a district court to have equitable jurisdiction, and thus entertain a request for 
15   equitable relief, the plaintiff must have no adequate legal remedy.  Sonner v. Premier Nutrition 
16   Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">971 F.3d 834</span><pre class="inline">, 844 (9th Cir. 2020).                             
17        Bayer argues that the UCL, FAL, and unjust enrichment claims should be dismissed 
18   because they are equitable claims, and Plaintiff has not established that she lacks an adequate 
19   remedy at law.  MTD at 18-19.  Sidhu argues that she has alleged that she lacks an equitable 
20   remedy at law.  Opp. at 18-19.  She further argues that she has no adequate remedy at law to 
21   rectify Bayer’s violations of the Sherman Act.  Opp. at 19-20.        
22        A plaintiff cannot state a claim for equitable relief “if the pleading does not demonstrate 
23   the inadequacy of a legal remedy.”  Sharma v. Volkswagen AG, </pre><span class="citation no-link">524 F. Supp. 3d 891</span><pre class="inline">, 907 (N.D. 
</pre><span class="citation no-link">24   Cal. 2021</span><pre class="inline">).  In arguing that she has pled the inadequacy of a legal remedy, Sidhu points to two 
25   paragraphs of the Complaint.  Opp. at 19 (citing Compl. ¶¶ 69, 91).  In one of these, Plaintiff 
26   alleges the necessity of injunctive relief.  See Compl. ¶ 91.  Because the Court has dismissed all 
27   claims for injunctive relief, Sidhu cannot rely on this allegation.  The second identified paragraph 
1   need for injunctive relief.  See Compl. ¶¶ 69-71.  Sidhu again cannot rely on this allegation.  And 
2   the Court will not consider Plaintiff’s arguments as to the Sherman Act, as they are not mentioned 
3   anywhere in the Complaint.  See Minor, 182 F. Supp. 3d at 977.        
4        Bayer’s motion to dismiss the UCL, FAL, and unjust enrichment claims for lack of 
5   equitable jurisdiction is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.                
6   VI.  MOTION TO STRIKE                                                 
7        Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(f) permits a court to “strike from a pleading an 
8   insufficient defense or any redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous matter.”  Fed. R. 
9   Civ. P. 12(f).  “While a Rule 12(f) motion provides the means to excise improper materials from 
10   pleadings, such motions are generally disfavored because the motions may be used as delaying 
11   tactics and because of the strong policy favoring resolution on the merits.”  Barnes v. AT &amp; T 
12   Pension Ben. Plan-Nonbargained Program, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2540569"><a href="/opinion/2540569/barnes-v-at-t-pension-benefit-plannonbargained-program/#1170" aria-description="Citation for case: Barnes v. AT &amp; T Pension Benefit Plannonbargained Program">718 F. Supp. 2d 1167, 1170</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2010).  “If 
13   there is any doubt whether the portion to be stricken might bear on an issue in the litigation, the 
14   court should deny the motion.”  Platte Anchor Bolt, Inc. v. IHI, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2299759"><a href="/opinion/2299759/platte-anchor-bolt-inc-v-ihi-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Platte Anchor Bolt, Inc. v. IHI, INC.">352 F. Supp. 2d 1048</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1057 
15   (N.D. Cal. 2004).  “Ultimately, whether to grant a motion to strike lies within the sound discretion 
16   of the district court.” Cruz v. Bank of N.Y. Mellon, No. 12-CV-00846-LHK, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 2838957</span><pre class="inline">, at 
17   *2 (N.D. Cal. July 10, 2012).                                         
18        A.  Punitive Damages                                             
19        Bayer argues that the Court should strike the request for punitive damages.  Reply at 14-
20   15.  This request is improperly raised for the first time in the Reply.  See MTD.  Regardless, the 
21   request fails on the merits.  Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(f) allows a court to “strike from a 
22   pleading an insufficient defense or any redundant, immaterial, impertinent, or scandalous matter.”  
23   Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(f).  Bayer has not met this standard.  Sidhu may be able to properly plead 
24   punitive damages in an Amended Complaint.  The motion to strike the request for punitive 
25   damages is DENIED.                                                    
26        B.  Class Allegations                                            
27        Bayer finally seeks to strike Sidhu’s class allegations.  MTD at 21-23.  Bayer argues that 
1   over class issues, and therefore the class allegations should be stricken.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  Sidhu asserts that 
2   Bayer’s arguments are premature.  Opp. at 25.                         
3        “There is a split in this District as to whether a motion to strike class action allegations 
4   may be entertained at the motion to dismiss stage.”  Ogala v. Chevron Corp., No. 14–cv–173–SC, 
5   </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 4145408</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Aug. 21, 2014) (collecting cases).  Even those courts that 
6   have considered such a motion early in the proceedings “have applied a very strict standard to 
7   motions to strike class allegations on the pleadings.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  “Only if the court is convinced that any

10   citation omitted).  Thus, even if a motion to strike class allegations is considered at the pleading 
11   stage, it may only be granted under “rare circumstances” where “the complaint demonstrates that a 
12   class action cannot be maintained on the facts alleged.”  Tasion, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2014 WL 1048710</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (citation 
13   omitted).

15   a class action could not be maintained under the facts alleged in an Amended Complaint.  
16   Therefore, Bayer’s motion to strike the class allegations is DENIED.
