25   II.  LEGAL STANDARDS                                                  
26        A motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) tests the legal 
27   sufficiency of the claims alleged in the complaint.  Ileto v. Glock, </pre><span class="citation multiple-matches"><a href="/c/F.3d/349/1191/">349 F.3d 1191</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1199-1200 (9th 
1   statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief.”  Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a)(2).  A 
2   complaint that fails to meet this standard may be dismissed pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6).  
3        To overcome a motion to dismiss brought under Rule 12(b)(6), the factual allegations in 
4   the plaintiff’s complaint “‘must . . . suggest that the claim has at least a plausible chance of 
5   success.’”  Levitt v. Yelp! Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2723330"><a href="/opinion/2723330/boris-levitt-v-yelp-inc/#1135" aria-description="Citation for case: Boris Levitt v. Yelp! Inc.">765 F.3d 1123, 1135</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2014) (quoting In re Century 
6   Aluminum Co. Sec. Litig., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8412570"><a href="/opinion/8441456/petzschke-v-century-aluminum-co/#1107" aria-description="Citation for case: Petzschke v. Century Aluminum Co.">729 F.3d 1104, 1107</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2013) (alterations in original)).  In ruling 
7   on the motion, courts “accept factual allegations in the complaint as true and construe the 
8   pleadings in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party.”  Manzarek, 519 F.3d at 1031. 
9        “[A]llegations in a complaint . . . may not simply recite the elements of a cause of action 
10   [and] must contain sufficient allegations of underlying facts to give fair notice and to enable the 
11   opposing party to defend itself effectively.”  Levitt, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2723330"><a href="/opinion/2723330/boris-levitt-v-yelp-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Boris Levitt v. Yelp! Inc.">765 F.3d at 1135</a></span><pre class="inline"> (quoting Starr v. Baca, 652 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="659857"><a href="/opinion/659857/united-states-v-michael-tracy-and-francisco-luis-aguilar/#1216" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Michael Tracy and Francisco Luis Aguilar">12   F.3d 1202, 1216</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011)).  The court may dismiss a claim “where there is either a lack of a 
13   cognizable legal theory or the absence of sufficient facts alleged under a cognizable legal claim.” 
14   Hinds Invs., L.P. v. Angioli, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="222220"><a href="/opinion/222220/hinds-investments-lp-v-angioli/#850" aria-description="Citation for case: Hinds Investments, L.P. v. Angioli">654 F.3d 846, 850</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2011) (citing Johnson v. Riverside 
15   Healthcare Sys., LP, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1200144"><a href="/opinion/1200144/johnson-v-riverside-healthcare-system-lp/#1121" aria-description="Citation for case: Johnson v. Riverside Healthcare System, LP">534 F.3d 1116, 1121</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2008)).  “[T]he non-conclusory ‘factual 
16   content’ and reasonable inferences from that content must be plausibly suggestive of a claim 
17   entitling the plaintiff to relief.”  Moss v. U.S. Secret Service, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="1450162"><a href="/opinion/1450162/moss-v-us-secret-service/#969" aria-description="Citation for case: Moss v. U.S. Secret Service">572 F.3d 962, 969</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2009).  
18        When deciding whether to grant a motion to dismiss, the court generally “may not consider 
19   any material beyond the pleadings.”  Hal Roach Studios, Inc. v. Richard Feiner &amp; Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8976708"><a href="/opinion/8984708/mcmahon-v-shearsonamerican-express-inc/#1542" aria-description="Citation for case: McMahon v. Shearson/American Express, Inc.">896 F.2d 
20   1542</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1555 n.19 (9th Cir. 1990).  However, the court may consider material submitted as part of 
21   the complaint or relied upon in the complaint and may also consider material subject to judicial 
22   notice.  See Lee v. City of Los Angeles, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="6998020"><a href="/opinion/7092482/lee-v-city-of-los-angeles/#688" aria-description="Citation for case: Lee v. City of Los Angeles">250 F.3d 668, 688-89</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2001).  The Court, 
23   however, is “not required to accept as true conclusory allegations which are contradicted by 
24   documents referred to in the complaint.”  Terraza v. Safeway Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="7242644"><a href="/opinion/7324732/terraza-v-safeway-inc/" aria-description="Citation for case: Terraza v. Safeway Inc.">241 F. Supp. 3d 1057</a></span><pre class="inline">, 1082 
25   (N.D. Cal. 2017).

18        B.   Motion to Dismiss                                           
19        Simpson moves to dismiss the complaint on nine grounds.  First, Simpson argues that the 
20   Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (“FDCA”) expressly preempts Valiente’s claims.  Mot. at 
21   18-20.  Second, Simpson argues that Valiente’s California UCL, CLRA, and FAL claims are 
22   implausible.  Id. at 20-25.  Third, Simpson argues that Valiente fails to plead her fraud claims with 
23   the requisite specificity.  Id. at 25-27.  Fourth, Simpson argues that Valiente’s breach of express 
24   warranty claim fails for lack of an actionable statement, promise or description.  Id. at 27-28.  
25   Fifth, Simpson argues that Valiente’s fraud claim fails because Valiente does not allege an actual 
26   misrepresentation or plead scienter or intent.  Id at 28.  Sixth, Simpson argues that Valiente lacks 
27   standing to pursue injunctive relief.  Id. at 29-30.  Seventh, Simpson argues that Valiente’s 
1   lacks an adequate remedy at law.  Id. at 30-31.  Eighth, Simpson argues that Valiente lacks 
2   standing to pursue claims based on products that she did not purchase.  Id. at 31-33.  Ninth, 
3   Simpson seeks dismissal of the class allegations or alternatively asks that the allegations be 
4   stricken because they are not tethered to any applicable statute of limitations.  Id. at 33-34.   
5        The Court first addresses the threshold issue of standing before assessing Simpson’s 
6   remaining arguments.                                                  
7             1.   Whether Valiente may assert claims concerning products she did not 
                   purchase.                                              
8                                                                         
9        Article III standing requires a showing of an injury in fact that is traceable to the 
10   challenged conduct and redressable by a favorable ruling.  See Spokeo, Inc. v. Robins, </pre><span class="citation no-link">578 U.S. 
11</span><pre class="inline">   338 (2016) (citing Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9432577"><a href="/opinion/112747/lujan-v-defenders-of-wildlife/#560" aria-description="Citation for case: Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife">504 U.S. 555, 560-61</a></span><pre class="inline"> (1992)).  In the Ninth 
12   Circuit, “[t]here is no controlling authority on whether Plaintiffs have standing for products they 
13   did not purchase.”  See Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8704589"><a href="/opinion/8721396/miller-v-ghirardelli-chocolate-co/#868" aria-description="Citation for case: Miller v. Ghirardelli Chocolate Co.">912 F. Supp. 2d 861, 868</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 
14   2012) (citing Donohue v. Apple, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="8699825"><a href="/opinion/8716632/donohue-v-apple-inc/#921" aria-description="Citation for case: Donohue v. Apple, Inc.">871 F. Supp. 2d 913, 921-22</a></span><pre class="inline"> (N.D. Cal. 2012) (collecting 
15   cases)).  However, “[t]he majority of the courts that have carefully analyzed the question hold that 
16   a plaintiff may have standing to assert claims for unnamed class members based on products he or 
17   she did not purchase so long as the products and alleged misrepresentations are substantially 
18   similar.”  Id. at 869.                                                
19        Courts have found substantial similarity across diverse products that bear similar labels, as 
20   “[t]he critical issue is whether the products are substantially similar with respect to the alleged 
21   mislabeling.”  See Baum v. J-B Weld Co., LLC, No. 19-cv-01718-EMC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2019 WL 6841231</span><pre class="inline">, at *4 
22   (N.D. Cal. Dec. 16, 2019) (concluding plaintiff had standing to bring claims based on 24 products 
23   with similar packaging and/or mislabeling); see also Bush v. Rust-Oleum Corp., No. 20-cv-03268-
24   LB, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2021 WL 24842</span><pre class="inline">, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 4, 2021) (holding plaintiff had standing to bring claims 
25   based on 13 products he did not purchase because he alleged that the front-label representations 
26   were substantially similar and alleged uniform misrepresentations across product lines); Astiana v. 
27   Dreyer’s Grand Ice Cream, Inc., Nos. C-11-2910-EMC, C-11-3164-EMC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2012 WL 2990766</span><pre class="inline">, at 
1   different ingredients is not dispositive as [p]laintiffs are challenging the same basic mislabeling 
2   practice across different product flavors.”).

4   products she did not purchase.4  Mot. at 31-32.  Simpson next contends that “[a]t the very least, 
5   plaintiffs seeking to sue for products they did not purchase must allege that the unpurchased 
6   products are so substantially similar to purchased products to satisfy Article III requirements.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline"> 
7   at 32-33.  On this point, Simpson asserts that Valiente’s conclusory allegation – “that [b]y its

1             2.   Whether the FDCA preempts Valiente’s claims.           
2        As amended by the Nutrition Labeling and Education Act (“NLEA”), the Food, Drug, and 
3   Cosmetic Act (“FDCA”), “expressly preempts all state statutes and law that directly or indirectly 
4   establish any requirement for the labeling of food that is not identical to the federal requirements 
5   set forth by statute and Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulations.”  Nacarino v. Kashi 
6   Co., </pre><span class="citation no-link">77 F.4th 1201</span><pre class="inline">, 1204 (9th Cir. 2023) (internal quotations, citations, and footnotes omitted); see 
7   also Hawkins v. Kroger Co., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="4318295"><a href="/opinion/4541042/shavonda-hawkins-v-the-kroger-co/#769" aria-description="Citation for case: Shavonda Hawkins v. the Kroger Co.">906 F.3d 763, 769</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018) (“The NLEA . . .  provides that no 
8   state may directly or indirectly establish any requirement for the labeling of food that is not 
9   identical to the federal requirements.”).                             
10        A state law requirement is not identical to federal requirements if it “directly or indirectly 
11   imposes obligations or contains provisions concerning the composition or labeling of food that . . . 
12   are not imposed by or contained in the FDCA or applicable federal regulation . . . or differ from 
13   those specifically imposed by or contained in the applicable provision of the FDCA or applicable 
14   federal regulation.”  Hollins v. Walmart Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">67 F.4th 1011</span><pre class="inline">, 1016 (9th Cir. 2023) (citing 21 C.F.R. 
15   § 100.1(c)(4)) (internal quotations and modifications omitted).  Therefore, “a state-law 
16   misbranding claim” that would allow “a state to impose requirements . . . different from those 
17   permitted under the FDCA . . . is preempted.”  Id. (citing Durnford v. MusclePharm Corp., 907 
</pre><span class="citation" data-id="664708"><a href="/opinion/664708/united-states-v-roger-p-reetz/#603" aria-description="Citation for case: United States v. Roger P. Reetz">18   F.3d 595, 603</a></span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2018) (internal quotations omitted)).         
19        Simpson characterizes Valiente’s claims as imposing a disclosure requirement that the 
20   FDCA does not contain, i.e., a requirement that Simpson specify the tomato varietal the consumer 
21   is purchasing.  Mot. at 19-20.  Relying on this characterization, Simpson argues that the FDCA 
22   expressly preempts Valiente’s claims because (a) the FDCA does not require Simpson to state the 
23   specific tomato varietal on the product label and instead only requires that Simpson state that the 
24   products are “tomatoes,” which the label does, and (b) Simpson complies with the FDCA’s 
25   requirement that the product label specify the county of origin, which is the United States.  Mot. at 
26   19.                                                                   
27        Valiente counters that she does not challenge Simpson’s representations that the products 
1   takes issue with the additional content on the product label—the illustration of a San Marzano

2        In order to protect its citizens from unfair, deceptive, or fraudulent business practices,

1        Accordingly, the Court finds that the allegations that Simpson (1) used to sell San Marzano 
2   tomatoes, (2) maintained all aspects of the old label except replacing “San Marzano” with “SMT” 
3   and adding “miniscule” text stating “San Merican Tomato,” and (3) now sells Roma tomatoes 
4   instead of San Marzano tomatoes under that nearly identical label sufficiently plead 
5   misrepresentation and scienter.                                       
6        The two cases Simpson cites in reply do not persuade otherwise.  In Smith v. Apple, Inc., a 
7   putative class action alleging that Apple misled consumers about the extent of the iPhone’s 
8   resistance to water exposure, the plaintiffs’ complaint “contain[ed] only a conclusory assertion 
9   that Apple acted with fraudulent intent” and “d[id] not plausibly allege that Apple knew its 
10   iPhones were not as water resistant as advertised.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">583 F. Supp. 3d 554</span><pre class="inline">, 568 (S.D.N.Y. 2022).  As 
11   explained above, Valiente’s allegations go well beyond those at issue in Smith.  Valiente alleges 
12   that Simpson sells its Roma tomato products under the label it previously used for its San 
13   Manzano tomato products, with very limited changes.  The limited changes – adding “SMT” and 
14   illegible text stating “San Merican Tomato” – support a strong inference that Simpson intended to 
15   make as few changes as possible to its product label to keep consumers under the impression that

              5.   Whether Valiente’s breach of express warranty claim fails for lack of 
2                  an actual misrepresentation.                           
3        To state a claim for breach of express warranty, plaintiff must establish three elements: 
4   “(1) the seller’s statements constitute an affirmation of fact or promise or a description of the 
5   goods; (2) the statement was part of the basis of the bargain; and (3) the warranty was breached.” 
6   Weinstat v. Dentsply Int’l, Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="2255890"><a href="/opinion/2255890/weinstat-v-dentsply-international-inc/#1227" aria-description="Citation for case: Weinstat v. Dentsply International, Inc.">180 Cal. App. 4th 1213, 1227</a></span><pre class="inline"> (2010) (internal quotation marks 
7   omitted).                                                             
8        Because “courts in this district regularly hold that stating a claim under California 
9   consumer protection statutes is sufficient to state a claim for express warranty,” the Court denies 
10   Simpson’s motion to dismiss Valiente’s warranty claim for the reasons set forth earlier in this 
11   order.  See DiGiacinto v. RB Health (US) LLC, </pre><span class="citation no-link">668 F. Supp. 3d 950</span><pre class="inline">, 966 (N.D. Cal. Apr. 11, 
12   2023) (denying motion to dismiss express warranty claim where plaintiff had sufficiently alleged 
13   deception under the reasonable consumer standard); cf. Kim v. Bluetriton Brands, Inc., No. 22-
14   56063, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2024 WL 243343</span><pre class="inline">, at *2 (9th Cir. Jan. 23, 2024) (dismissing plaintiff’s claims, including 
15   claim for breach of express warranty, where plaintiff failed to plead consumer deception). 
16             6.   Whether Valiente has standing to seek injunctive relief.

(TOC), Inc., </pre><span class="citation" data-id="9433887"><a href="/opinion/118329/friends-of-the-earth-inc-v-laidlaw-environmental-services-toc-inc/#185" aria-description="Citation for case: Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. 23        Simpson argues that Valiente now knows that Simpson’s products are not San Marzano 
24   tomatoes, does not allege a desire to purchase Simpson tomatoes in the future, and as a result, 
25   cannot establish a risk of future harm necessary to sustain a claim for injunctive relief.  Mot. at 29-
26   30.  Valiente does not address this argument in her opposition, perhaps because Simpson is correct 
27   that the complaint contains no allegation that there is a risk of future harm and that such an 
1   956, 969-70 (9th Cir. 2018) (“In some cases, the threat of future harm may be the consumer’s 
2   plausible allegations that she will be unable to rely on the product’s advertising or labeling in the

</pre><span class="citation no-link">4   WL 1191685</span><pre class="inline">, at *7 (noting that “[e]ven where courts find standing, most require that the plaintiff

8             7.   Whether Valiente can pursue equitable claims if she has an adequate 
                   remedy at law.                                         
9                                                                         
10        Simpson next argues that Valiente cannot pursue any equitable claims because she has an 
11   adequate remedy at law.  Mot. at 30-31.  Relying on Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">971 F.3d 
12</span><pre class="inline">   834 (9th Cir. 2020), and Guzman v. Polaris Industries, Inc., </pre><span class="citation no-link">49 F.4th 1308</span><pre class="inline"> (9th Cir. 2022), 
13   Simpson contends that Ninth Circuit law clearly resolves this issue in its favor.  Mot. at 30. 
14        In Sonner v. Premier Nutrition Corp., a mislabeling class action involving “Joint Juice,” 
15   the plaintiff initially brought claims for equitable relief under the UCL and CLRA and for 
16   damages under the CLRA.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">971 F.3d 834</span><pre class="inline">, 838 (9th Cir. 2020).  On the eve of trial, after litigating 
17   the case for four years, the plaintiff dismissed her state law damages claim and elected to move 
18   forward only on the state law equitable claims for restitution and injunctive relief.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. at 837</span><pre class="inline">.  The 
19   district court then dismissed the restitution claims because an adequate remedy at law, i.e., 
20   damages, was available.  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id.</span><pre class="inline">  On appeal, the Ninth Circuit affirmed the dismissal because the 
21   plaintiff “fail[ed] to establish that she lack[ed] an adequate remedy at law” in the CLRA, holding 
22   that “a federal court must apply traditional equitable principles before awarding restitution under 
23   the UCL and CLRA.”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. at 841, 844</span><pre class="inline">.                                  
24        In Guzman, also a mislabeling case, the district court granted summary judgment in favor 
25   of the defendants and dismissed the plaintiff’s UCL claim with prejudice.  49 F.4th at 1310.  The 
26   Ninth Circuit affirmed in part and reversed in part.  Id.  Based in part on Sonner, the Ninth Circuit 
27   affirmed the portion of the district court order dismissing the UCL claim.  Id. at 1313.  It agreed 
1   though he could no longer pursue the claim because it was time-barred.  Id. at 1311, 1313.  As to 
2   the portion of the district court’s order that dismissed the UCL claim with prejudice, the Ninth 
3   Circuit reversed with instructions to dismiss without prejudice for lack of equitable jurisdiction, 
4   which would allow the plaintiff to re-file the claim in state court.  Id. at 1315. 
5        Here, Sonner and Guzman do not mandate dismissal of Valiente’s claims because the 
6   decisions “require far less at the pleading stage.”  See In re Natera Prenatal Testing Litig., </pre><span class="citation no-link">664 F. 
7</span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d 995, 1012 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 28, 2023).  Valiente alleges that she “may lack an adequate 
8   remedy at law” if the amount of damages recoverable is less than the price premium she paid for 
9   Simpson’s tomatoes.  See Compl. ¶¶ 58, 105.  She also alleges that “[r]estitution and/or injunctive 
10   relief may also be more certain, prompt, and efficient than other legal remedies . . . .”  </pre><span class="citation no-link">Id. ¶¶ 60</span><pre class="inline">, 
11   106.  These allegations are sufficient to survive the pending motion to dismiss.  See Murphy v. 
12   Olly Pub. Benefit Corp., </pre><span class="citation no-link">651 F. Supp. 3d 1111</span><pre class="inline">, 1129 (N.D. Cal. 2023) (declining to dismiss the 
13   request for equitable relief where the plaintiffs alleged that legal remedies were not as certain as 
14   equitable remedies because, for example, a full refund would require a showing that the product at 
15   issue had no market value but no such showing was required for restitution).  Cf. Roffman, </pre><span class="citation no-link">653 F. 
16</span><pre class="inline">   Supp. 3d at 731 (granting motion to dismiss and allowing plaintiff leave to amend her allegations 
17   regarding the inadequacy of her legal remedies where the plaintiff “d[id] not suggest that she 
18   s[ought] a different amount in damages than she d[id] in restitution” or that there was a limitation 
19   inherent to the available legal remedy itself that would make it inadequate); Bryan v. Apple, No. 
20   22-cv-00845-HSG, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 2333893</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 2, 2023) (granting motion to dismiss 
21   on a “narrow basis” where plaintiffs did not explain how their claims for damages and equitable 
22   relief were based on different theories and did not allege that they lacked an adequate remedy at 
23   law); Smith v. Apple, Inc., No. 21-CV-09527-HSG, </pre><span class="citation no-link">2023 WL 2095914</span><pre class="inline">, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Feb. 17, 
24   2023) (dismissing claims for equitable relief based on Sonner and Guzman where the plaintiffs 
25   had “not adequately alleged that their remedies at law are inadequate” or “address[ed] why 
26   monetary damages would be inadequate to make [p]laintiffs whole.”).   
27        For these reasons, Simpson’s motion to dismiss Valiente’s equitable claims is denied.
